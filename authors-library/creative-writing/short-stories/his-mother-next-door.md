# His Mother Next Door

**Course / Context:** Creative Writing, English 283 (Holly Baker), University of South Dakota  
**Date:** 7 August 2015  

---

<p class="content-note"><strong>Content note:</strong> child abuse and the death of a child.</p>

I was throwing the last of the carrots into the pot when I heard a thump come from upstairs. I sighed, dried my hands on the kitchen towel and made my way towards the staircase. As I made my way up the stairs, I stopped to fix our family portrait on the wall. It was a standard family set up with me and my husband behind our son with each of our hands on his shoulders. I snickered a little as I remember that the only way we could get little Tommy to smile was by promising him that we would play pirates with him in our backyard afterwards.

With the frame placed properly, I made the final three steps to the top of stairs, turned left, and tapped on Tommy’s door.

“Tommy, honey, dinner won’t be ready for another hour. Maybe you could go down to the park and throw the ball at the hoop instead of your wall?” I heard a groan and opened the door a smidge just in time to see my nine-year-old son’s arms collapse to the floor and the basketball roll towards his Lego figurines. Tommy was lying on his back, feet against the wall.

“Mom, no matter how much I practice, I’m never going to be any good. Besides, no kids want me to play anyways. A whole month has gone by and I still don’t have any friends.”

“Well, you don’t have to play a sport. You could try something new. What about theatre?” All I got in response was a groan as Tommy turned onto his stomach with his face smashed into the carpet. Tommy has always struggled to fit in because he’s always been more mature for his age, which puts him aside from other kids. Tommy likes creating things, not in an artsy-fartsy way, but in an engineering way. There aren’t very many kids that age into that sort of thing. Tommy tried other things to fit in, but has never succeeded.

“Fine. I’ll call you when dinner’s ready, but in the meantime, please stop bouncing the ball against the wall?”

During dinner, Tommy nudged his vegetables around in his bowl, “When does dad get back?”

“I think he said Wednesday morning is his last presentation, so he should be back in four days. Why?” I know Tommy usually missed his dad when he was gone, but this was the first time he’s ever brought it up.

“I don’t know, I guess it would be nice to have someone to do something with.”

My heart broke a little to know that I couldn’t make my son happy. Try as I may, I never seemed to put the right pieces together or would ask amateur questions like why the cowboy holds a laser gun.

“Not that you’re not fun to spend time with,” Tommy said after he realized he had hurt my feelings, “but dad just, I don’t know, likes what I like. I’m sorry…”

Wanting to change the subject, I asked “Did you notice that the house next door finally sold? I think I heard the neighbors across the way say that the new people are moving in tomorrow. Maybe after dinner you could help me bake them brownies to welcome them to the neighborhood.”

Tommy shrugged, “I’m not really hungry mom. Can I go watch TV?”

And with that I lost him, “Sure, honey. Just put your leftovers in the fridge.”

The next morning I was woken by the sound of car doors slamming. I looked outside to see a family of three unpacking a U-Haul truck. There was a woman, a man, and a younger boy that looked to be about Tommy’s age. I jumped out of bed, got ready, and headed downstairs to make blueberry pancakes, Tommy’s favorite. The aroma of my food pulled the tired boy out of bed and into the shower, as I had intended it to. By the time I finished breakfast, he was sitting at the table, ready to eat.

While we ate, I told Tommy what I had seen that morning and told him he should go over to introduce himself. After two fake excuses and a bribe, I had finally talked him into going over. I handed Tommy the plate of brownies I’d made the night before and he left. As I was cleaning up the dishes, I could see Tommy through the window, introducing himself. The boy gave Tommy a small grin and waved while the parents seemed too busy moving to notice Tommy or even give a smile. As the parents grabbed another load from the back of the trailer, I saw them tell the boys to move. Then the boy asked them something; they shrugged and continued walking. Pretty soon Tommy and the new boy were walking towards our house.

Tommy’s face shone like the rising sun, “Mom, this is Oliver.” Oliver quickly glanced in my direction and gave a small wave. Then Tommy asked, “Is it okay if I go show him my figurines?”

“Sure!” I said, excitedly. Finally, someone that was into the same things as Tommy! “Let me know if you need anything, okay?” They were half way up the stairs before I had even finished my sentence. The two stayed up there playing with the figurines until I called them for lunch. The boys didn’t really talk a whole lot during lunch. They seemed more interested in finishing their food quickly so they could continue playing. Once they were finished eating, they disappeared upstairs again. I expected Oliver’s parents to come over and introduce themselves and check on their son, but they never appeared. I figured they must have wanted to get situated in the new house; I guess with their son busy, they didn’t have to worry too much.

When dinner came along, Tommy asked if Oliver could stay and eat with us. “Should you go ask your parents if it’s okay? I don’t want them worrying about you.”

Tommy answered for him saying that they were only planning on getting pizza.

“Alright, how do you like tomato soup?”

Dinner was exciting. Turned out Oliver didn’t really like tomato soup, so I reheated some vegetable soup from the night before. Tommy told me stories about Oliver’s old school and how he used to have a forest in his backyard where he would pretend to be an explorer adventuring through the rain forest. Tommy then told Oliver about how we use to play pirates in the backyard. I was happy that he and Oliver had so much in common, but the boy never really talked a whole lot to me. That was alright by me, he was Tommy’s friend, not mine.

“Are you starting school tomorrow, Oliver?”

“Yeah, Tommy said he could walk with me tomorrow.” It seemed as though he already knew what I was going to ask next. “My parents work tomorrow, so they can’t take me.” He then changed the subject quickly and said, “Thanks for the vegetable soup. It’s really good.”

“Oh, well you’re welcome and thank you. You are welcome at our house any time.” As soon as dinner was over, the boys went back upstairs. At 7:50 they came back downstairs because Oliver needed to go home. As we waved good bye from the door, Oliver ran back to his house and soon disappeared.

“Mom, I really glad you made me go over there! I’m going to show him the treehouse tomorrow after school and we’re going to play pirates!”

“What about basketball practice?”

“I don’t really want to go. No one will miss me. You said I could try something else, so maybe I could just do stuff with Oliver instead.”

By the time my husband, Thomas, came home, Oliver and Tommy were inseparable. Thomas came home expecting his son to give him one of his feet-wrapping hugs, but Tommy was in the backyard with Oliver dragging himself through the desert. It was nice to have Thomas home, but it was nicer to see our son so happy.

This is how things went from then on; Tommy and Oliver were attached at the hip. Every day, the boys would find a new adventure or something new to build and then Oliver left at 7:50. I felt like I had a second son except Oliver didn’t really talk a whole lot. He was one to keep his feelings to himself.

I didn’t meet his parents until the middle of winter, there was always something they were doing or somewhere they had to be. We had accidentally gotten a letter addressed to Oliver’s mother, Tara, so I went over there one evening to return it while the boys were down at the park protecting their snow castles from evil elves. As I approached the house, all the curtains were drawn, which was normal. I then was startled by a man yelling somewhere inside. I couldn’t quite figure out what he was saying, but then I heard a woman yell back followed by a thump. The noise was similar to the sound of Tommy’s basketball hitting the wall a few months back. With that I shakily rang the doorbell, hoping to stop whatever it was that was happening inside, but dreading what was to happen when the door opened. To my surprise, the door opened with a woman smiling.

“Hi, you must be Tara, Oliver’s mother. I’m Tommy’s mom. I can’t believe we have never officially met. Life can be hectic sometimes!”

“I’m sorry. I hope Oliver doesn’t bother you too much. He can be a little crazy some times. If he ever bothers you, you can send him right home. Is that what you’ve come over here for?” She said this as though she was a little out of breath, but kept the smile plastered on her face.

“Oh, no, Oliver is a wonderful boy. We love having him over. Tommy hasn’t had such a great friend in his life! The two are joined at the hip! I came over to formally introduce myself and give you this letter I got in the mail that I believe was supposed to be in your box.”

“Oh, thank you! I really appreciate that. If you don’t mind, though, my husband and I are in the middle of one of our favorite shows and I don’t really want to miss anything.” I was relieved to hear this. I only heard the TV!

“Oh, sorry to bother you. Enjoy your show!” She shut the door and I made my way back home.

I told my husband about this odd encounter in January when he asked if I’d ever met Oliver’s parents. He furrowed his brow and told me when he and Tommy were going to go hiking to bring in the New Year. The weather was nice, so the two planned a father-son hiking trip. Thomas walked Oliver home before he and Tommy left. Oliver tried to make excuses as to why Thomas should just let him go home, but Thomas insisted on meeting his parents. When they approached the house, Thomas heard a crash come from inside. Oliver ran to the door, opened it and hollered, “Mom, stop cooking and come meet Tommy’s dad!” There was a forced smile on Oliver’s face, which puzzled Thomas.

The woman briefly introduced herself and said she had a pie in the oven and didn’t want it to burn, “But thank you for bringing Oliver home, I hope he wasn’t too much of a hassle for you.”

One day in early spring, when the sun decided to show its face more, Tommy and Oliver were Holmes and Watson jumping through puddles after the criminal. It was humid outside and the two were sweating pretty well from running around. Tommy had his shirt wrapped around his head as a hat, but Oliver still had his long-sleeve shirt on. When the boys came inside around three in the afternoon, I told them they needed to go straight to the laundry room and take their clothes off. I told Oliver I would grab him one of Tommy’s t-shirts and he could wear that. Oliver looked up at me and said he would just go home and change quickly. He never came back that day. Tommy told me that his parents needed him to clean his room. Oliver seemed to only tell things to Tommy, never to me.

Oliver showed up to our house Easter morning with a black eye. When I asked about it, he told me that he had gotten trapped by a dragon, but that he had gotten free, so he was fine. Thomas and I felt as though something wasn’t right with Oliver’s family. With Oliver’s oddly specific departing time, weird absence, softly spoken personality, and black eye, Thomas and I decided that we should get to know Oliver’s parents a little better. We didn’t want to draw wrong conclusions and get someone in trouble over nothing; however, we didn’t want anything to get worse. Maybe showing concern would clean up anything that was going on.

Thomas and I talked to Oliver’s mother briefly about the black eye. We voiced our concern about how we never seemed to have a good conversation with either parent. We also said we wished to meet Oliver’s father. Tara explained that Oliver’s father worked a lot and Oliver’s eye was due to him messing around too much before bed. She said he accidentally ran into the hand rail that lead down their steps. She seemed to really appreciate our concern, but assured us that Oliver was fine.

Oliver didn’t come over after school the day after that talk. This worried me, but he was over the day after and was his normal, energetic self, so I brushed it off.

When summer came and the boys were out of school, we invited Oliver to come with us to the state fair, kind of as an end-of-school celebration. The boys had a blast thrashing their fried bananas like knight swords and using the Ferris wheel to climb to the top of Mount Everest. We were all having so much fun that we lost track of time and didn’t get home until 9 o’ clock.

I walked Oliver over to his house, where he ran ahead of me saying over his shoulder, “Thank you for a great day! Have a good night!” Then he went inside to the window and waved to me until I got to my front door. I looked over to him once more, waved back, and went inside.

That was the last time I saw Oliver.

He didn’t come over the next day or the day after that. I got concerned and sent Tommy over to see if he wanted to play. Tommy reported back that Oliver had gotten sick from the fair food and was throwing up so he couldn’t play. The following day Thomas took Tommy to a movie while I made some vegetable soup and brought it over for Oliver. His mom opened the door before I rang the bell, “What brings you over? Oh, did you make something for Oliver? He loves your cooking. I’m sure he’ll be thrilled, tomato soup is his favorite! Thank you.” Then she went to shut the door.

Oliver hated, well didn’t care for, tomato soup. This grew my concern more. The gears in my mind turned as I put the pieces together: the odd curfew, the wall thump, the crash Thomas heard, the black eye, not seeing Oliver’s father, the odd days he was gone. I put my arm out and stopped the door, “it’s actually vegetable soup. Oliver hates tomato soup, but you would know that, you’re his mother. Would it be possible for me to see Oliver? Tommy is gone with his dad today and the house is pretty quiet.”

Her plastered smile drooped, just slightly; it wouldn’t have been noticeable if I hadn’t been watching for it. “No, Oliver is… sleeping. I don’t want to wake him. I’ll make sure he gets your soup though!” The smile was back.

“I’d appreciate that.” Itching to keep her attention for a bit longer, I said, “Is your husband home? I don’t believe I’ve met him before, and you have lived here for so long. I feel rude to not introduce myself.”

“Actually, he got called in to work today.”

“But his car is parked outside y— “

“I do wish he could be with me to watch Oliver, but what can you do? Money’s money. It was nice talking to you, but I must be going.” Then she quickly shut the door without even an excuse to leave.

Thomas and Tommy arrived home half an hour later. I gave Tommy a new figurine for his collection that I had saved for his birthday coming up next month, but I needed to talk to Thomas. I knew this would keep Tommy busy for a while so I could explain to Thomas what had happened. We needed to discuss what to do next. Once I finished explaining what had happened, an unsettling silence fell over us. I knew something was wrong and my stomach twisted. The silence was interrupted by a truck door slamming next door. Rushing to the kitchen window, Thomas and I were able to catch Oliver’s mother pulling out of her driveway and speeding away.

Thomas told me to stay with Tommy as he ran next door. I watched him ring the doorbell and stand there for a few seconds. I saw no movement from inside. He knocked on the door. No response. He slammed on the door and still no one came. Fed up with waiting, I saw Thomas break the door open with his foot and rush inside. I grabbed the kitchen phone, dialed the police, and watched the house like a hawk. I finally turned my eyes away when I saw Thomas emerge from the house with his hands covered in blood.

The next few days flew by in a blur after I saw two paramedics pull out a small body in a black bag and haul it into the truck. I answered the officer’s questions solemnly and tuned out when they talked to Thomas. To be honest, I really didn’t want to know what had happened to Oliver. I preferred to keep our final wave from the front door as the last image of him. One thing that I clearly remember is Tommy’s face when I told him Oliver couldn’t play anymore. Telling my son that the only real friend he’s ever had has gone to heaven is the hardest thing I have ever had to do in my life. One job of a mother is to protect her child and in this moment I felt as though I had failed twice.

We put our house up for sale the next week and it sold a month later. We couldn’t stand living there anymore; looking next door was a daily remembrance of what had happened. We moved three towns over, but, no matter how far away we go, the events that happened in that house will forever loom in our hearts.

Tara killed both her husband and son that day. They never found her, which bothers me to no end. I would never want to see that woman again. I don’t know if I could control my emotions or actions if I saw her.

The events surrounding Oliver’s death were terrible, but a blessing. If I had never been in his life, he never would have felt the real love a mother could give, even though we didn’t really talk a whole lot. In a way, this idea brings me reassurance that we lived there for a reason; however, I will always have this fear in the back of my mind telling me that the world is never a safe place.
