<p class="paper-title">Through the Eyes of Others</p>

Imagine a bully pushing down a little boy on a playground. The bully feels power over the little boy, but also sees that he has hurt the little boy’s feelings while the other kids in the playground witness this scene happening. In this scenario, there are three different perspectives to see this scene through; the eyes of the bully, the little boy, and the other kids on the playground. The idea of looking at a situation through different eyes is put to use in Rachel Linnea Brown’s poem, “Squeezebox Poem” (Brown 102). Although the poem is all through the perspective of the same narrator, the narrator sees what they are doing through the eyes of others throughout the poem. This poem demonstrates how an author can use various perspectives, the narrator, the victim, and the observer, to impact how a reader views a situation.

The first perspective shown in “Squeezebox Poem” can be found on the first two pages and is through the eyes of the narrator. The tone in the first two pages is violent and dark. The way one can see the violence is through the way the specific words used by the speaker. “I/ cut you” is not a pleasant image for a reader to open with (Brown 1-2). The idea of someone cutting someone else is vicious and illegal. The lines “like you’re not/ already slit/ inside” also give the image of violence (14-16). Someone bleeding internally because of a gash is not easy to accomplish and gives off a violent impression. By the speaker saying “Dare/ to laugh” they are making themselves appear threatening (17). When someone is threatening someone else, the situation portrays violence, much like the speaker is doing in this part of the poem.

Along with the violence, the narrator also comes off as dark by the words used and how they are arranged. The line “Laugh, dear” gives the reader an impression of a possible light side to the narrator, but then the lines following, “like you’re not/ already slit/ inside,” demonstrates the darkness and gives the impression that the narrator has a twisted sense of humor. Laughing usually indicates that something is funny, but someone being cut inside is not funny at all, unless someone has a dark sense of humor, like the narrator. The idea of the narrator being dark is also used in the lines “Dare/ to laugh/ like each peal/ doesn’t deepen/ your already/ wound” (17-22). This sentence starts out threatening and then becomes dark by using the word “deepen.” By insinuating that the wound is deep, the narrator shows how well they know the victim’s injuries and they know that the wounds are painful. The speaker is standing their ground by showing that they do not care how much pain the victim is in. This is torturous and because of this, the speaker is shown as dark. By seeing through the perspective of the narrator, the reader is able to see how the narrator sees the situation and is able to see how they are not really seeing how they are effecting others.

The next two pages, gives the reader a glimpse into the perspective of the person the speaker is interacting with, the victim. This is the first time in the poem the speaker brings up how the victim feels or says. The narrator says, “You warn I might/ pull your arms/ from their/ sockets” (27-30). By the narrator bringing this up, it shows that they are able to acknowledge how their actions are effecting the other person. This also gives the reader a glimpse of how the victim feels, endangered and helpless. By the victim warning the narrator, they are showing their concern for the situation and also showing how they do not have very much control over what is happening. The victim warns instead of actually doing anything about the position they have been placed in.

The narrator also gives the idea of seeing the thoughts of the other by reflecting on what they have done and will not do. The next second page of this section says, “I didn’t/ fix your charcoal/ face… Your eyes will only/ last until your/ rest is gray” (31-33, 36-38). This shows that the speaker knows what they have done and what the effects will be, but does not want to do anything about it. This puts shows how much more of a victim the other person is because they do not have any hope of being released from the position they are in, making them even more helpless. With seeing that the narrator understands what is happening to the victim, the reader is able to more fully comprehend the level of comprehension the speaker has of the situation. The reader is also able to see what the narrator is planning on doing.

The final perspective that the narrator looks at the situation through is the observers’, which can be found on the final two pages of the poem. By the speaker saying, “I/ grow still,” they point out how they appear to others, making the reader conscious of their own size compared to the size of the narrator (39-40). This sets the reader in the position of seeing what is going to happen from their own point of view and out of the eyes of the speaker on the following page.

The next and final page of the poem gives the feeling of the reader watching the narrator “looking/ at the horizon” (48-49). With adding this emotional distance between the reader and the narrator, the reader is able to see what has happened in this situation and more fully understand when the speaker says, “now am I --/ little/ prison” (52-54). When the narrator says this, they are realizing that they have control over the victim and that they are the prison themselves. They are the reason the victim is helpless and endangered. The reader is able to make this connection because of the journey the speaker has taken them on. By the reader feeling the threatening presence of the narrator, they are able to see what the motivation is behind the narrator. By the reader understanding the helpless feeling of the victim, they are able to see why the narrator feels as though they have full control over the victim.

The journey the speaker takes by looking at the situation from different perspectives helps the reader more fully understand how the narrator feels. This is seen by the reader understanding how both the narrator and the victim feel during the situation before the reader makes any judgement on what is happening. It is important for a person to take a look at a situation through different eyes because people do things for a reason, but others are too quick to make judgement on those they think have done bad.
