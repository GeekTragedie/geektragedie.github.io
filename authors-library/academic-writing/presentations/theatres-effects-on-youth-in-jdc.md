## Theatre’s Effects on Youth in JDC

GeekTragedie, with a colleague · University of South Dakota

### Hypothesis

Theatre has an effect on youth in juvenile detention centers.

### Objectives

- Analyze the change in JDC youth’s self-esteem, self-consciousness, and self concept.
- Teach youth about theatre.
- Determine what the youth believe they have learned.
- Discover what changes staff have noticed.

### Project Overview

An eight-week theatre program (one hour a week) at the Minnehaha JDC was evaluated. The evaluation measured the changes in youths’ self-esteem, self-consciousness (public, private, and social anxiety), and self-concept. The youth were given the surveys at the beginning and ending of their time in the program. The program consisted of eight weeks of theatre, including games, improvisation, mask making, scene rehearsals, scripts writing, and a final performance for JDC staff.  The scripts written by the students during the sixth week were the scripts rehearsed and performed. After the final performances, the youth were given their final evaluation survey and staff that observed the program throughout the summer were also given surveys regarding the program. The surveys of the youths were  then analyzed by the researchers.

2. Step 2: Complete initial evaluation surveys
3. Step 3: Implement eight-week theatre program

### Observations

- The youth…
  - learned they could have fun doing theatre.
  - discovered how to get out of their comfort zone.
  - found how to make the best in the worst situations.
  - redirected their negative feelings into positive ones.
- The staff claimed youth…
  - were more apt to talk to staff about issues.
  - learned leadership skills, found out more about themselves, and discovered new skills.
  - became more comfortable, confident and outgoing.
  - that were shy became more vocal and interactive.

### Results

The poster graphed the initial and conclusion score distributions (N = 16) for three scales. Their means and standard deviations, computed from the poster’s chart data:

| Scale | Initial M (SD) | Conclusion M (SD) |
|---|---|---|
| Social Anxiety | 7.25 (3.94) | 5.62 (3.84) |
| Self-Esteem | 19.31 (3.40) | 20.81 (4.23) |
| Public Self-Consciousness | 14.56 (3.61) | 12.84 (5.10) |

- Social Anxiety Distributions: The graph shows the SD of the initial and conclusion data. While using the differences between the initial and conclusion scores with N = 16, a t-test was done and found that social anxiety had a statistical significant decrease at a .05 effect size.
- Self-Esteem Distributions: The graph shows the SD of the initial and conclusion data. While using the differences between the initial and conclusion scores with N = 16, a t-test was done and found that Self-Esteem had a statistical significant increase at a .1 effect size.
- Public Self-Consciousness Distributions: The graph shows the SD of the initial and conclusion data. While using the differences between the initial and conclusion scores with N = 16, a t-test was done and found that Public Self-Consciousness had a statistical significance at a .1 effect size.

### Conclusion

In the analysis of the finished 16 surveys after the eight-week program, self-concept and private self-consciousness showed no statistically significant information; however, the youth showed a significant decrease in social anxiety and public self-consciousness and a significant increase in self-esteem. The youth reported learning how to work as a team, get out of their comfort zone, and be creative. The staff noticed that the youth were more open to sharing new ideas and apt to talk to staff about issues, as well as more comfortable, confident, and outgoing.
