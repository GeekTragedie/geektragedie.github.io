## May I Touch You? Ethical Use of Touch in Drama Therapy

While many mainstream forms of counseling do not use touch, it is an important tool for drama therapists (DTx) because not using touch in certain dramatic situations can limit the healing abilities of the playspace. With the encouragement to use touch in drama therapy (DT), it is key to understand the ethical use of touch through reviewing multiple ethical codes. For this paper, I incorporate the ethical standards written by the American Dance Therapy Association and Dance/Movement Therapy Certification Board (ADTA & DMTCB), the North American Drama Therapy Association (NADTA), and Developmental Transformation (DvT) experts. In this reflection paper, I explore my understanding of using touch in DT, its benefits, and the challenges I may face in my future career.

## Personal Understanding

There are many items to consider when attempting to understand when and how to use touch in a DT session. Finding a specific explanation is difficult, but rules that appear to be easily understood are being mindful of the client’s “therapeutic goals, safety, and best interest” (NADTA, 2019, p. 2) and “not touch sexual body parts or use touch to sexually arouse” (ADTA & DMTCB, 2019, p. 4). When using physical contact in DT, DvT experts also recommend the following: “non-evocative vs. evocative touch, brief vs. sustained touch, offered touch vs. initiated touch, and role-bound touch vs. spontaneous touch” (“Appendix F,” 2019, pp. 93-94). Keeping all these considerations in mind can be a daunting task and impossible to remember in the moment. When questioning whether use of touch is ethical, it is recommended that the DTx “seek supervision and consultation as issues arise” (ADTA & DMTCB, 2019, p. 5). These items listed in various ethical codes have created my personal understanding of how and when to use touch in DT.

## Benefits of Touch

There are multiple benefits touch can offer to a client. As discussed in section 1.5 of “The Code of Ethics and Standards of the ADTA and DMTCB,” “touch may provide safety, support grounding, organization, and regulation, establish body boundaries, facilitate self-awareness, support group cohesion and human connection, comfort and ease pain” (ADTA & DMTCB, 2019, p. 4). I used touch to enhance a therapeutic theatre experience while teaching a theatre workshop at a juvenile detention center. The youths wrote their own plays and had other members act them out, and one play required an actor to be lifted. To promote trust, ensure safety, and fulfill the playwright’s wishes, I showed the actors the most secure way to perform the lift for their performance. The actor lifted increased his trust with other group members, the lifting members learned group communication, and the playwright’s dream of being a future director was enhanced.

Another benefit to using physical contact in DT is to help the clients when they leave the session. Clients experience touch outside of the therapeutic realm and depriving them from touch in sessions does not help prepare them for life when they leave. When touch happens in the playspace, there is an opportunity for the client and DTx to work with the feelings that arise from that touch. The client learns these skills in the session and takes the skills with them outside of the playspace.

## Personal Challenges

I have two challenges when using touch in DT sessions: knowing when touch is appropriate and understanding my discomfort regarding touch. It is difficult for me to define exactly when touch is appropriate because it depends on the relationship I have with a future client. I believe it is important for a DTx to remember the rules of the playspace when using physical touch in a DT session. Ethical standards in DvT state that touch “will involve no harm, be performative (that is, discrepant), and mutually agreed upon” (“Appendix F,” 2019, p. 93). These three standards are also rules of the playspace, as described by Johnson (2019). The DTx needs to trust themselves, build a strong therapeutic relationship with the client, and be mindful of the playspace during the therapy session. A DTx understanding the playspace with the client will help ensure touch is appropriate and agreed upon by both parties. If touch happens and it causes a reaction in the client, the touch creates a possibility for the DTx and client to explore the initial reaction.

Along with knowing when it is appropriate to use touch, I am also personally challenged by touching and being touched. Being in the military, it is ingrained in members during basic training to not touch others. I accidentally backed into a sergeant, and she shouted at me that she could file sexual harassment claims against me for touching her. In basic training, everyone needed to be granted permission before touching another by asking, “May I touch you?” These along with other military experiences cause me to question whether I can touch another person without being accused of sexual harassment. I am comforted by the fact that physical contact is in the “NADTA Code of Ethical Principles.” The DTx is protected from accusations that may arise from touch by the ethical codes granting the DTx ability to use physical contact during therapy, “with the individual’s consent and in a safe and respectful manner” (NADTA, 2019, p. 2). Permission to touch is in writing, preventing the need to ask every time the DTx feels touch is beneficial. I need to trust my own instincts as a DTx to know when touch is appropriate.

## Conclusion

It is important for a DTx to understand the ethical codes around using touch and consider the benefits physical contact has for clients. Due to my personal history, I was reluctant to consider using touch in a therapeutic environment. By exploring my understanding and the benefits of using touch, I hope to practice touch and decrease my own resistance to touch. Maybe through the creative arts utilizing touch in therapy, other disciplines can also learn to use touch in a safe and ethical manner, allowing more clients to reap the benefits.
