<p class="paper-title">Ill Usage</p>

In William Shakespeare’s play *Romeo and Juliet,* the word “ill” means “wicked” or “evil;” however, for 21st century audiences, people define it as “unwell” or “sick” (“ill, *adj.* and *n.*”, "ill."). With the Shakespearean definition having a different meaning, readers during 21st century obtain a different understanding of certain lines and perceive the play differently. The different meanings of “ill” can be observed through Tybalt’s feelings during act one, the nurse’s advisement for Romeo in act two, and Romeo’s outlook on Balthasar’s news in act five.

Towards the end of act one Tybalt points out to Capulet that Romeo, a Montague, attends the party. Capulet tells Tybalt to leave Romeo alone; Tybalt does not want to obey and voices this to Capulet. Capulet tells Tybalt to “put off these frowns/ An ill-beseeming semblance for a feast” (1.4.184-85). If a reader had understood “ill” as “sick,” then the line would be comprehended as Capulet signifying that Tybalt feels sick, when really, Tybalt has wicked feelings towards Romeo and wishes to attack him. Understanding Tybalt’s hatred helps readers understand later why Tybalt sword fights with Romeo in act three.

A second instance where the word “ill” can be misinterpreted appears between the nurse and Romeo in act two. The nurse gives Romeo a message from Juliet, but also warns him saying, “if you should deal double with her, truly it were an ill thing to be offered to any gentlewoman” (2.3.153-55). By understand these lines in terms of “sick,” a reader would not be able to feel the depth of what the nurse says. Romeo’s actions would not cause a sickness in Juliet, but be truly wicked. The nurse defines Romeo as “morally evil” and, therefore, seems more threatening. The nurse is close to Juliet, so understanding her depth during this situation is important to the play.

The final use of the word “ill” to be analyzed for contextualized understanding can be read in act five when Balthasar tells Romeo of Juliet’s death. Romeo asks Balthasar about Juliet and adds, “That I ask again, For nothing can be ill if she be well” (5.1.15-16). 21st century audience may think that no one can be sick if Juliet feels well, because nothing can be “unwell” if she stays well. The sentence does not have this meaning, however. This line really means that nothing can be evil if Juliet remains well. Romeo may have some good left in his life. The Shakespearean understanding puts more pressure on Balthasar to not bring bad news. This pressure causes Balthasar to be careful with his words: “Then she is well, and nothing can be ill” (5.1.17). Balthasar lies in saying this because Juliet appears to be dead. Balthasar later claims that his news was “ill news,” meaning that the news was rooted in evil and appears wicked. These two words have roots with witchcraft. Witchcraft was illegal during this time, showing that using the word “wicked” was certainly worse than simply being “unwell” (Gaskill 33). The news given is evil or wicked news. Overall, the exchange between the two men changes context because the two are talking about evil things having happened rather than a sickness.

A different understanding of certain lines can be seen when readers understand the definitions of words as Shakespeare did when he wrote the play *Romeo and Juliet*. The word “ill” having an alternate definition to 21st century audiences is only one example of comprehending differently lines throughout the play. With Shakespearean audiences understanding “ill” as having roots with evil and witchcraft, it has more depth than understanding “ill” as “unwell” or “sick” (“ill, *adj.* and *n.*”, "ill."). Importance emerges when understand the playwright’s definitions when reading a play from different time periods.
