Discussing low socioeconomic status (SES) has always been difficult for me. Whenever I talk about people who financially struggle, I always feel as though I am not making enough of an impact or doing enough for those who experience poverty. I am financially stable and have been most of my life. [After I moved out of my family’s house, I largely supported myself.] I had the privilege to get to where I am today, while others with low SES probably did not have the same privilege. I always feel I am not doing enough for this population. For these reasons, I decided to join the group presenting on SES to increase my knowledge base of those experiencing poverty and better understand my feelings regarding this population.

People with low SES have a particular view of the world. They may feel shamed and stigmatized due to marginalization. They may feel uncared for due to the lack of resources and essential needs being met. They do not have as much agency in their life because of our capitalist society: “having financial resources allows one to rely less on others” (Foster, Elischberger, & Hill, 2018). There are different barriers faced by different demographics living in poverty. Within the low SES population, the majority race is Black, and the majority gender is women (Smith, 2015). The intersectionality of these minority groups is important for counselors to be aware of because the different intersections of oppression should be taken into consideration by the counselor.

I have a better understanding of how the world views low SES and how the world has influenced my own view. Blaine (2013) discusses the traits the majority places on those with low SES: uneducated, unmotivated, depressed, capable, embarrassed, alcoholic, have too many children (p. 159). These stereotypes are how the world views people experiencing poverty, though these traits are not supported by research. While growing up, I know I held some of these views, but never experienced anyone living in poverty. When I moved to Seattle and interacted with more people with low SES, I felt wrong about biased view and needed to change. I now know the systemic factors and oppression experienced by this marginalized population. There are many barriers faced by someone experiencing poverty that make it very difficult for them to change their situation: insurance, healthcare benefits, affordable food and housing, and educational opportunities.

When working with people experiencing poverty as a counselor, there are many ideas to keep in mind, of which I cannot list all the factors. Two of the key elements I am taking away from my research are the application of Maslow’s hierarchy of needs and considering the risk factors of those experiencing poverty. Maslow shows that a person needs to have their lower level needs taken care of first in order to approach higher levels of needs (Sharf, 2016). People with low SES struggle meeting their basic level needs: food, water, shelter, safety. A counselor needs to help clients meet these needs in order to help clients with higher needs, such as relationships, self-esteem, and feelings of accomplishment. There are also many risk factors for those with low SES, such as a higher risk of physical and mental illness (Smith, 2015). There are also safety risks to be considered for the client. Clients with low SES also risk meeting barriers from the system (Schmid, 2016). It is important to assess the client for where they are and consider what needs can be met. As a counselor, I have privilege to advocate for my clients on various levels. So, in my future work, it might be my duty to help my client navigate the system; which means, I need to be knowledgeable about the system being navigated.

I appreciated the ability to focus on my bias feelings regarding people experiencing poverty. I know I will need to continue learning about my bias and where it originates. I also need to take action to change my internal bias and automatic responses. I appreciate looking at the world through the lens of someone living in poverty and consider the majority’s stigma in relation to reality. I hope to take what I learned in this presentation and working with my group into my future practice as a counselor.
