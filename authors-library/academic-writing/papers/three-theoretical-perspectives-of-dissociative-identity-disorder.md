<p class="paper-title"><strong>Three Theoretical Perspectives of Dissociative Identity Disorder</strong></p>

Dissociative identity disorder (DID) has made multiple debuts in Hollywood, but the lives of people with DID do not particularly appreciate Hollywood’s not-so-knowledgeable perspective. DID is generally understood to be rooted in childhood trauma where an individual uses dissociation to cope with traumatic events (Haddock, 2001). Through dissociation, the person, also known as the host, develops other personalities, also known as alters, to cope with the stressful trauma (Haddock, 2001). There are multiple ways theorists view the development, symptoms, and treatment of DID, and understanding these different approaches can help best understand the individual and choose a proper course of treatment.

## Disorder Overview

### Symptoms

Before theoretically viewing DID, clinicians should know the symptoms, etiology, epidemiology, and cultural factors that should be considered when approaching the disorder. Most clinicians in the United States combine their theoretical orientation with the *DSM-5* criteria to create their diagnosis of DID. According to the American Psychiatric Association (APA, 2013), there must be “presence of two or more distinct personality states…recurrent gaps in the recall of everyday events, important personal information, and/or traumatic events that are inconsistent with ordinary forgetting” (p. 292). Memory loss is contributed to dissociative amnesia, which can present itself in three ways: “gaps in remote memory of personal life events… lapses in dependable memory… and discovery of evidence of their everyday actions and tasks that they do not recollect doing” (p. 293). The final criteria state that symptoms must cause clinically significant distress and are not better explained by cultural practices, substance use, or other medical condition (p. 292).

### Etiological Perspectives

The etiology of DID is typically associated with childhood trauma. There are similar brain functions between individuals with posttraumatic stress disorder and DID through the hyperarousal of the amygdala (Haddock, 2001). However, there is very little biological evidence to support the existence of DID. Early research, that has gained continued support, shows that early childhood trauma and abuse contribute to the evolution of DID (Bell et al., 2015; Biswas et al., 2013; Gold et al., 2001; Stolovy et al., 2015). Ross (1997) explains the process by saying,

> the abused child cannot escape from the trauma, nor can she control or predict it… [she] can use dissociation to block out some or all of the actual abusive events, but this strategy does not solve a more pervasive and inescapable problem: The child is overwhelmed, helpless, and powerless… [which] deepens the trauma. (p. 65)

The child develops dissociation as a coping mechanism to escape trauma and continues to use this coping mechanism in other aspects of their lives to cope with and escape from stress, which may or may not relate to the original trauma (Ringrose, 2011).

While DID stems from childhood trauma, not everyone who experiences trauma in childhood will develop DID. While someone with DID can create alters at any age, it is not possible for a person to develop DID after a certain period of childhood brain and identity development, which is generally before the age of 9 (DID Research, 2016). Ross (1997) theorizes four different “pathways” a child takes to establish whether or not they will develop DID: child abuse, child neglect, factitiously, or iatrogenicly (pp. 64-69). Even with these predictions, there is no solidified research to explain why one individual may develop DID and not another. There are theories regarding biological brain elements possibly influencing the development of DID. Haddock (2001) states “the human brain is built to process trauma, but it is not meant to process severe trauma on an ongoing basis” (p. 28). Neuropsychiatrists have found amygdala development associated with DID, although it is uncertain whether trauma causes the decreased development, or the development causes the DID (Biswas, Chu, Perez, & Gutheil, 2013). Future research regarding development of DID is needed to establish a firm etiology.

### Epidemiological Data

Most clients with DID do not initially attend counseling for issues related to the disorder but rather seek counseling for “managing an anxiety disorder, a mood and/or psychotic disorder, or a substance [use] disorder” (Levy & Swanson, 2008). Many people living with DID fail to receive the correct diagnosis until they have been in the mental health system between 5 to 11.9 years (International Society for the Study of Dissociation, 2005). For many years, clinicians did not want to work with clients with DID and often misdiagnosed them. With this information in mind, clinicians should screen for DID in their initial assessment to ensure the clients’ treatment is appropriate and minimizes harm.

Due to the delicate nature of a client having multiple personalities, clinicians need to be mindful of the alters’ and the hosts’ thoughts and feelings. Many clients with DID have a high risk of suicide attempts, about 61 – 91%, which can be attributed to the alters’ barriers in relation to the hosts’ disclosure (Ringrose, 2011, p. 296-297). Some therapeutic approaches can “exacerbate rather than resolve clients’ difficulties” (Gold et al., 2008, p. 7). It is important to pace the discussion of traumatic events because one personality may feel comfortable discussing the information, but an alter may be overwhelmed (Ringrose, 2011, p. 300). Ringrose (2011) suggests therapists and clients ask permission when discussing sensitive information (p. 297), because any alter can cause self-harm to the host if permission is not granted (Ringrose, 2011).

### Cultural Implications

Religion plays a large role in the lives of many clients with DID. Studies show that many clients with DID have some sort of religious background related to their traumatic experiences (Bell et al., 2015). Bell et al.’s (2015) research also shows that many clients with DID struggle with “negative religious coping” due to their history of abuse. Regardless of the client’s spirituality, it may be important to address religious upbringing with the client. Clinicians should also differentiate dissociation from possession; the APA (2013) lists the distinguishing factors between these two forms, which are further supported historically and explained in Ross’s (1997) chapter “The History of Dissociative Identity Disorder.” There are also some cultures that practice channeling and absorption which includes dissociations (Stolovy, Lev-Wiesel, & Witztum, 2015). Counselors should know the difference between DID and these types of cultural rituals, making the diagnostic criterion regarding cultural rituals helpful.

Bell et al. (2015) show that age and socioeconomic status (SES) are also important to consider when treating DID. Bell et al. (2015) found “higher levels of income relate to higher amounts of self-reported resilience” (p. 158) and higher resilience is found to be a beneficial factor when treating clients with DID (p. 161). If a client with DID is struggling with low SES, they may also struggle with negative coping skills (Bell et al., 2015). In terms of age, the longer DID symptoms go unacknowledged or untreated, which is likely for clients with DID, the more likely a client is to “practice negative coping skills” (Bell et al., 2015, p. 158). Counselors should attempt to diagnose DID at an early age, but if not noticed, a clinician may need to focus more on instilling positive coping skills.

## Psychoanalytic Theory

Psychoanalysis, founded by Sigmund Freud, has a specific way of diagnosing disorders and behaviors that cause clinical distress. The three key factors to psychoanalytic theory are ego development, the unconscious, and the creation of defense mechanisms (Sharf, 2016). It is possible to view DID symptoms from the lens of psychoanalysis to gain insight into the development of this disorder.

### Ego Dilemma

Psychoanalytic counselors view DID as an individual not being able to create a solid personal identity or ego. An ego is created during childhood, and Sharf (2016) defines the ego’s function as “mediat[ing] between the world around the infant and the instincts or drives within the infant… it is the function of the ego to test reality” (p. 36). If trauma is influencing a person’s childhood and the child is dissociating, the child will be unable to solidify their reality and create a personal identity. When dissociation occurs, it “temporarily alters one’s sense of personal identity to avoid some forms of emotional distress;” there is a “deficit involving the integrative functions of memory, consciousness and identity” (Biswas et al., 2013, p. 48). The dissociation becomes a norm for the individual with DID, and it becomes part of the ego development.

In psychoanalysis, therapists work towards creating a functional ego which matches some of the treatment goals researchers recommend when working with clients with DID. Levy and Swanson (2008) recommend helping a client integrate their personalities into one identity (p. 83). This practice aligns with psychoanalysts striving to have the ego work with internal forces to best present the self to reality and has helped stabilize clients with DID; research supports that the more they integrate or work with the alters, the more they are able to improve their functioning (Biswas et al., 2013, p. 47).

### Unconscious Material

Approaching DID from a psychoanalytic perspective also involves looking at materials that may be in the individual’s unconscious. The unconscious contains “memories and emotions that are threatening to the conscious mind and must be pushed away” (Sharf, 2016, p. 35). Due to DID’s roots in trauma, the information regarding the traumatic event(s) that may be hidden in the unconscious. With DID, “fragmentation and encapsulation of traumatic experiences may serve to protect relationships with important (though inadequate or abusive) caretakers and allow more normal maturation in other developmental areas” (Biswas et al., 2013, p. 46). For a person with DID to cope with their reality, others around them, and their trauma, it is necessary for them to move their memories into their unconscious.

When working to treat DID, many researchers recommend recalling traumatic materials (Levy & Swanson, 2008; Biswas et al., 2013; Bell et al., 2015); in psychoanalytic terms, this involves making unconscious material conscious. Ringrose (2011) recommends clients recalling their traumatic event(s) and believes that “through this realization and sharing of information amongst the host and alters that the dissociated alters can become connected with the host” (p. 300) which can help with creating a healthy ego.

### Defense Mechanisms

In psychoanalysis, dissociation can be viewed as a defense mechanism that previously served purpose in the client’s life but is now causing distress. Psychoanalysis believes unhealthy defense mechanisms lead to distress in an individual’s life (Sharf, 2016, p. 37). These defense mechanisms once served the client but is no longer helpful. Dissociation and the creation of alternate personalities is viewed as a learned coping skill to deal with trauma as a child (Stolovy et al, 2015; Biswas et al., 2013). The child’s brain was overwhelmed by the trauma and was unable to process the information, thus dissociating to cope (Ringrose, 2011, p. 298). Through psychoanalysis, the client can realize that their current coping mechanism is no longer suiting them and create new ways of coping with future problems in a healthier way.

## Dialectical Behavioral Theory

Dialectical behavioral theory (DBT), founded by Marsha Linehan, takes a dialectical and behavioral approach to therapy and diagnosing disorders such as DID. Three aspects of DBT include reality orientation, biosocial issues, and emotional dysregulation.

### Nature of Reality

DBT finds importance in a person’s reality in comparison to those around them, which can be an issue for individuals with DID. DBT views reality as fluid; it is “made up of internal opposing forces out of whose synthesis evolves a new set of opposing forces” (Linehan, 2015, p. 4). When the two forces are unable to integrate into one, a person may become distressed. From a DBT approach, an individual with DID is unable to integrate different aspects of themselves which results in alternate personalities. Reality understanding is also difficult for people with DID; “sometimes clients will be unclear about what is real and what is dreamed or imagined” (Ringrose, 2011, p. 299). It is through therapy that a client with DID can differentiate between the reality of an experience or the influence of a dream being considered a reality.

DBT takes certain approaches in therapy to help clients with DID organize their historical information into real or imagined. Many researchers have found grounding techniques beneficial to clients with DID (Levy & Swanson, 2008; Biswas et al., 2013). Through grounding, clients are able to be mindful of the reality surrounding them and presently differentiate between reality and fantasy. These grounding techniques can help clients gain a sense of safety in their surroundings and gain agency of their self-regulatory practice (Levy & Swanson, 2008, p. 83).

### Biosocial Issues

DBT frames issues with emotional regulation in DID as coming from an individual’s “social environment, particularly the family” (Linehan, 2015, p. 7). DBT believes healthy emotional regulation is learned through a person’s environment and interactions with the outside world (Linehan, 2015). With dissociation and trauma, a person’s ideas of regulating in social situations is lacking. A person with DID has created various ways of coping with social situations, based on the various alternate personalities wanting to interact differently with the surrounding world. According to Bell et al. (2015), many people with DID, especially those who are older in age, tend to use negative religious coping. Those who use this style of coping lack strong social connection and have lower levels of education (p. 158). These findings are not surprising given individual’s with DID having difficulty regulating their reality and grew up with trauma influencing their ability to understand social cues.

DBT approaches have been shown to be helpful while working with clients with DID. Levy and Swanson recommend psychoeducation, safety planning, learning new coping skills, and solidifying “the gains he or she has made and arranging for appropriate follow-up” (p. 83). These items can be beneficial to individuals struggling with DID because there is a proven lack of education and social connection. Having a plan that educates clients about social interactions and possibly practices these items are useful. Biswas et al. (2013) also found education regarding PTSD and dissociation helpful to clients; clients are able to understand what is happening in their bodies and why these coping skills came to be (p. 46). Understanding the root of a problem can help gain information on how to approach a solution.

### Emotional Dysregulation

The issue of emotional dysregulation in clients with DID stems from their traumatic experience in childhood. From a DBT approach, the three primary causes of emotional dysregulation are when a person’s environments includes “(1) a tendency to invalidate emotions and an inability to model appropriate expressions of emotions; (2) an interaction style that reinforces emotional arousal; and (3) a poor fit between the child’s temperament and the caregivers’ parenting style” (p. 7). Childhood trauma can involve any or all of the three criteria, leading a person who develops DID to also struggle with emotional regulation.

Emotional regulation is key in the approach of DBT and has also been seen as an issue for people with DID. Pierce (2014) recommends two phases of treatment for clients with DID, both phases include helping clients regulate their emotions. The first stage of treatment is learning to notice and learn from somatic arousal of emotions and attribute them to possible behaviors (Pierce, 2014, p. 10). A counselor should keep in mind the alters’ personalities, emotions, and behaviors if possible to help with regulation and possible integration. Phase two of Pierce’s (2014) approach to treatment is to “secure inner stance and skills for self-regulation” by “cultivating the ability to rack one’s emotional, cognitive, and somatic experience through attention to interoception, or the process of afferent information that occurs within the body” (Pierce, 2014, p. 10). Pierce’s (2014) phases of treatment deal directly with emotional dysregulation in patients with DID, showing that this item is an issue. Therefore, from a DBT approach, clinicians would view emotional dysregulation as a problem for clients with DID.

## Narrative Theory

Narrative theory, a constructivist approach founded by Michael White and David Epston, views disorders and diagnosis from a story-based and strength-based perspective. It is believed that a person creates and analyzes their own story, to include setting, characterization, plot, and theme (Sharf, 2016, pp. 488-489). Using these elements with a client with DID includes looking at the client’s plot, what has happened; character, who it is happening with; setting, where it all happens; and theme, the reasons behind what has happened. Due to a client’s trauma history and having alternate identities, it is possible that the client cannot answer all of these questions. Approaching DID from a narrative perspective can be very beneficial as some theorists believe that “dissociation appears to be a characteristic of resilience in response to trauma rather than a mark of pathology” (Bell et al., 2015, p. 152). This approach is highlighting a client’s symptomology and alters as resilience, which is another key element to narrative theory.

### Creating a Story

If a client is working through DID and trauma, it may be beneficial to focus on the client’s characterization and plot, as these two parts can be difficult to understand given the multiple identities and memory gaps.

**Plot.** Due to memory gaps, it may be difficult for a client with DID to create a plot of their life. Narrative therapists views the lack of full plot as a potential issue for clients with DID. Levy and Swanson (2008) recommend clients and counselors work to create a history of the client’s experiences to help “paint a clear picture of the client’s problematic experiences” (p. 83). Ellis (1988) found it beneficial for clients to “learn a great deal more about [themselves] and [their] history” (p. 67). A narrative therapist can then help the client understand these experiences and view them from different perspectives to help develop motivations or developments of different characters in a person’s life.

The task of creating a client with DID’s plot can be difficult. Clients are “having to come to terms with the traumatic past, [and] the [client] faces the task of creating a future” (Pierce, 2014, p. 11). The counselor and client need to have permission from all alternate personalities to work on memories of traumatic experiences to prevent self-harm and possible overwhelming information (Ringrose, 2011). Again, clients have difficulty differentiating fact and fiction in their life (Ringrose, 2011). Therefore, it may take a lot of time and negotiation between the client, therapist, and Others to best create a firm plot or history.

**Characterization.** Characterization in narrative theory is unique when working with a client with DID because the client has more than one identity to try and understand. For narrative therapists, it is important for a client to understand their motivations, goals, and emotions to continue the story of their lives (Sharf, 2016). Counselors have found understanding alternate personalities beneficial in the therapeutic process and emphasize “talking to the personality system as a whole, facilitating internal meetings of the different self-states” (Levy & Swanson, 2008, p. 83). These can be ways of understanding Other’s characterization and identify if there are any “identities who behave in unsafe or dysfunctional ways” to help develop “agreements between alternate identities to help the [client] become more functional” (Biswas et al., 2013, p. 47). By creating an understanding of all personalities, clients can develop a co-presence, which is alters and the host can “share elements of daily living, which can mean the sharing of talents and abilities with each other prior to complete integration, and thus can be a useful stepping stone to this end” (Ringrose, 2011, p. 303). This step can create a fluid characterization for the client.

Through understanding the individual characteristics of the client and their alternate personalities, there may be ways a client can best understand themselves and empathize with themselves. Clients with trauma “may find empathy for themselves almost impossible and empathy from the therapist may be too much for the host or one of the alters to bear… trauma survivors struggle as much with feeling positive affect as they do with negative”  (Ringrose, 2011, p. 298). Creating a self-empathy for the self can be powerful for helping a client use positive self-talk and viewing their abilities as a personal strength.

### Negative Self-Narratives

In narrative theory, it is important for a person to have a positive view of themselves, which is difficult for clients with DID. Outside beliefs combined with negative mental health experiences can influence self-talk with clients with DID. Narrative theory takes a strength-based approach (Sharf, 2016) which can help clients with DID feel empowered by their story. Levy and Swanson (2008) find it important for clients with DID to find their internal strengths and use those strengths to approach future problems that may arise. Bell et al. (2015) state, “the ability to dissociate are seen as a client’s strength, and something counselors can use to draw on clients’ successes” (Bell et al., 2015, p. 161). They recommend counselors “take steps to promote resilience in treatment… setting small and attainable goals for the client, focusing and building on strengths and past successes, and reframing failure to meet goals as practicing for success” (Bell et al., 2015, p. 161). Through understanding a client’s plot, characterization, and creating a strength-based approach, clients can write a story for their future that is positive and uplifting (Pierce, 2014).

## Professional Application

There is a lot of information throughout this paper that counselors should consider applying to their work, and there are some other aspects that need to be highlighted. Clinicians should know the symptoms and act appropriately according to their ethical obligations.

When working with a client with DID, it is important to understand that the clinician is working with a system that has been functioning a particular way since childhood. Any changes to the system should be understood by all parts of the system to keep the client safe (Ringrose, 2011).

## Appendix A

### Limitations to Research

In terms of contributions to the field of clinical mental health counseling, there is a lot of research that still needs to be done regarding DID. Bell et al. (2015) state “empirical research surrounding DID remains limited” (p. 152). The field needs to increase the amount of research involving people with DID to best understand how to treat them. The research regarding treating DID is also lacking (Biswas et al., 2013, p. 46), which makes it difficult for counselors to know the best course of treatment for their clients with DID. The best way for counselors to prevent harm and increase well-being is to know what is beneficial to their clients, and the best way to know this information is to research it. So, counselors need to take action to increase the literature regarding DID and the best courses of treatment of this disorder.
