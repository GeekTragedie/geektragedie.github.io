<p class="paper-title"><strong>Critical Conversation</strong></p>

There are multiple ways of taking notes when reading academic articles. This paper gives two examples of how to take information from an article, put it into your own words, and reflect on the content to see how the article may fit into a research paper. The article used for the purpose of this paper is *Social Justice Advocacy: Community Collaboration and Systems Advocacy* by Lopez-Baez and Paylo (2009).

## Summary

The article *Social Justice Advocacy: Community Collaboration and Systems Advocacy* by Lopez-Baez and Paylo (2009) encourages counselors to advocate for their clients who may be experiencing oppression in their lives that causes issues in their functioning.

## First Passage

This section discusses a passage from Lopez-Baez and Paylo’s (2009) article on that carries over from page 278-279. The passage that is paraphrased and responded to can be found in Appendix A.

### Paraphrase

Lopez-Baez and Paylo (2009) discuss steps that should be taken by a counselor to advocate for their clients through teaming up with community members who may be able to work together to solve the systemic issue at hand (pp. 278-279).  The steps Lopez-Baez and Paylo discuss are creating an alliance with members at the school, contacting the client’s mother, and contacting any other agencies that may be involved in the incident at hand; in the example given in this passage, the counselor contacts the Department of Social Services because they were involved with the client’s case (p. 279).

### Response

When I first read this passage, I was drawn to an idea I learned in my Family of Origins course.  Nichols (2008) describes the Bowen’s Family Therapy theory that discusses creating triangles in terms of relationships (pp. 127-128).  For example, let’s say person A has an issue with person B.  Person A does not want to confront person B, so they go to person C.  Person A and C then collaborate against person B and the issue between person A and B creates anxiety in those around them (pp. 127-128).  I felt as though through this passage, the authors encouraged readers to create triangles.  I felt as though if many people got together to go against the workers at the transportation company, then the transportation company workers may feel threatened.  Feeling threatened, those in transportation company may be more resistant to change because they feel as though they were ganged up on.  They may feel as though they are being bullied, as Danny does.  While I think it is good that people are getting together and collaborating to make a change, I feel as though creating this group to go against another group is an unhealthy route.  It may cause anxiety within the communication between groups.  It makes me wonder what *would* be the best way to approach this situation, if I felt as though this way doesn’t quite feel right?

### Comments on Response

Lopez-Baez and Paylo (2009) encourage counselors that want to advocate for their clients to “create[e] an alliance” with other members in the client’s life to face the systemic issue at hand (pp. 278-279). Bowen’s System Theory (as cited in Nichols, 2008) explains the idea of triangulation as bringing in a third-party member to decrease your own anxiety against the other party (p. 127-128). Bowen states this tactic is an unhealthy way of dealing with conflict. The way Lopez-Baez and Paylo explain their advocacy steps appears to be a form of triangulation, and in the example provided, the therapist gathers allies from the school, family, and Department of Social Services to go against the members at a transportation service. When gathering these third-party groups, the therapist is creating multiple unhealthy triangles and the workers at the transportation company may feel teamed up against. When someone feels teamed up on, they can react in defensive ways to support their way of functioning, decreasing the likelihood of them changing their ways. The system should change and it is important for counselors to advocate for their clients; however, Lopez-Baez and Paylo’s advice for advocacy and systemic change appears unhealthy and may cause more conflict rather than positive change. So, what *would* be the best way to advocate and support a client?

## Passage Two

This section discusses a passage from Lopez-Baez and Paylo’s (2009) article found on page 281. The passage that is paraphrased and responded to can be found in Appendix B.

### Paraphrase

In this passage, Lopez-Baez and Paylo (2009) are asking counselors to do research regarding the issues the client is experiencing and use the findings to lobby for change (p. 281).  Lewinian’s force field analysis (as cited in Lopez-Baez & Paylo, 2009) supports the idea that research positively influences systemic change (p. 281).

### Response

I think this research is important for others to see, and it will probably change some people’s minds about the topic of bullying.  Some people may view it and decide to join in to advocate, which is good for them. I, on the other hand, do not have the time in my life to research and advocate for a client of mine.  I know it is extremely selfish, but I do have a life of my own.  I love helping people, but time for self-care is crucial and I need my time outside of my job to provide care for myself and my family.  I’m a person that needs to leave work at work.  If I am doing research and advocating for a single patient outside of my own work time, that seems too unrealistic to me.  I will be honest and say, I don’t want to do that.  Should I do that? In a perfect world, I would.  If my child had the same issue as my client, I might use my own time to advocate and make a change, but there is only so much work I can personally do.

Maybe there will come a time where I feel propelled to advocate for my clients.  If I’m truly invested in a cause, I could see myself advocating for my clients.  I could see myself advocating for rehabilitation in prisons and juvenile detention centers.  Those are the facilities I mainly want to work in, and if the facility is systematically not treating my clients right, I will advocate for them!

I guess I will have to pick and choose my advocacy battles.  If I do that, wouldn’t that show favoritism? How do you advocate what you are passionate about without leaving some of your clients out?

### Comments on Response

While many counselors may find it important to advocate for their clients, I believe it is important for the counselor to keep their self-care in mind. Lopez-Baez and Paylo (2009) describe counselors advocating for their clients through doing outside research (p. 281). Research takes a lot of time to conduct: finding articles, reading the articles, and then applying the information. The research needed can take time out of a counselor’s personal life to conduct. It may be viewed as selfish, but I need time in my life to relax, spend time with family, and take care of myself. I like to keep my personal life and professional life separate and feel as though Lopez-Baez and Paylo’s argument would result in decreasing the counselor’s self-care time. However, counselors may need to become involved in their clients’ life in order to best support them. If the majority of the counselors’ clients are facing a major issue, it may be important for the counselor to become an advocate and research the issue to create a solution. The counselor needs to be invested in the problem to best advocate and research to find a solution. If the counselor decides to advocate for their client, it is recommended for them to make sure they receive enough self-care time to balance out the extra research work.
