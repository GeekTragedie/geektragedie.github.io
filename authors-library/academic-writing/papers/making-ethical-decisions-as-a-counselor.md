## Making Ethical Decisions as a Counselor

Licensed clinical mental health counselors (LMHCs) have many ethical decisions to make in their careers. It is important for clinicians to know and follow the guidelines of their accredited association. As a future LMHC in the state of Washington, I will be obligated to follow Washington state law and the American Counseling Association (ACA)’s code of ethics. While I am still in graduate school, it is difficult for me to grasp the practicality of ethical issues arising in my future work. To supplement my learning, I interviewed two practicing counselors in Washington and asked them about ethical dilemmas they have faced in their practice. These interviews allowed me to apply my ethical knowledge to a real-life event, making my education appear more realistic. In this paper, I utilize Corey et al.’s approach to ethical dilemmas to analyze two issues LMHCs have faced during their counseling careers.

## Background

Before describing the ethical dilemmas faced by clinicians, it is important to understand the model that will be used in the analysis of these situations. Corey et al. (2019) analyzed various models of ethical decision making and created a list of steps a person should take before choosing a course of action (pp. 19-23). Corey et al. recommend the following steps:

> identify the problem or dilemma... identify potential issues involved… review the relevant ethics codes… know the applicable laws and regulations… obtain consultation… consider possible and probable courses of action… enumerate and consider the possible consequences of various decisions… choose what appears to be the best course of action. (pp. 20-23)

It is important for a counselor to also document these steps as they consider their course of action to ensure others understand the thought process behind the final decision. These stages help a clinician look at a dilemma from multiple perspectives and take making the decision seriously. Taking these steps can ensure clinicians make the best choice for themselves and their clients.

## Self-Care & Organizational Issue

### Information

As a clinician at a treatment center, Counselor A navigated two interwoven ethical dilemmas: lack of self-care and organizational conflict. Counselor A realized that she has been too stressed with her job and was experiencing burn out (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). Corey et al. (2019) describe burnout as “a state of physical, emotional, intellectual, and spiritual depletion characterized by feelings of helplessness and hopelessness” (p. 60). She gave all her energy to her many clients at the center and went home with no motivation to take time for herself. Counselor A then became concerned that she was not giving her clients the amount of attention they deserved due to her burn out. Upon this realization, she knew a change needed to be made (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019).

Before taking action regarding her burn out, Counselor A thought about what may be the cause of her burn out and realized it was her work environment (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). There are not enough offices in her center; there are not enough counselors in her center; the supervisors do not listen to the suggestions and needs of the counselors. After realizing the cause of her burn out, Counselor A took action (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019).

### Action Taken

When realizing she was faced with these ethical dilemmas, Counselor A took actions to combat stay true to her ethical beliefs: increasing self-care and quitting her job (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). Self-care is known as “positive actions that promote wellness and effective coping” (Corey et al., 2019, p. 63). Counselor A made a plan to increase her amount of self-care and create boundaries for herself in her workplace to decrease the amount of stress she was taking home. Counselor A also made the decision to quit her job. Her needs were not being met when she attempted to set boundaries for herself and advocate for the best interest of her co-workers and clients. She quit her job at the center to find work that let her increase self-care, which she hopes will increase the amount of care she can provide her clients (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019).

### My Analysis

**Identify the issue.** Analyzing Counselor A’s actions regarding these ethical dilemmas, I would add more stages before making my decision on a course of action. Similar to Counselor A, I would identify the problem as therapist burnout with the issues involving organization conflict and lack of self-care. Counselor A’s organization is overloading their therapist’s caseload and not listening to counselors’ knowledge regarding best care practice (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). Therapist burnout can cause impairment and has the potential to cause harm to clients or limit the care clients may need because the therapist is not able to provide their best services (Corey et al., 2019, p. 60-61).

**Ethical codes and laws.** The ACA has written many ethical codes regarding Counselor A’s dilemma. By lacking on self-care, therapists have the possibility of breaking two “fundamental principles of professional ethical behavior:” beneficence and veracity (ACA, 2014, p. 3). Beneficence is “working for the good of the individual and society by promoting mental health and well-being” (ACA, 2014, p. 3). By not taking care of the self, a therapist cannot be a positive role model to others of good health and well-being. If a therapist is burnt out, they are not able to show veracity: “dealing truthfully with individuals with whom counselors come into professional contact” (ACA, 2014, p. 3). Burnout can impair one’s ability to function in a way they would like, which can cause a therapist to not be fully present and honest when working with a client.

The ACA fundamental principles are further supported later in the document in sections A and C. Section A.4.a. states that counselors must actively avoid harming their clients (ACA, 2014, p. 4). Becoming burnt out can potentially cause harm to clients, thus breaking this ethical code, which is supported by section C.2.g.: counselors need to “monitor themselves for signs of impairment from their own physical, mental, or emotional problems and refrain from offering or providing professional services when impaired” (ACA, 2014, p. 9). It also states that counselors need to assist fellow counselors and supervisors of impairment in the workplace to “prevent imminent harm to clients” (p. 9). Burnout is important for counselors to be aware of in themselves and others as it causes impairment in ethical decision making and can cause harm to others.

The ACA (2014) also addresses organizational conflicts in terms of ethics. According to the ACA (2014) section D.1.g., it is the job of the counselor to accept an institution's policies and principles regarding “acceptable standards of client care and professional conduct” (p. 10). In this case, Counselor A did not agree with the way the institution regarded acceptable care for their clients (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). The ACA Code of Ethics does state,

> If the demands of an organization with which counselors are affiliated pose a conflict with the *ACA Code of Ethics,* counselors specify the nature of such conflicts and express to their supervisors… their commitment to the *ACA Code of Ethics* and, when possible, work through the appropriate channels to address the situation. (ACA, 2014, p.19)

So, Counselor A has a responsibility to the ACA to discuss her issues with the organization to her supervisor to help change the policies that do not follow the ethical codes or cause therapists to not follow the ethical codes.

**Possible courses of action.** There are many courses of action to take in this case including, but not limited to, seeking consultation, increasing self-care, discussing ethical issues with the organization, and quitting. My first course of action before making any decision would be to seek consultation from a trusted counselor or supervisor not associated with the institution. Corey et al. (2019) includes consultation within the stages of approaching an ethical dilemma (p. 22), and the ACA (2014) also states that if a counselor does not know what action to take, they should seek consultation (p. 19). A different perspective on the situation can help a counselor understand all the possibilities of the situation and whether to take further action. The consultant can also help brainstorm possible consequences for the possible courses of action.

A simultaneous course of action should be to increase levels of self-care both inside and outside the workplace. Inside the workplace, Corey et al. (2019) suggest taking more time for yourself, gaining peer support, and “avoid[ing] making your work the center of your life” (p. 62). To aid with Corey et al.’s suggestions, Counselor A could set personal boundaries with her supervisors and herself to try decreasing her workload and taking more time to check in with herself. Counselor A can also create a ritual for herself when leaving the workplace so she leaves does not take the stress of work home. When not at work, Counselor A may need to do more self-care. Corey et al. (2019) suggest making therapeutic lifestyle changes for self-care, such as “exercise, nutrition and diet, time in nature, relationships, recreation, relaxation, stress management, [and] religious or spiritual involvement” (p. 63). By increasing self-care, Counselor A can attempt to minimize harm to clients and herself.

A course of action that should be taken is to discuss the ethical issues with a supervisor at the organization, because it upholds the *ACA Code of Ethics* section D.1.h. and the introduction of section I. These codes state which state that a counselor should inform their employers if policies do not support ethical practices and take action to “ensure that standards are upheld” (pp. 19). Actions were taken to change these policies, such as advocating for the benefits of the clients (A.7.a., p. 5), monitoring effectiveness (C.2.d., p. 8), and monitoring the wellness of team members (D.1.c., p. 10). Counselor A took these steps with her supervisors, but no action was taken to change the environment of the institution (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). With no action taken by supervisors, Counselor A could discuss burnout with co-workers to brainstorm ways of creating a healthier work environment or work together to request changes to the institution level as a larger group. This option could benefit all of the employees, future clinicians and, in turn, the clients. This course of action still has the possibility of no change to the work environment if not listened to by the employers.

**Recommended course of action.** If I were in the situation as Counselor A, I would take all the steps listed in possible courses of action to see if my needs start to be met. If I did not foresee change happening, I would search for a job elsewhere, which is what Counselor A decided to do (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). I want a job cares about my talents and respects me as a clinician. I do not want to be in a position where change is required to prevent harm from clients. I also do not want to work for an institution that does not respect my moral beliefs. When quitting, I would ensure to note the reasons for leaving to possibly help current and future counselors employed at the center.

To end this ethical dilemma discussion, Counselor A was considerate enough to give her institution three months notice before leaving (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). The institution is realizing how much work Counselor A was putting into her job (Counselor A, personal communication, August 2019). Maybe the center is realizing change needs to happen, and they should start supporting their clinicians more but the story is not finished yet.

## Harm to Self Issue

### Information

Counselor B faced an ethical dilemma involving a teenage client possibly harming themselves by taking an unknown amount of an illegal drug (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019). The client did not say how much they had taken. Counselor B did not know if the amount the teen took was life threatening and questioned whether or not to disclose the information to an authority figure outside of the therapeutic alliance (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019).

### Action Taken

When faced with this dilemma, Counselor B took multiple actions before settling on a decision with this client. In the therapy session, Counselor B decided to assess the client for suicidal ideation (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019). Counselor B noted that life-threatening harm is reportable and wanted to know the intentions and motivations of the client. She noted that the client did not intend self harm, and that the client had no life-threatening intent going forward (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019).

Counselor B decided to bring this dilemma to her weekly consultation team (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019). The team told Counselor B that breaking confidentiality could harm the therapeutic relationship and the teen did not plan on taking their own life or the life of someone else. The team advised Counselor B to not take action outside of the counseling and suggested Counselor B continue to ask the teen about their drug use to ensure that it does not cause self-harm in the future (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019).

It is important to consider possible damage to a therapeutic relationship when making ethical considerations. Counselor B believed that if she broke the adolescent’s confidentiality, their relationship would suffer, and the client would not be as open to share information in the future (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019). With consideration of the relationship and the lack of life-threatening harm, Counselor B made the decision to not break confidentiality.

### My Analysis

If I were faced with this dilemma in my future work, I would follow very similar steps as Counselor B. Before deciding a course of action, she identified the issue, considered ethical codes regarding the issue, sought consultation, and considered different courses of actions and their consequences.

**Identify the issue.** The ethical issue in this case is whether or not to break confidentiality with the client due to a possible life-threatening situation and the use of an illegal drug. In terms of counseling and confidentiality, “mental health professionals have an ethical responsibility, as well as a legal and professional duty, to safeguard clients from unauthorized disclosure of information” (p. 208). The amount of drugs the teenage client is using could be life-threatening, but the client is choosing to not disclose the amount. I would also add the use of illegal drugs to the ethical dilemma; Counselor B is also faced with whether to report the illegal activity to the police.

**Ethical codes and laws.** There are multiple ethical codes and regulations to take into consideration when considering breaking confidentiality with a client.

**Consultation.** It is important to seek consultation to receive another person’s perspective and opinion on the matter. This ethical dilemma involves a client breaking the law, so I would recommend a counselor seek consultation from a counseling supervisor as well as a trusted attorney. Counselor B met with a team of fellow counselors, which is a good form of consultation, and it is important to note that a counselor could also seek consultation from a trusted supervisor as well.

**Possible courses of action.** After seeking consultation, a counselor should consider all possible courses of action and the consequences before making a decision. As a preventative measure before seeing clients, a counselor should ensure that their informed consent includes a section informing their clients about confidentiality. When meeting any client for the first time, a counselor should read through the informed consent form to ensure the client understands what rights they have regarding their information (Corey et al., 2019, p. 208). While making an ethical decision regarding confidentiality and danger to self, a counselor should document all communication with a client, any consultation received, all courses of action considered, and any consequences considered (Corey et al., 2019, p. 208).

One course of action could be to report the illegal drug use and danger to self to the police and have the client involuntarily hospitalized. This course does not seem ethically sound because there is not enough evidence to prove the client wants to end their life.  Ethical codes of confidentiality also protect the client from a counselor turning them into the police. This course of action could ensure that the adolescent was safe, but the counselor risks the trust built in the therapeutic relationship.

Another course of action is break confidentiality and involuntarily hospitalize the client for possible danger to self. While there is a small argument that the client could pose harm to themselves via drug use, there is no intent or motivation from the client to end their life. The counselor would keep the client safe, but risks making an ethical code violation and breaking the trust in the therapeutic alliance with the client.

A third course of action is to uphold confidentiality and discuss the dangers the client faces with drug use. This option supports a trusting therapeutic alliance and practices the client’s rights to confidentiality in the relationship. The client is able to still obtain information regarding substance use and there is a trusting adult, the counselor, that is aware and monitoring substance use in the case of possible addiction. Using psychoeducation, the clinician can inform the client about harmful substances to prevent accidental self-harm, and the client still has the choice to continue the behavior if wanted. While the behavior is risky, it is not the counselor’s decision. This option does risk the client misusing the substances or getting caught with the illegal substance outside of the relationship.

A final course of action could be to refer the client to a clinician who is a chemical dependency professional (CDP). This option ensures the client is working with a counselor who understands their substance use. The counselor would not have to worry about the ethical dilemma of possible danger to self. However, there are ethical codes that can come into play when a clinician over-refers clients (Corey et al., 2019, p. 76). A clinician cannot make discriminatory referrals, so a clinician needs to note why a referral is being made first. If the client was coming to see Counselor B for other concerns, she can focus on those items. Counselor B can also become more knowledgeable about substance use within her scope of practice. If the client specifically wanted to work on their substance use, referring out is a helpful option. However, if Counselor B refers this adolescent to a CDP, she risks the client’s parents being suspicious of possible substance use.

**Recommended course of action.** After reviewing ethical codes and possible courses of action, I agree with Counselor B’s course of action in this case, option three: not breaking confidentiality and assessing future drug use motivation and intention (Counselor B, personal communication, August 2019). This course of action upholds the client’s legal and ethical right to confidentiality, keeps the therapeutic alliance intact, and ensures the drug use is being monitored by an outside source.
