## Group Proposal Theoretical Orientation

I am proposing a therapy group using the drama therapy theoretical orientation titled Healing the Wounds of History (HWH) (Appendix A). HWH is a drama therapy technique developed by Armand Volkas in which the therapist uses “theatre techniques to work with a group of participants from two cultures with a common legacy of conflict and historical trauma” (Volkas, 2009, p. 146). Historical trauma is “collective, cumulative, emotional wounding across generations resulting from cataclysmic events… target[ing] not just individuals, but a whole collective community” (University of Minnesota Extension, n.d.). Utilizing this orientation, the purpose of my group proposal is to address historical trauma between Native and Euro-Americans to help prevent the transition of trauma to next generations.

I am working to become a drama therapist because I deeply believe that healing happens through collective and somatic experiencing. I am drawn to the drama therapy technique HWH because its theoretical tenets deeply resonate with me. The first goal is “recognizing and deconstructing cultural or national identity” (Volkas, 2009, p. 147). I am a naturally curious person and enjoy the Socratic method of getting to understand why someone behaves the way they do. This curiosity also applies to understanding others’ culture, which is what interests me about HWH because it draws from the collective conscious and unconscious.

The second tenet is resolving intercultural conflict and learning non-violent communication (Volkas, 2009). I have always been drawn to looking at situations from multiple perspectives and understanding the ripple effect that can come from these perceptions. For example, I enjoyed going to the World War II museum in Kiev, Ukraine to see how the war influenced people in a different country. These multiple perceptions, however, can cause major conflict between people who do not yet know how to use healthy communication skills. I want to help be the change in these negative communication habits and bring forth more non-violent communication pathways.

The third tenet is helping members understand their personal experience and collective grief (Volkas, 2009). I am drawn to working with marginalized populations because they are people who have been outcast from society and deserve compassion. I want to create a dialogue that can start a ripple effect on the US to decrease stereotyping and stigma and I feel this orientation can do, and has done, just that. The Guerrand-Hermès Foundation for Peace (2018) is assisting teachers of refugees from Lebanon and Syria. The main goal of their HWH program is to decrease the transmission of hatred and conflict from the previous generations. Their overarching goal is to decrease the conflict between the governments of Lebanon and Syria, but their ripple effect is starting small (Guerrand-Hermès Foundation for Peace, 2018). I want to have a goal similar to this foundation and bring peace to my own communities in the United States.

To influence that change, another tenet is “create a culture of empathy” (Volkas, 2009, p. 148). I want to be a social justice advocate for marginalized populations. I want to use my power and privilege as a counselor to advocate for my clients. Through building bridges in advocacy work between diverse backgrounds, I want to influence the community to bring awareness and justice and hopefully create a society with empathy.

The most important tenet that draws me to HWH is “create meaning out of suffering” (Volkas, 2009, p. 148). I am drawn to mindfulness and the idea of being one with suffering. Having lost my brother, I have learned the need to find meaning from suffering. I want to bring this lesson to aid other people in times of suffering, especially suffering that has been passed down through generations.

One other item that resonates with me in HWH is the idea that everyone has a perpetrator within themselves (Dintino & Johnson, 1997). Due to stereotypes and stigma, it is impossible for someone to not have biases, which is why critical thinking skills are important. By listening to someone else’s story and empathizing, a person can realize how their behaviors or microaggressions influence someone else. The person can then realize that they are not perfect and are, in fact, a perpetrator. By finding the perpetrator within, a person can better empathize with those they view as perpetrators (Dintino & Johnson, 1997).

## Group Experience Reflection

### Group Member

A role I took on in the group process was that of the overfunctioner. I often assume this role in other group settings and in my family of origin, feeling like a parental child growing up. The way that I found myself overfunctioning in the group was feeling the needs of the facilitators and offering information to decrease their facilitation anxiety, even though it did not particularly serve my own purposes. This role normally serves me because I have a particular way of wanting things done to please others. To get things done the way I would like, I overfunction for other people to prevent myself from being let down. I resonate with the saying, “if you want something done, get it done yourself.” Though, I would have appreciated a different role in the group because I believe this overfunctioning role leads to a lack of trust in others. Maybe I would have gained more trust in others or the group would have learned something new if they had followed their own impulses instead of my overfunctioning ones.

My cultural identity came into play as a group member in the termination session due to my religious beliefs involving death. I am Christian and believe a large majority of people go to heaven after dying. At termination, I do not feel I would be saying a permanent goodbye to anyone because, no matter what happens after the closure of the group, everyone in the group will be with me in heaven after we die. On a negative note, my religious affiliation prevents me from believing in a permanent ending, but others may believe that to be true. In reflection, I wonder how I can help facilitate a healthy goodbye for those who believe it is permanent.

Though my group goals are still a work in progress, I worked towards accomplishing these goals during the group process. My first goal was to be more open to receiving constructive feedback from other members in a non-defensive manner. I really do not know how I worked on this goal. I do not remember receiving too much constructive feedback. I took information that was given in class and then adapted my personality and role in the group to that information. For example, in class, it was taught to not let the same person go first in the group. From previous reflection on my role in the classroom, I know that I am one to answer questions first because I want to decrease the anxiety the teachers or facilitators from looking for an answer. I stopped being the person to answer first in the group. I was not given criticism for this action though.

My second goal was to be more comfortable approaching conflict with another member if I was hurt by someone else’s actions. I took the risk of confronting a group member I was angry with. I shared my frustration in session. While this risk was on behalf of the group and still in line with my overfunctioning role, I was happy to have modeled this risk taking and sharing of anger for myself and others. Trying to take what I have learned from the group into my real life, I have been thinking of ways to share my anger with others in a healthy way.

### Group Leader

I did not have the opportunity to accomplish both of my leader goals for this quarter. My first goal was to make a drama therapy technique approachable and insightful for group members. I am proud to have accomplished this goal but would not like to take up space in this paper by reflecting more on this goal, as I reflected on it in a previous paper. My second goal was to respond to a somewhat difficult member. I did this, in a way, when working with [a group member] in session to not take personally [their] resistance to my drama therapy intervention. I became the person of which [they] projected [their] negative feelings on, which is okay. Though, I did not have the opportunity to deal with other difficult members, as many members in the group are fellow counseling students and were very willing to aid facilitators in their process.

My cultural identity influenced my leader role through gender, religious affiliation, and age. In previous reflections, I have addressed how my gender and religious affiliation showed up in session. I have now noticed that I felt inadequate to facilitate the group because most, if not all, of the members were older than me. I need to work on building my confidence in helping people who have had more life experiences than me. Just because I am younger does not mean my perspective or gifts are not helpful to others.

I had many successful moments while leading the group this quarter. I have always been confident in my ability to use silence in sessions, and I believe I used silence a good amount to challenge members to speak more. Sometimes my co-facilitator filled the silence but that is entirely welcomed! I was happy with [a question I asked one group member]. My reasoning behind this choice point was to allow [the member] space to express [their feelings] in session. Upon reflection, it would have been nice to use this as a linking activity by having [the member] speak to someone else as if they were [the people involved]. I take this idea from the film shown in class where Corey and Corey facilitated group members role playing for each other. One reason I did not do this is because I do not quite feel comfortable with my abilities to hold space for that intervention yet in my group leadership development. This practice group should have been a place where I could explore that, but I did not want to risk harm or create an unsafe space. This moment of confidence also turned into a challenging moment for me.

While I had many moments that challenged me as a leader, I can only discuss a few in this limited space. One challenge was working with [a group member]’s negative reaction to my drama therapy technique and learning to not take it personally. The second session was challenging for me. There was a lot of deep emotion expressed in the group, and I became anxious about my ability to fully hold space for those feelings. I have been told I hold space for others well, but I always second-guess myself. I am challenged by my own self-confidence in my ability to hold space. I should always be curious of my choices to prevent myself from becoming complacent, but I should not allow this curiosity to influence my self-esteem or self-confidence in skills I hold. I am comforted in the task that Moreno (2007) gives to group therapists: protect. If I can view myself as a protector, not just to a scapegoated client in the group, but for my marginalized clients, then I can increase my confidence in my role and ability. I will be working with clients that are scapegoated by society, so it is even more crucial for me to not allow clients in the group sessions to be scapegoated.

## Overall Learning

I learned a lot about myself by participating in the group counseling process. I learned the subtle ways that I overfunction, and I try to disguise my overfunctioning defense mechanism from myself and others. I can make drama therapy techniques approachable to counselors that do not normally do theatre activities. I can create a plan for a group session. I have knowledge of what to do with a difficult client and what may be going on in their mind. I now may be less scared to approach conflicts with other people, especially if it is causing me pent-up stress; the other person may actually appreciate it. My biggest take-away from this course is that I have learned to trust the group counseling process.

## Future Application of Knowledge

Given my successes and challenges this quarter, I have certain strengths as a leader as well as skills that have room for improvement. My strengths as a group facilitator are to listen to group members, feel somatic impulses from members, and create a safe space for group members to explore emotions. I also have a strength in remaining curious about individuals’ stories and being unjudgmental about experiences.

I still have room for improvement as a group facilitator in trusting the process. While this class helped me in trusting the process, I know I will have to constantly remind myself to do this. Being an overfunctioner, I tend to over plan, and if I over plan a session, I could miss important moments in the session to dive deeper into the here-and-now processing. These skills that need improvement are my main concerns when thinking about facilitating my own group in the future. My plans to work through my main concerns include continuing to practice group counseling in my internship. I will do personal therapy to aid my overfunctioning boundaries. I also plan to always have supervision to catch any perspectives that I might miss. Continuing my education about group therapy and the group process will aid my knowledge base and confidence in my abilities.
