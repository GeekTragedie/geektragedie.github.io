<p class="paper-title"><strong>Spontaneous Therapy: Using Improvisation in Therapy</strong></p>

Improvisation has therapeutic effects on those who participate in it, whether or not there was intention behind these effects. Drama therapists can offer their clients the therapeutic effects of improvisation alongside therapy benefits. Improvisation has therapeutic impacts on participants that can also be applied in therapy settings with incarcerated populations; this paper gives examples of specific activities and how they can benefit this population.

## Improvisation in Therapy

Before considering improvisation techniques with inmates, it is key to look at how improvisation can be therapeutic. When conducting therapy with a group, Ormont (1992) states there are five benefits (p. 29), which improvisation with groups also supports. Group therapy allows “self-destructive behavior… on-the-spot self-definition … [and] diverse views of behavior” (Ormont, 1992, p. 29). Atkins (1994) describes how difficult it is to be yourself, not knowing how others may react to you: “as adults… we go through life consciously stopping our thought processes and squelching our impulses” (p. 8). Improvisation allows people to trust their “gut instincts and go with them” (Atkins, 1994, p. 8). Spolin (1999) explains how improvisation is spontaneous (Chapter 1), which demands clients to react to situations on-the-spot, which may be “self-destructive behavior” (Ormont, 1992, p. 29). Spontaneity sparks a “process of transcending structure itself – the process of questioning definitions and making redefinitions” (Blatner & Blatner, 1997, p. 5), allowing change to on-the-spot reactions and a different view of behaviors that may be present within clients.

Working in groups also allows “the chance to practice new behavior …  [and] enable[s] the members to see how others respond to them” (Ormont, 1992, p. 29). With improvisation being spontaneous (Spolin, 1999), clients can create new worlds and interactions with each other (Wiener, 1994, p. xv). By creating these new interactions, clients can “try out possible roles by enacting them dramatically” (Wiener, 1994, p. xv) so they can see how others may react to this new role, thus creating the benefit Ormont strives for in group therapy.

## Improvisation with Inmates

When implementing improvisation into therapy, it is important to keep the goals of the clients in mind. According to Frisch and Emery (n.d.), some treatment goals when working with inmates are: “changing dysfunctional attitudes, beliefs and values, and criminal behavior… lower drug usage within the institution… decrease violent, actingout behaviors… improve custody status… lower stress… [and] increase inmates’ facility in interactional communication skills and improve interpersonal relations” (p. 4). These goals were considered when selecting the following improvisation activities: Yes, Because; Getting Connected, and Coma.

### Yes, Because

Carrane and Allen (2006) describe the exercise Yes, Because Circle, (p. 22) which can be found in Appendix A. By changing the original instructions, this exercise can be used with incarcerated individuals to help reach their treatment goals. In the modified version of this exercise, clients create statements and say them to their neighbor client. The neighbor then responds to the statement to enhance the reason why the statement was made (Carrane & Allen, 2006, p. 22). The therapist can prompt the clients’ statements to gain information regarding the clients’ goals. To align with treatment goals for inmates, the therapist can ask for statements regarding their criminal behavior, values, feeling towards substance use, or stress level. When the clients respond to these statements to elaborate why, it can increase interpersonal relations. An example of this interaction may be: Client 1, “I’m glad I robbed that bank;” Client 2, “Yes because your son really needed that money for his surgery.”

### Getting Connected

Getting Connected is another exercise described by Carrane and Allen (2006, p. 42) which can be found in Appendix B. At the beginning of this exercise, partners watch each other walking around in the space and gauge how the other person is feeling through non-verbal communication. A therapist can use this exercise to show inmates how their behavior or beliefs are read by others and increase interpersonal communication when the clients study their partner’s body language and emotion. Drawing attention to how an inmate is seen by others can help the inmate possibly understand their violent behaviors. Clients then assign relationships between the partners, which the therapist can use to evoke different therapy goals, depending on the clients’ history. The other partner then specifies what is going on in the scene, which the therapist can push the client to work on an issue real to them by reinforcing “that scenes need to have stakes, life-and-death stakes” (Carrane & Allen, 2006, p. 43). The high stakes in a scene may push the client to face their values, criminal behavior, or stress level. An example of how this exercise may play out follows: Client 1, he appears calm; Client 2, he seems pissed off; Client 1, we are brothers; Client 2, he turned me into the cops after I killed my wife.

### Coma

Coma is another game used by Carrane and Allen (2006, p. 38), described in Appendix C, that can be used with inmates to achieve treatment goals. In this exercise, one client is unable to speak while the other client talks to that client and acts as though they cannot hear. This exercise helps one client control their behavior, as they are unable to respond to what the other person in the scene is saying. The other client can also explore different levels of their emotions, as they must continue the scene. Clients can express their thoughts regarding anyone in their life, because the other role is not specified. The exercise continues by the client who spoke before is rendered silent and the previous silent client can choose a time before or after the previous scene to elaborate on the relationship. The second client is unable to choose their role, which demands creativity and taking a different point of view. The first speaking partner can learn the point of view of the person they just talked at. Any treatment goal items can surface during the process of choosing how the client is unable to hear or speak, assigning the roles, and responding to the scene partner. An abridged example of this exercise may be: C2 is in a coma, C1 yells at C2 for attempting suicide and the feelings regarding their relationship, C1 is asleep in their room, C2 chooses the night before the coma and says goodbye to C1, telling them it is not their fault.

## Conclusion

Improvisation is a good technique to use when working with clients as the group can benefit from the demands of improvisation. Therapy goals can be worked through during modified improvisation exercises matching specific goals. It is important to consider the therapy goals of the clients when selecting exercises to facilitate in sessions and how to organize a specific session to match a population’s therapy goals. While a therapist still plans for a session, improvisation still is spontaneous therapy.

## Appendix D

### Introduction

Rap – Society has a nice systemic way of boxing you up and hiding you away. This creates a lack of trust you can have other people, so you have to put on a mask to help you fend for yourself. Well if you are only taking care of yourself, how can you go out into the world and know what others expect? Today, we are going to try to peek a little bit behind the security mask together and try to understand how others show what’s behind their mask. Maybe others feel the same way being shoved away from society. They may not fully get what you feel, and some frustration may arise, but we’re all in the same box, so let’s try and make the best of it.

### Body

- Yes, Because
  - Start in a circle
  - Explain the game
  - One person makes a statement regarding how you are feeling so far this quarter
  - The next person says “yes [repeats the person’s statement] because” then states why the person may have said that statement
    - The “because” can either relate to the other person personally or can be completely fantasy
    - Example:
      - A: I am drowning this quarter!
      - B1: Yes because your classes are demanding more from you this quarter.
      - B2: Yes because you were so tired of treading water in the middle of the ocean that you started to sink!
  - The next person in the circle creates a new statement. Once everyone has gone, start with someone else so everyone has a chance to do a statement and a because
- Emotional Fruit Salad
  - Explain the game
    - I’m going to call out an emotion. When I call that emotion out, find a partner to emote that emotion with. I will then call out another emotion; find a new partner to emote that emotion with. If I go back to an emotion already stated, find the same partner you shared that emotion with.
    - Give an example
  - Facilitator asks everyone to walk around the space
  - Find someone to be silly with
  - Find someone to be angry with
  - Find someone to be sad with
  - Find someone to be suspicious of
- Getting Connected
  - Walk around the room
  - Make eye contact with someone and they are your new partner
  - Study your partner, look at their whole body
    - How do they feel?
    - How are they expressing that feeling?
  - Get closer to your partner
  - Ask each person what they thought their partner’s emotion was
  - Have one partner decide the relationship
  - Have the other partner decide what is happening in the scene
  - Ask the clients to have high stakes for what is going on in the scene
  - Have clients do their scenes, all at once
  - Ask the scenes to come to a close

### Closure

- Mindfulness
  - Walk around the space
  - Shake off the characters you have played today
  - Connect to your breath
  - Take a deep breath in and out
