<p class="paper-title"><strong>Depressive Reframing: Viewing Persistent Depressive Disorder through a DBT Lens</strong></p>

Based on the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5*’s (*DSM-V)* diagnostic criteria of persistent depressive disorder (PDD), Reichenberg and Seligman (2016) describe it as “a chronic, low-grade depression that persists for years, and sometimes decades, robbing people of pleasure, of hope, and sometimes even of their lives” (p. 157). While there is research to support multiple types of medication to treat PDD (Kriston, von Wolff, Westphal, Holzen, & Harter, 2014), it is also recommended to combine any medication form of treatment with a form of counseling. Dialectical behavioral therapy (DBT) is a form of behavioral therapy found to be beneficial treating depressive symptoms (Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh & Lenz, 2018ab; Lenz, Taylor, Fleming, & Serman, 2014; Wadsworth et al., 2018; Cook & Gorraiz, 2016; Hejazi, Sobhi, & Sahrzad, 2014). The DBT treatment model and approach also aligns with the *DSM-V*’s criteria for diagnosing PDD. A new perspective of PDD can be taken by analyzing the *DSM-V* diagnostic criteria through a DBT lens.

## DBT Foundation & PDD

DBT’s theoretical base frames how a clinician views the symptoms of PDD. Sharf (2012) describes dialectical as an argument with a belief, “trying to find a resolution between two extremes” (pp. 324-325).  Sharf (2012) defines the behavioral aspect as behaviors that do not align with the belief system and may be self-destructive in nature (p. 325). According to DBT’s framework, someone with PDD has an internal argument where their behaviors are not be aligning with their value system.

DBT clinicians approach PDD behaviors from a strength-based perspective. A strong therapeutic relationship is important for DBT clinicians (Lenz et al., 2014, p. 27) and part of having a strong relationship while practicing DBT includes empathy (Sharf, 2012, p. 324). A DBT clinician views behaviors as having served a purpose before, but is no longer serving a client’s best interests (Sharf, 2012). From a human development standpoint, a client’s behaviors were developed either from “an individual’s biological predisposition” or “interactions with an invalidating environment” (Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh & Lenz, 2018b). Neither of these developments are of any fault to a client. So, from a DBT perspective, people with PDD either had a predisposition for their behaviors or created coping mechanisms to protect themselves; either way, these mechanisms no longer benefit the client and are now causing distress.

## Clinically Significant Distress

The American Psychiatric Association (APA)’s criteria of clinically significant distress can be enhanced by DBT’s foundational theory and treatment stages. People with PDD have behaviors that cause severe impairments affecting work and relationships and increase risk of suicide (Ishizaki & Mimura, 2011). If behaviors of depressed mood, difficult interpersonal relations, and increased thoughts of suicidal ideation oppose a person’s value system, which in most cultures it does, than DBT would believe there is distress in a client’s behaviors, aligning with the *DSM-V*’s criteria of clinically significant distress.

DBT’s framework of treatment also aligns with the idea of having clinically significant distress. DBT works from a hierarchical framework, treating the most severe, life-threatening behaviors first, followed by any behaviors that may negatively impact the counseling relationship (Sharf, 2012). Research has supported DBT’s effectiveness in decreasing life-threatening behaviors. Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh and Lenz (2018b)’s research shows that DBT reduces adolescent symptoms of depression, self-injury, and suicide risk. Cook and Gorraiz (2016) also provide research of DBT decreasing nonsuicidal self-injury and decreasing depressive symptoms. These symptoms can be life-threatening and interfere with counseling, so these findings show that DBT decrease the level of clinically significant distress a client may be facing. Client distress is of high importance in DBT’s role of treatment and can be the meaning between life and death in terms of clients with PDD.

## Depressed Mood

DBT’s view of dialectical and emphasis on interpersonal effectiveness align with the APA’s view of prolonged depressed mood when diagnosing PDD. The *DSM-V* criteria A states that person needs to have experienced depressed mood most of the day for a majority of days for at least two years, one year for children and adolescents (APA, 2013, p. 168). In the perspective of DBT, if an argument within one’s self goes along untreated, it does not seem surprising that the depressed mood would last over two years. If the symptoms are biological or the behaviors do not match a person’s surroundings, as defined by DBT, then it is probable that a diagnosis of one year for children or adolescents is given as well. It is important that a child or adolescent learn helpful behaviors earlier in their life to continue practicing the behavior throughout their life.

The depressed mood can be noticed by the individual experiencing the mood or by others close to the individual (APA, 2013, p. 168). This idea is enhanced by DBT’s emphasis on interpersonal effectiveness (Sharf, 2012, p. 326). DBT requires multiple actions to take place during treatment including, but not limited to, “individual therapy, psychoeducational skills groups, intersession phone coaching, and a consultation group for counselors” (Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh & Lenz, 2018a). Viewing a client’s interpersonal skills and relationships can give evidence towards DBT helping a person experiencing symptoms of PDD.

## Depressive Symptoms

The APA (2013) has specific criteria for depressive symptoms that differ DBT’s view of negative behaviors. The *DSM-V*’s PDD criteria states an individual needs to have experienced at least two of the following symptoms for the length of two years: “poor appetite or overeating, insomnia or hypersomnia, low energy or fatigue, low self-esteem, poor concentration or difficulty making decisions, [or] feelings of hopelessness” (APA, 2013, p. 168). DBT does view self-destructive behavior or distress from behaviors as notable for clients, but there is a discrete distinction between what the APA says and what DBT treats. DBT does not state that the symptoms listed are necessarily self-destructive or cause distress. DBT takes a cultural standpoint and only considers behaviors that are opposing to a client’s personal beliefs. Some of the symptoms listed above may be a cultural norm for non-western civilizations, so it is important that a DBT clinician knows what cultural factors influence a client’s behaviors.

While taking cultural factors into account, a DBT counselor still aids a client with their depressive symptoms, to include those listed in by the APA. DBT aims to increase quality of life by teaching skills to clients to learn or relearn coping mechanism, increase resilience and emotional regulation, and help with interpersonal effectiveness (Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh & Lenz, 2018a; Lenz et al., 2014). With PDD being a prolonged and possibly life-long disorder, DBT teaches the skills clients need to become resilient and hopeful in their life. Hejazi et al. (2014) state, “DBT gives patients another way to look at their life… [it] can be a model of resistance to hardships of life” (p. 394). DBT is also providing research efforts to decrease the cost of treatment for clients (Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh & Lenz, 2018a; Lenz et al., 2014). So, with PDD being a long-term disorder, DBT may be a good client fit to individualized depressive symptoms.

To help control or decrease a client’s depressive symptoms in criteria B of the *DSM-V* and beyond, DBT utilizes mindfulness, emotional regulation, and distress-tolerance. Mindfulness in DBT includes reasonable mind (logical thinking), emotional mind (emotional thoughts and mood), and wise mind (synthesis of reasonable and emotional) (Sharf, 2012, p. 326). These techniques can be utilized to battle all of the depressive criteria in the *DSM-V.* DBT also significantly aids with emotional regulation (Hunnicutt Hollenbaugh & Lenz, 2018a) which can increase self-esteem and decrease feelings of hopelessness. Finally, DBT teaches distress-tolerance (Sharf, 2012, p. 326), which can aid in resilience to help decrease the higher levels of relapse seen in PDD clients (Reichenberg & Seligman, 2016). DBT’s approach to therapy aligns with the *DSM-V*’s criteria of depressive symptoms but expands on the symptoms to make the criteria more individualized.

## Conclusion

In terms of PDD symptoms, a DBT approach aligns well with the *DSM-V* diagnosis, though there are different definitions for the main ideas and approaches. Looking at the *DSM-V* specific criteria, DBT may not diagnose someone with PDD due to the difference in symptom definitions and possible cultural factors. If the symptoms causing distress align with the exact list the *DSM-V* provides, then PDD may be diagnosed by a DBT clinician. Every case brought to a clinician will have to be taken into consideration individually.
