<p class="paper-title">President Donald Trump as Papa Ubu</p>

Often during presidential elections, people compare incumbent presidential candidates to historical figures to have a better sense of who they are voting for. During the United States’ 2016 presidential election cycle, people compared Donald Trump to other historical figures, such as Hitler (Schechner 7), Lenin, and Putin (Connolly S-23). In June of 2017, Isaac Reed added to this discussion when he published his article titled “Trump as Ubu Roi: On the charismatic appeal of vulgarity.” Catulle Mendes, quoted in Youker’s article, writes “Pere Ubu exists.” Throughout Reed’s essay, he helps show that Ubu is living today when he compares President Donald Trump to Papa Ubu in the play *Ubu the King*. Reed states that “Pere Ubu is, indeed, like Trump in his contradictory assertions, combination of supreme verbal cruelty and self-regarding cowardice, imperious manner, and vulgar mediations on women.” While he claims these idea, the remainder of the article compares Papa Ubu and President Trump’s speech acts and the comparison listed are not supported. Reed’s comparison can be seen as credible if one combines arguments discussing the character Papa Ubu and analyze the performance aspects of President Donald Trump. When making this comparison, it is also important to understand that there are possible consequences that can follow. This paper explores the ideas behind Isaac Reed’s comparison of Papa Ubu and President Donald Trump (“contradictory assertions…self-regarding cowardice, imperious manner, and vulgar mediations on women”) and discusses the possible consequences that may present themselves by making this comparison.

One of Reed’s first comparisons between Papa Ubu and President Trump is that both make contradictory assertions. Media outlets have commented that President Trump has contradicted himself on many occasions. John Cassidy of *The New Yorker* writes that President Trump contradicted himself when discussing nuclear warfare with North Korea and Iran and that he also contradicts himself constantly when it comes to foreign-policy issues. Cassidy warns that people need to “watch what \[Trump\] does, not what he says.” The Editorial Board of *The New York Times* discusses that Trump contradicted himself when discussing human rights in the Middle East. Not only have these sources considered President Trump’s words and actions contradictory, many scholars have also supported this accusation. Richard Grusin, in his article “Donald Trump’s Evil Mediation,” calls President Trump “lying” and “contradictory” (S-89). In a journal article in the *Kennedy Institute of Ethics Journal,* Shelley Park quotes Palmer claiming Trump “was deemed a ‘pathological liar’” (E-26). By looking at media outlets and the words of different scholars, it is seen that President Trump can be seen making contradictory assertions, as Reed claims. With establishing that President Trump can be contradictory, as Reed claims, it is easier to connect President Trump’s actions to that of Papa Ubu in *Ubu the King.*

Papa Ubu is contradicts himself in Alfred Jarry’s play with Garbage and saying he will fight alongside his men against the king. Papa Ubu tells Garbage that he has “decided to make \[Garbage\] Duke of Lithuania” (1.4.5). Papa Ubu tells Garbage that Papa Ubu considers Garbage a friend and that Papa Ubu and Mama Ubu were “happy to meet” Garbage (4.4.29-30). Papa Ubu contradicts himself when he goes back on his word, throwing Garbage “in jail on the pretext of conspiracy” (3.6.5). Papa Ubu also claims that he will stand up with his men and fight to kill the king. Papa Ubu claims he will “put \[him\]self in danger for” his men (1.7.19). Later, when they are fighting the king’s men, Papa Ubu gets scared and tells the men, “Get me out of here” (2.4.4). While Papa Ubu claims to stand up for his men, he does not fulfill this promise, is scared of the battle, and does not put himself in the way of danger. Papa Ubu not making Garbage the Duke of Lithuania nor fighting as he promises shows the contradictory nature of Papa Ubu. Both President Donald Trump and Papa Ubu are not truthful in the positions held. They lie to their people by not fulfilling the acting on what they say they will do.

The next claim made by Reed is that President Donald Trump and Papa Ubu both have self-regarding cowardice. How this can be defined is by one neglecting to face issues that may not benefit oneself and to ignore negative criticism regarding oneself. One way this trait can be seen in President Trump is in his “’monstrous spew of racist, sexist, and xenophobic proclamations” (Grusin S-90). Instead of tackling these issues in America and attempting to understand them, President Trump claims them as either non-existent an the enemy to America, such as the media. President Trump gains more support from wealthy, white, heterosexual, cisgender males in American, and blames the Other is easier than attempting to fix a problem. Many of the actions President Trump has done while in office have been for his own benefit and supporting the majority populations in America and not supporting minority groups. Richard Schechner points out the decisions President Trump has made to benefit himself:

> appointing Neil M. Gorsuch… to the Supreme Court to better reverse *Roe v. Wade*… appointing racist Jeff Sessions Attorney General to roll back voting rights… \[appointing\] Betsy De Vos as Secretary of Education who can’t wait to dole out money for charter schools, starving the public system. (7)

Through these actions, President Trump shows self-regarding cowardice because he is benefiting the majority, privileged population and not taking on these issues to help Americans be inclusive of minority populations. Balz claims that President Trump has “an us-vs.-them style of leadership.” In Balz’s quote, the “them” can be considered as minority groups, such as women, members of the LGBTQ community, and racial minorities, while the “us” can be considered white, heterosexual, cisgender males. While this style gains President Trump some support, it makes America seem unethical. President Trump is “leverage\[ing\] small investments for large personal and political gain” (Grusin S-92) and that is self-regarding cowardice. After breaking down President Trump’s self-regarding cowardice, one can relate these actions to Papa Ubu in Alfred Jarry’s *Ubu the King*.

Papa Ubu also has self-regarding cowardice by making laws that only benefit himself and killing other people to make himself more powerful. Timothy Youker, in his discussion of Papa Ubu, states, “He breaks and remakes all laws and rules to suit himself, from the laws of his king to the laws of physics and geography” (536). Papa Ubu also benefits himself when he “raids the treasury and executes all of the nation’s bankers” (Youker 550). Papa Ubu takes all of the money for his own selfish gain. He then increases taxes to get himself more money. When the citizens run out of money, he does not face the issue and fix the problem, he simply blames other people. Instead of claiming that there is an issue, he leaves the problem for others to solve. This solution has also been known to be used by President Trump; Park claims that President Trump likes to make “generalities, he thinks and acts in grand…terms…leaving details for others to sort out” (E-27). Papa Ubu and President Trump like to benefit themselves, and leave others to solve the issue, thus showing their self-regarding cowardice of actually solving issues.

Another aspect that Isaac Reed discussed in his article was that President Trump and Papa Ubu both have an imperious manner. One support for this manner in President Trump is through, what the *Trump Syllabus 2.0* calls, “’Trumpism: personal and political gain marred by intolerance, derived from wealth, and rooted in the history of segregation, sexism, and exploitation’” (Grusin S-87). President Trump feels entitled to the power the he possesses and his belief is supported because other people claim that Trump holds “a position that many think makes him the most powerful man in the world” (S-88). After becoming president, his imperious manner has only increased. He more believes that he has the ability to control others. Michael D’Antonio believes that President Trump “gets a thrill out of disturbing other people and proving that he doesn’t have to go along with what other people expect” (Schechner 9). Those around him only feed his authority because President Trump is “absorbed into a few who hyperadvise him, who saturate him with his own self-importance making him believe that what he says/does is his own self” (9). Those around him feed his ego, reifying his belief that he has the right to hold the authority that he has.

To further support that President Trump has an imperious manner, many scholars have studied President Trump’s personality traits. With these studies, scholars believe that President Trump’s personality is very narcissistic (Schechner 9; Park E-23, E-27). President Trump’s narcissism only helps support the that that he has an imperious manner, fully believing that he has the right to the power that he possesses. President Trump’s imperious manner is mirrored in *Ubu the King.*

Papa Ubu holds an imperious and this is seen by Mama Ubu feeding Papa Ubu’s authority and Papa Ubu creating his own title of “Master of Finance.” Papa Ubu feels as though he is entitled to have riches and power. This feeling of entitlement is fueled by his wife when she talks Papa Ubu up. She tells Papa Ubu that he deserves to be king when she tells him, “It’s that very ass I’d want to put on a throne. You could get infinitely rich, eat stuffed sausage all the time, and drive through the streets in a horse and carriage” (1.1.26-28). Mama Ubu helps Papa Ubu get into higher power by helping him with his schemes, if not giving him the full idea for the scheme, such as attempting to take the throne from the king. When attempting to fulfill Papa Ubu’s plot to be king, he orders around the military men he brings over for dinner because they are in his house. He treats them as though they should adhere to his authority because he believes he deserves it (1.3). Due to the fact that the men listen to Papa Ubu, it furthers his imperious manner because he is listened to. When other people help fulfill Papa Ubu’s expectation, it further increases Papa Ubu’s feeling of entitlement. The king himself event regards Papa Ubu as a “fine gentleman” (2.1.16). Papa Ubu knows how others view him and these ideas fuel his imperious manner.

Once he has taken the throne, Papa Ubu acts as though he fully deserved it. He starts to order people around as though he is better than them. He also starts to kill people to show his authority over others. By killing other people, it shows that Papa Ubu thinks he is better than others. The fact that Papa Ubu gives himself the title of Master of Finances also helps demonstrate that he thinks highly of himself. He thinks that he is obligated to all of the money that he has inherited. Papa Ubu calling himself the Master of Finances shows that he views himself in high regard and better than those who do not have the wealth that he has accumulated. These situations reflect Papa Ubu’s imperious manner, connecting him and President Trump. The two believe that they deserve the power they have been given and are treated by those around them to further increase their feeling of entitlement.

The final attribute discussed by Isaac Reed is the fact that both President Donald Trump and Papa Ubu have vulgar mediations on women. There are many instances where President Trump has been seen to have vulgarly treated women. Alicia Machado, 1996 Miss Universe, claims that Donald Trump “publicly shamed” her because she had “gained weight” (Barbaro and Twohey). Machado claims, “After that episode I was sick, anorexia and bulimia for five years” (Barbaro and Twohey). This example is only one of many women who have been victim to the current president’s vulgar view of women. There are many women who have accused Donald Trump of sexual harassment, as listed and supported in Meg Kelly’s article from *The Washington Post*. During President Trump’s campaign period alone, there were “at least 16 women \[who\] accused the president of sexually harassing \[them\]” (Estepa). These accusations have been regarding President Trump’s actions, but it is also important to look at what has been said by President Trump. Due to his imperious manner, President Trump was quoted in a transcript saying “I just start kissing them. It’s like a magnet. Just kiss. I don’t even wait. And when you’re a star, they let you do it. You can do anything… Grab ‘em by the pussy. You can do anything” (Bullock). Not only did he simply say these words, but he also acts on his words, as shown via a story from Temple Taggart, Miss Utah in 1997. She told *The New York Times* that “he introduced himself in an unusually intimate manner. He kissed me directly on the lips.” President Trump believes that his power gives him the authority to do what he wants to women, and this belief is sexist and unethical. Shelley Park, however, points out that “women closest to \[President Trump\] continue to defend and excuse behaviors that late night television hosts have characterized as ‘abusive,’ ‘repulsive,’ ‘repugnant,’ and ‘disgusting’” (E-28). This situation seems to directly correlate to Mama Ubu and Papa Ubu’s relationship. President Trump, as Isaac Reed suggests, does have vulgar mediations on women, supporting the comparison between him and the character Papa Ubu.

Alfred Jarry’s character Papa Ubu is shown to have vulgar mediations on women through his constant referral throughout the play to his “green candlestick” (1.1.12) and his interactions with his wife. During a class lecture, Dr. Raimondo Genna, said that Papa Ubu’s “green candlestick” is his penis. By Papa Ubu constantly referring to his penis consistently in the play, he shows little regard to those around him. He shows that a lot of his power seems to come from his penis and the fact that he has one. This idea belittles women because it gives the insinuation that Papa Ubu only has the ability to have his power is because he has a penis, which women do not. The fact that he also constantly refers to his penis also shows that he is vulgar around many people.

Throughout the play, Papa Ubu constantly insults his wife, showing that he has a particular standard for women. He said to her, “Mama Ubu, you are insulting me and you will soon get dumped in the lobster pot” (1.1.21-22). These words were said to Mama Ubu because she suggested that her husband should deserve to be king, which is not particularly insulting. Papa Ubu is saying that his wife, alone, is an insult to Papa Ubu. Later, Papa Ubu refers to his wife as “Madam my Female” (3.1.5) insinuating that he owns his wife, even though she is her own person. To further Papa Ubu’s vulgar mediation towards women, Papa Ubu, while talking to Mama Ubu in disguise, says that he is married “to the worst shrew in the world” (5.1.57). In the play, Mama Ubu does not show that she likes her husband, but she fully supports him and even helps give him ideas on how to become king and rule. Mama Ubu is a large part of Papa Ubu’s support system, but he does not treat her with respect. This relationship correlates to the women in Donald Trump’s life, as previously stated in Park’s article. Both of these men treat women vulgarly and with disrespect, verbally. They both believe that they have power over these women. However, the women closest to them in also seem to not act against the fact that the men treat them in this manner. Evidence, shown by scholars, media outlets, and the play, support Isaac Reed’s claim that “Pere Ubu is, indeed, like Trump in his contradictory assertions, combination of supreme verbal cruelty and self-regarding cowardice, imperious manner, and vulgar mediations on women.”

While there is a lot of indication that Papa Ubu and President Donald Trump are a lot alike, there are certainly consequences that can arise from making this claim. There are multiple times where the Ubu story has been used to portray political people in the past. Youker writes about two of these instances in “how the Ubu story has been used as a vehicle for depicting past and present political corruption in Central and Eastern Europe” (534). In these instances, the countries had overcome an oppressive government and there was a new rise in power. These past situations could then be applied to President Trump in the United States. In the U.S., however, the government has not been taken over by a new way of governing. If the comparison between Papa Ubu and President Trump is made, people may see this correlation and view poorly, possibly causing some sort of pushback.

Another consequence that may arise from making this correlation between President Trump and Papa Ubu is that the play is not real. While the location of the play is said to be Poland, there are many accounts that claim that the setting of the play is nowhere (Genna). If the setting for the Ubu story is nowhere, then comparing the story to Donald Trump would mean that the U.S. is nowhere. This idea can equate to the U.S. not being important, when that is not the case. The idea of the U.S. being nowhere can be seen as an insult to some people. The U.S. has a lot of power and is viewed as a highly developed country. In this comparison, it could be dangerous to view the U.S. as being nowhere because of the power that the U.S. possess. The play, *Ubu the King,* is can also be viewed as an absurdist play. Through this action, Youker says that the play has “nothing that might be called serious content” (544). If President Trump and Papa Ubu, are connected, then President Trump can be seen as not having to be taken as seriously. Along these lines, Schechner compares Trump to “an inflatable toy bumping into other inflatables in zero gravity. Except that the damage to others is real” (10). If people do not take President Trump seriously, as Papa Ubu is not taken seriously, there can be real consequences that can immerge. Therefore, comparing a fictional character to a real person can cause issues. One issue could be that those under him do not take him seriously. Schechner says that if that happens, “somewhere down the chain, officers refuse to carry out the rough beast’s order. Either they are arrested and warheads fly or the Commander in Chief is disobeyed” (8). Schechner’s example portrays what can happen when not taking President Trump seriously. It is important to follow orders those over you and there are bad consequences to those who disobey. If someone does not take President Trump seriously, it can put multiple people in danger. It is important to take President Trump seriously, but comparing him to Papa Ubu can cause people to not take Trump seriously. At the end of the play, Papa Ubu’s life stops; Donald Trump’s life, however, will continue to effect other people’s lives. Comparing Papa Ubu and Donald Trump can lead one to not see President Trump as serious, even though his actions effect those in the real world.

Isaac Reed draws a unique comparison between Papa Ubu and President Donald Trump. This paper discusses the ideas behind Isaac (“contradictory assertions…self-regarding cowardice, imperious manner, and vulgar mediations on women”) and the possible consequences that may present themselves by making this comparison. While this paper explores in more detail what Reed claims, there are more ideas in this paper that can be enhanced. These ideas only graze the surface of the comparisons between Papa Ubu and President Donald Trump. While there can be other consequences that may arise from comparing the two, looking at these two characters, side by side, can help understand both more fully, whether one wants to analyze the character of Papa Ubu or analyze President Trump.
