## Abstract

During the Early Modern England period, society did not have an advanced idea of medicine, so people were accustomed to the idea of the four humours, as proposed by Hippocrates, as a way of deciding what was wrong with a person. The four humours are sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, and melancholic. Each humour is appointed a bodily substance and organ that is causing the substance; the doctors of the time would then cure a patient based on such knowledge. This idea was not only applied to medicine, however; playwrights of the Early Modern England period also used these ideas to help explain and enhance character’s personalities. While audiences during the time period the play was performed understood the humours, modern audiences do not fully understand the idea. It is important for today’s directors to embrace this idea when performing the play because the idea of the four humours is no longer common knowledge. This means the director has to portray these characteristics to an audience that does not fully understand the concept. In John Webster’s play, “The Duchess of Malfi,” he uses the idea of the four humours to help the audience understand the personalities of the different characters and current directors and actors need to consider these ideas when performing the play for present audiences.

<p class="paper-title">Understanding the Humours: Analysis of <em>The Duchess of Malfi</em></p>

Imagine a young woman with three crying children at a flea market. She walks calmly down the street purchasing things as her children are yelling at each other, crying, and causing a scene. An onlooker in today’s society may think she needs to do something to take care of her children; however, during the neoclassical era an onlooker may think she is too calm, and because of this they think something wrong with the level of phlegm in her body. During the period of Early Modern England, society did not have an advanced idea of medicine and people were accustomed to the humoral theory, developed by Hippocrates, as a way of diagnosing a patient (Bockler 106). The word “’humoral’ derives from the word ‘humor,’ which, in this context, means ‘fluid’” (“Humoral Theory”). There are four humours and each is also associated with a specific organ, bodily fluid, season, and element (Bockler 106, “Humours”). Doctors of the time would draw their diagnosis “based on the person’s stage in the life cycle, normal temperament, sex, occupation, and other individualized factors” (Bynum 967). This idea was not only applied to medicine; playwrights of period also used these ideas to help explain their character’s personalities and actions. While audiences during the time period understood the humours, modern audiences do not fully understand the idea. It is important for today’s directors to embrace the humours when putting on a play from the Early Modern Period, because the idea of the four humours is no longer common knowledge. In John Webster’s play, *The Duchess of Malfi*, he uses Hippocrates’ four humours: sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, and melancholic, to help the audience understand the personalities of the different characters, and modern day directors need to comprehend these ideas and portray them to their audiences.

The most evident humour found in *The Duchess of Malfi* is melancholic and can be shown mainly through the character of the Cardinal. Earth is melancholic’s element, and this humour is prominent in autumn (“Humorism,” Larrimore). This humour is associated with the spleen thought to control black bile in the body (Paul 1399). The characteristics that come with melancholic are quiet, moody, anxious, and pessimistic (Hisek). With this knowledge, one can understand the Cardinal differently. Antonio says that the “foul melancholy/ Will poison all \[the Cardinal’s\] goodness…It then doth follow want of action/ breeds all black malcontents…he is a melancholy churchman” (1.1.76-77, 80-81, 1.1.162-63). With the knowledge of the humoral theory, one understands that Antonio is saying the Cardinal has too much black bile in his system. Because melancholy people are pessimistic, this causes him to act as though there is no positive outcomes in life. With this understanding, the Cardinal’s actions are reasonable. He is unhappy with his marriage, so he cheats on his wife with Julia (2.4). He also does not foresee a positive side to his sister, the Duchess, getting married, so he sabotages her life and has Bosola murder her (4.2.365). Act One happens during autumn, which is when melancholy is most prominent; this helps understand why the Cardinal does not speak as often as others and also why he is opposed to the Duchess marrying again (2.2.18-19). All of the schemes start at the beginning of the play, when the Cardinal is most melancholy. To give the impression that he is not diseased, the Cardinal must continue the scheme later on, even though the season may or may not be autumn.

Another distinguishing characteristic of a melancholy person is that they are “ideally equipped for recognizing \[a\] sinful creature’s need of salvation” (Larrimore 265). When applying this idea to the Cardinal’s actions, it explains why he did not like his sister having a child out of wedlock (2.5). He continually saw the faults and sins of his sister even though he was sinful himself by cheating on his wife. Roy Baumeister and Brad Bushman write that “people underestimate their faults” and do not take into account their own sin when putting sin onto others (98). This is the reason for his disregard for his own sin of adultery. The Cardinal saw Julia as being sick, and that he thinks he saved her from disease. The evidence for this is shown when he tells her, “I have taken you off your melancholy perch/… and let you fly” (2.4.28-30). The Cardinal believes he saved her from feeling melancholy, even more so shielding his eyes from his own sin. By the Cardinal seeing the sin in others, he is shown to have a problem being melancholic.

Due to the fact that melancholy usually has the most effect on people during autumn, one can understand the Cardinal’s affair with Julia. Julia tells the Cardinal, “do you suspect/ I, that have been a secret to your heart/ these many winters” (5.2.253-55). If the Cardinal is melancholy during the autumn, he calls on Julia towards the end of the season to help with his feelings. His melancholy makes him recognizes his failures in his life and he no longer can handle these feelings. Wanting to rid himself of these feelings is why he feels the need to seek out Julia for assistance towards the end of autumn and into winter. Overall, the Cardinal is sick with melancholy, and this is shown through his actions and through the description he is given by other characters.

The next humour evident in *The Duchess of Malfi* is choleric as shown through the characters Julia and Ferdinand. Choleric personality types are excitable, impulsive, aggressive, and restless (Hisek). The element associated with choleric is fire and it is represented by the liver and yellow bile (Bockler 106). It also affects people most in the summer (Larrimore). With this information, Julia’s choleric qualities are understood when she tells the Cardinal “You told me of a piteous wound i’th’heart,/ and a sick liver, when you wooed me first” (2.4.37-38). The sick liver would mean she has too much yellow bile in her body, which causes her sexual excitement when she sees the Cardinal and why she has the impulse to cheat on her husband.

Choleric can be seen in Ferdinand when he curses, “Rhubarb, oh for rhubarb/ to purge this choler” (2.5.13-14). Rhubarb is “used as a purgative” during this time period and Ferdinand understands that he is choleric and wishes to be rid of the high levels of yellow bile in his system; however, his words show that he has had no luck doing so (Gainor 1025). The fact that he has not freed himself from his choleric impulses is shown later in that same scene when he says, “we must not now use balsamum, but fire,/… to purge infected blood – such blood as hers” (2.5.25-27). Because Ferdinand decides to use fire, the element of a choleric personality, it shows that he has not purged this temperament. Ferdinand’s choleric humour can explain many of his impulsive and aggressive actions. Both of these traits are shown when he decides to have his sister killed (4.1). Ferdinand wants what money and land the Duchess owns, and has her killed so he and the Cardinal can possess it. Ferdinand is also seen as aggressive when he finds out his sister is having, what he believes is, an illegitimate child, and he bursts out in fury saying, “a sister/ damned! She’s loose i’th’hilts” (2.5.3-4). He is aggressive with his feelings towards her and easily angered instead of wanting to help. These traits and actions show how Ferdinand can be described as choleric.

Another humour shown in *The Duchess of Malfi* is sanguine as shown through the character Bosola. The sanguine humor is associated with spring, air, blood, and the heart (Larrimore 262, Bynum 967, Bockler 106). People with a sanguine temperament like to “suppress human nature” and have a “love of sensual pleasure” (Larrimore 267). Sanguine people are thought to be carefree, talkative, and outgoing leaders (Hisek). Bosola does not believe the humours are true and does not believe physiognomy (‘the attempt to determine temperaments and character on the basis of facial features”) to be true either (“Physiognomy”). This is seen when Ferdinand tells Bosola that the Cardinal was set off by Bosola because of “some oblique character in \[his\] face” and that “made \[the Cardinal\] suspect \[Bosola\]” (1.1.241-42). Bosola responds to this by saying, “there’s no more credit to be given to th’face/ than to a sick man’s urine, which some call/ the physician’s whore because she cozens him./ He did suspect me wrongfully” (1.1.243-46). With this Bosola says that the Cardinal is wrong with his belief that Bosola is not meant to have the job. He may deny that there is truth to physiognomy, but this does not mean he cannot be sanguine.

This idea of Bosola denying physiognomy is enhanced later in the play when he tells the Cardinal that he does not need assistance with the murder of Antonio. He says, “Physicians that apply horse/ leeches to any rank swelling use to cut off their tails,/ that the blood may run through them faster. Let me/ have no trains when I go shed blood” (5.2.337-40). Leeches were a means of getting rid of blood in the body when people were thought to have too much. People believed this would help people with sanguine problems by evening out their blood level. By Bosola denying that he needs leeches, he is denying the fact that he has too much blood in his system and enforcing the idea that he does not believe in physiognomy.

Even though Bosola may not believe in physiognomy towards the beginning of the play, he dies seeing some truth in it. In Act Five Malateste calls Bosola a “wretched thing of blood” because of all the blood he has shed throughout the play (5.5.110). Malateste is saying that Bosola has too much blood in his body, and, because sanguine is associated with blood, Bosola is told that he is sanguine. Malateste is saying that Bosola does not care about the blood he has shed, and that he is too carefree; he should care more about what he has done. After being told this, Bosola says that people “are only like dead walls or vaulted graves/ that, ruined, yields no echo… Oh, this gloomy world!” (5.5.115-18). He is realizing all that he has done, and saying that people should care more about things in life and not be so carefree. This is him admitting two things: there is truth to physiognomy, and he has done wrong things in his life.

The final humour displayed in *The Duchess of Malfi* is phlegmatic which is seen in Ferdinand, and is shown by characters talking about fate. The humour phlegmatic is represented by the brain and associated with phlegm in the body (Paul 1399). Its element is water, and its season is winter (“Humours”). Mark Larrimore claims that “phlegmatic represents the balance which corresponds to ‘patient’… love” (Larrimore 267). Ferdinand believes that he has overcome his temperament of being sanguine and that he has become more phlegmatic. The evidence for this is found when he tells Pescara, “I am studying the art of patience…the patient’st man i’th’world/ match me for an experiment” (5.2.46, 50-51). He believes that he is patient and no longer choleric. This helps him believe that his choices are better than those previously made in the play. Towards the end of the play Ferdinand believes that he has turned into a werewolf. This helps understand why Ferdinand has a change in humours. By believing he is diseased, he can believe he has also changed humours. He believes he is now diseased in a phlegmatic way and this causes him to believe he can have patience.

An example of phlegmatic can be seen as the characters talk about fate. Because phlegmatic’s season is winter, Antonio is cursing fate when he says, “’Tis ev’n like \[fate\] that in winter night/ takes a long slumber o’er a dying fire/ as loath to part from’t, yet parts thence as cold/ as when he first sat down” (3.2.207-10). This talks about how long fate takes to come to its conclusion. Antonio is wishing the end would happen and that fate would not be so patient. Phlegmatic people are also known for being passive and careful (Hisek). While Antonio is cursing fate for being patient, he also knows that it is careful because it is phlegmatic, but he also is wishing fate would not be as passive as the phlegmatic humour. Other people, such as Ferdinand and the Cardinal, take advantage of fate too much, even though fate still holds strong in the end.

The Duchess also claims that fate is phlegmatic when she says, “and those three smiling season of the year/ into a Russian winter, nay, the world to its first chaos” (4.1.99-101). She is explaining that winter is taking over the rest of the year. Fate still has control the entire year, not only in winter. Because people are doubting and cursing fate for what has happened, the Duchess is saying that fate will turn the whole year into winter. One can draw the conclusion that fate is phlegmatic because of the way the characters talk to and about fate in the play.

In conclusion, John Webster’s play, *The Duchess of Malfi,* utilizes Hippocrates’ humoral theory through the characters displayed throughout the piece. Melancholy is shown in the Cardinal as he was described by other characters and through his actions. Choleric is seen through Julia and Ferdinand’s words and by Ferdinand’s actions. Sanguine is displayed by Bosola’s actions and beliefs in physiognomy. Finally, phlegmatic is presented in the play by Ferdinand’s change in personality and through the discussion of fate. These four humours are important when discussing characterization between directors and actors. It may change the way actors portray their characters. Understanding the theory also opens a door to new ideas and motives a character might have. The humoral theory was important to early modern audiences, and that is why the playwright utilized the theory. It is significant for modern audiences to understand the humours as well. When analyzing a play or its characters it is important to understand many aspects of what is happening during the time the play is written, because there may be hidden meanings that enhance the story.
