<p class="paper-title">Inside the Struggles</p>

Everyone struggles; rich or poor, hero or villain, African American or white, perfect or broken. Every person you see either has gone through a large struggle or is currently going through one. Most struggles are caused by external forces: family, friends, teachers, peers, or society. Although everyone struggles, everyone also copes with these struggles in different ways. “Richard Cory,” written by Edwin Arlington Robinson, and “If We Must Die,” written by Claude McKay are two poems that present examples of different struggles. These two poems have similarities and differences found through both their structure and message.

Through the structure of both poems, there are similarities to be seen through both their rhyme scheme and narrative point of view. In both poems, the rhyme scheme uses rhyming with the last word of every other line, abab, then switches after four lines, cdcd. The first and third lines in “Richard Cory” end in “town” and “crown” and the second and fourth lines end in, “him” and “slim” (Robinson 1-4). Also, in “If We Must Die,” the first and third lines are “hogs” and “dogs” and the second and fourth lines are “spot” and “lot” (McKay 1-4). Along with their similar rhyme scheme, both poems are written in first person talking about a group of people using the pronoun “we.” Robinson’s poem reads, “We people on the pavement looked at him” (Robinson 2) while McKay’s poem reads, “If we must die, O let us nobly die” (McKay 5). Other than these two comparisons, the other aspects of the poems’ structure contrast.

The contrasts found in the poems’ structures are shown by the type of narrative structure, the use of enjambment, and type of figurative language. While “If We Must Die” is a narrative sonnet that uses enjambment, “Richard Cory” is a narrative ballad without enjambment. “If We Must Die” is a sonnet because it has fourteen lines and uses abab rhyme scheme. It also uses enjambment because the lines run into the next line. “So that our precious blood may not be shed/ In vain; then even the monsters we defy/ Shall be constrained to honor us though dead” (McKay 8). “Richard Cory” is a ballad because each stanza contains four lines and is narrated using a direct style, first person.

<pre class="verse-quote">So on we worked, and waited for the light,
And went without the meat, and cursed the bread;
And Richard Cory, one calm summer night,
Went home and put a bullet through his head. (Robinson 13-15)</pre>

Each line ends with a comma, semi colon, or period bringing a proper ending to the line. This non-enjambment and ballad narrative structure contrast McKay’s enjambment and narrative sonnet structure.

The two poems also contrast in structure by the use of figurative language. “If We Must Die” uses imagery and is an extended metaphor. This poem uses imagery when it animalizes people calling them “dogs” and “monsters” (McKay 3,7). The poem also symbolizes the African Americans’ struggle of segregation in America. It represents them being called to defend themselves from the white people attacking their civil rights using segregation. “Richard Cory” does not use as much literary term examples. The example of figurative language is an alliteration found in line thirteen when it says, “we worked, and waited” (Robinson 13). This smaller use of figurative language contrasts the extensive figurative language used in McKay’s poem, but does not make it any less tactful. Along with these structural contrasts, there are also contrasts found within the poems’ story.

A contrast found in the poems’ story is the way the reader interprets the poem. While “If We Must Die” is a call to action, “Richard Cory” is seen as a parable. Even though McKay’s poem is asking people to defend themselves, the action is to defend, not to attack. However, Robinson is trying to teach his audience a lesson regarding people who seem to have their lives put together. He wants his readers to see how much society pressures people to be perfect by telling the story of a man who committed suicide due to pressure. By using a story to portray his meaning the poem can be seen as a parable.

Another contrast found in the poems’ story is the idea of which side of the battle the narrator can be found. “If We Must Die” is seen through the eyes of those being attacked while “Richard Cory” is seen through the eyes of the enemy. In McKay’s poem, he states, “If we must die” giving the idea that they only die if they absolutely have to, but are reluctant to do so (1, 5). The poem also says, “Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back” (14). Only those who are being attacked would be able to say they can fight back. However, in Robinson’s poem, the narrator is the enemy. The poem portrays people discussing how amazing the man Richard Cory is. In the words of the narrators, “we thought that he was everything/ To make us wish that we were in his place” (11-12). Little to the narrators’ knowledge, they had put enough pressure on Richard to drive him to “put a bullet through his head” (16). The narrator in this poem is society putting pressure on people needing to be “perfect.” However, being perfect is not legitimately obtainable. If someone seen as perfect messes up, they can never seem to live it down. Instead of dealing with the pressure of having to be perfect, Richard Cory takes himself out of the equation. So, the narrator in Robinson’s poem is the enemy while the narrator in McKay’s poem is the victim. Although there are contrasts seen through the poems’ narrators, there also are similarities found within the poems’ story.

The comparisons found in both McKay and Robinson’s poems are through the fact they both have one side seen as more superior to the other and both portray internal struggles. The two poems have one side that seems to be superior to the others: the African Americans in “If We Must Die” and Richard Cory in “Richard Cory.” In McKay’s poem, the African Americans, or the narrator, are seen to be superior to their enemy. This idea is shown through the narrator animalizing the enemy, calling them “dogs” “monsters” and a “cowardly pack” (3, 7, 13). By animalizing the enemy, it makes the narrator and his group seem superior to them because humans are superior to animals. Another way they are superior is by the way they are defending and not attacking. They say they are “Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back” (14). One can only fight back if they are being attacked. This demonstrates that they tried not to fight, but they have no room to retreat and they are not going to simply cower and give up. Mature adults try to talk things over instead of fight about things. The fact that the African Americas tried not to fight indicates they are more civil than the others than to sink to the level of animals because they do not fight unless it is absolutely necessary. In Robinson’s poem, Richard Cory is perceived as superior to the rest by the way everyone describes and talks about him. The “people on the pavement” (2) say “he was rich –yes richer than a king –“ (9) and “\[they\] thought that he was everything/ To make \[them\] wish that \[they\] were in his place” (11-12). The people “went without the meat, and cursed the bread,” meaning they were unable to purchase nutritious food to eat and despised the food they had to always eat, emphasizing their lower class status (Robinson 14). Because they were poor and Richard Cory was rich, this gives the image that Richard Cory was superior to the narrators like the narrators were superior to their enemies in “If We Must Die.”

Coinciding with the idea that both poems show a superior force, both poems have someone suffering from an internal struggles. One poem shows the African Americans deciding whether or not to fight and the other showing Richard Cory’s struggle to be perfect. Both are internal struggles caused by the same outward force, society. In “If We Must Die,” the white society is fighting to keep the African Americans segregated because they believe they are too different to be considered as members of the ‘normal’ society. In “Richard Cory,” Cory is fighting to be the perfect person society perceives him to be; however, no one is perfect and the pressure of being perfect finally pushes him over the edge. Richard Cory feels as though he is “Pressed to the wall, dying” so he kills himself (McKay 14). So, both poems are portraying an internal struggle caused by external powers, but the two poems have one final contrast, their resolution.

The resolution of each poem is the biggest contrast found between them. While one poem ends with men coming together to fight for their rights, the other ends with a man giving up his struggle and taking himself out of the picture. McKay’s poem ends triumphantly with, “Like men we’ll face the murderous, cowardly pack,/ Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back” (13-14). The good people all unite to fight against the evil together as one. This is a heroic ending of people struggling, but fighting for their rights. On the other hand, Robinson’s poem ends with Richard Cory putting himself out of his pressured misery by, “\[going\] home and \[putting\] a bullet through his head” (16). Instead of fighting back at society and showing them his faults, he decides to give up his fight and eliminate himself from the equation of life. So, although both poems show men struggling, they both end with different messages: one triumphant and one disheartening.

Overall, both poems, “Richard Cory” written by Edwin Arlington Robinson and “If We Must Die” written by Claude McKay, have many similarities and differences seen through their structures and stories. The similarities can be seen through rhyme scheme, narrators’ point of view, the idea of a superior side, and internal struggles. The differences are shown by figure of speech, narrative style, message, and resolution. Although both poems have many differences, they both are similar because they are two poems written to portray internal struggles, but just in different ways.
