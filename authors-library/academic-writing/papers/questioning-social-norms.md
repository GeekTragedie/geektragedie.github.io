<p class="paper-title">Questioning Social Norms</p>

William Shakespeare wrote, “Lord, we know what we are, but know not what we may be” (*Hamlet* 4.5.43). Society has difficulty accepting new ideas, even though they may change. Shakespeare understands this, but people still struggle with this idea today. Humanism consists of the idea that all things in nature should be questioned: social norms, scientific conclusions, even the Bible. The emerging Humanist ideas created tension with the present medieval thinking. This idea is shown through the use of meta-theatre in Shakespeare’s *Twelfth Night* and Calderon’s *Life is a Dream* by causing the audience to question different ideas hinted at in the plays.

Shakespeare uses meta-theatre by the use of identity confusion. This was shown in *Twelfth Night*, though the character, Viola. She disguises herself as a man in order to be accepted as a servant for Duke Orsino (1.2). Viola is performing the role of Cesario. This is meta-theatre because the audience is watching a play where the actor is performing the role of Viola who is then performing the role of Cesario. With the use of dramatic irony, the audience knows that Cesario is Viola, which makes the meta-theatrical idea even more profound. Calderon used this idea towards the beginning of his play by Rosaura dress like a boy. This, however, was not for the entire play; this is only for the beginning scene. Rosaura needed to dress like a man to safely travel from Russia to Poland, but the disguise is only for reality purposes. She does fool Segismund and Calderon into believing she is a boy, but later, when they realize she is a woman, the characters do not react entirely different about the situation.

While both audiences are viewing disguises, both plays are perceived differently. In Spain women are allowed to perform on stage; however, they are not allowed to wear pants. So, while Rosaura is supposed to be dressed like a boy, the actor must remain in a skirt because woman are not allowed to wear pants. The audience is watching a girl that is disguised as a boy, but still wearing a skirt. This makes the more obvious to the audience that Rosaura is still a woman. This idea connects with the residual medieval thinking because, even though a woman is dressing like a man, she must still wear a skirt.

In England, however, woman are not allowed to perform on stage; all of the actors are male. In *Twelfth Night* the audience watches a boy actor play Viola, a woman, who dresses disguises herself as Cesario, a boy. So, the audience watches a boy play the role of a boy for the whole play. This idea is not a huge culture shock to audience of the time because they watching this is normal for them. Shakespeare hints towards a different idea during the content of the play. There is an unspoken romance between Viola’s brother, Sebastian, and his travel companion, Antonio. Through Antonio’s language, it seems as though he is in love with Sebastian. Antonio says, “I have many enemies in Orsino’s court/… but come what may, I do adore thee so/ That danger shall seem sport, and I will go” (2.2.41, 43-44). Antonio decides to travel with Sebastian, even though he could be in danger. He decides to do so because he is fond of Sebastian. The relationship only grows between the two throughout the play, but is never shown to become a full romantic relationship. Antonio and Sebastian’s relationship emphasizes the homosexual idea brought up in *Twelfth Night*.

The idea of homosexuality is enhanced by the use of meta-theatre at the end of *Twelfth Night*. The play ends with Sebastian marrying Olivia and Orsino promising to marry Viola. However, throughout the play, Orsino only knew Viola as Cesario. This left no room for a romantic relationship to grow between Orsino and Viola. Orsino takes the news of Cesario being a woman may too easily and decides to marry her. At the end of the play, the actor playing Viola does not even go change into woman’s clothing, so the audience is left with the image of Orsino marrying Cesario. Orsino, played by a male actor, marrying Cesario, played by a boy actor. While the play does not upset the patriarchy, the ideas of homosexuality are hinted in the play. Because humanism is evolving during this period, the final image leaves the audience to question the right and wrong of homosexuality. The play gives the image of it being acceptable, but society’s ways at the time makes it unacceptable.

While Shakespeare mainly uses gender confusion as meta-theatre, Calderon uses identity confusion. In *Life is a Dream*, there is one character that can be viewed as performing a role and that is Segismund. Because his horoscope said he was going to overthrow his father’s reign, his father locked him in a tower for his life. Segismund grew up thinking of himself as a monster. He is confused by his identity or who he is supposed to be. When released from the tower and brought to his father’s palace, Segismund performs the role he thought he was given; the role of the monster. He then proceeded to murder a servant, threaten to kill Clostaldo, and prove to his father that he is not set to be king (2.3-10). Later in the play, Segismund believes that his time in the palace was only a dream (2.17). He sets out to live a better life; striving to prove that he is not the monster everyone believes he is. He succeeds, only to show the audience that when he was acting like a monster, he was truly acting. It was not in his nature to be harmful.

Segismund rejecting the role he has been given goes against the horoscope his father had given him. This leads audience members to question whether or not horoscopes are always correct. Horoscopes are a residual belief from the medieval era, so this causes tension between humanist thinking and the medieval thinking. The play is questioning whether horoscopes are real. Humanism ideas are related to people questioning ideas that are stated as facts. For the audience watching the play, they start to consider what other old beliefs they should question. If horoscopes and fate are not always true, as Segismund portrays, than what other old beliefs could be incorrect? This not only causes audience members to question old beliefs, but also society’s stereotypes and the great chain of being. If Segismund rejects playing the role given to him by society, then other people can do the same.

Overall, both plays use meta-theatre to question different ideas in society. The questioning of society’s values shows the emerging humanism side, Shakespeare questions homosexuality while Calderon questions stereotypes. Through this acknowledgement, the plays also shine light on their residing, medieval thought process and whether or not to break the trend. This causes a tension in society on whether they should question their current standing or if they should continue along the same path they are in. It is fascinating how the ideas of homosexuality and stereotypes still struggle in today’s society, over 200 years later. It seems to be a reoccurring problem that seems as though it will never be put to rest.
