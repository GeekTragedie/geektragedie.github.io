<p class="paper-title"><strong>This was Improvised: How I will Incorporate Improvisation in Therapy</strong></p>

While in graduate school to become a clinical mental health counselor, I find it important to revisit course concepts and growth for future integration as a counselor. Having years of improvisation experience excited me to take the Improvisation course as part of my drama therapy (DT) curriculum. In my future work as a drama therapist, I see myself primarily using improvisation techniques. This course aided in my ability to learn how to incorporate improvisation into my future work as a counselor. Over the course of my improvisation class, I have become fascinated by DT group work which aided me in recognizing my personal strengths and areas in need of growth.

## Therapy Concepts

Reflecting on my experiences in the Improvisation course, I am intrigued by various concepts discussed, specifically Ormont’s (1992) discussions about resistance and group therapy benefits and the DT core processes of life/drama connection, dramatic projection, and embodiment (Antioch University Seattle [AUS], 2019; Jones, 2007).

### Group Therapy Concepts

My view of resistance has been formed in this class through the writings of Ormont (1992) and Emunah (1985). Ormont (1992) writes that resistance is important for group therapy and finding the resistance is central to a therapist’s work (p. 138). When therapists are able to notice and bring clients’ attention to resistance, they can “resolve lifelong problems not amenable to any other approach” (Ormont, 1992, p. 125). I mentally combined this idea with Emunah’s (1985) article about DT and adolescent resistance. Emunah (1985) demonstrates ways therapists can incorporate resistance into theatre games (pp. 72-73), which can highlight resistances for adolescents and possibly help clients work through their resistances. I currently view resistance on a four-quadrant coordinate plane: the x-axis being low to high resistance and the y-axis being covert to overt. I am excited and intrigued by the challenge of creatively incorporating clients’ resistance to help them grow from these experiences.

I am more confident in my choice to work with groups based on Ormont’s (1992) discussion of group therapy benefits (p. 29). Ormont (1992) states that groups in therapy, “elicit self-destructive behavior… enable the members to see how others respond to them… affords patients diverse views of their behavior… affords the opportunity for on-the-spot self-definition… [and] afford the chance to practice new behavior” (p. 29). These five items are less likely to happen outside of a group setting (Ormont, 1992). Before graduate school, I envisioned myself doing DT in a group setting, so my decision was only secured after reading the benefits that only come from doing group work and enjoying myself as a facilitator.

### Drama Therapy Concepts

I find excitement and conflict when considering the DT core processes of life/drama connection, embodiment, and dramatic projection. Life/drama connections, as described by AUS (2019), is when clients connect their dramatic process with their life outside of the therapy room (p. 5). Coming from a theatre background, I appreciate this connection because I grew up being able to express myself through improvisation and playwrighting. I appreciate this idea being brought into therapy sessions so others can benefit similarly. With this excitement, I believe it is important for me to be mindful that my clients can connect the two aspects. Jones (2007) warns that if clients do not make a connection between life and drama, they risk creating a separate DT world, which is counter-therapeutic because the changes and growth will only take place in the therapy sessions (p. 118). It is important for me to know my clients lives enough to make this differentiation and prevent therapy from plateauing.

I am cautious when considering using embodiment and dramatic projection, as I appreciate both forms, but in my ability to know when they would be best utilized because of my own countertransference. One of the beauties of DT is a client’s ability to embody their experience. Embodiment allows clients and therapists the ability to “physically express and encounter material” (AUS, 2019, p. 2). There is a spiritual nature when connecting an experience in the body, and a client can explore new aspects of themselves through embodiment. Dramatic projection happens when clients “project aspects of themselves or their experience into” the theatrical realm to externalize their problem (AUS, 2019, p. 3). A new point of view can be taken when an issue is externalized through projection. I have personal difficulty combining these two ideas; I personally find it difficult to externalize issues because they are embedded physically inside of me. I have tried to externalize them using narrative therapy with my counselor with no success. I worry that my countertransference may prevent me from using this useful technique with clients. I am conflicted in how embodiment and projection can come together. One feels under distancing while the other feels over distancing, which can help a client safely work through their issues, but how do you project and embody something at the same time? I find it important to return to the body, so after a projection, how can I help a client embody that projection?

## Self-Reflection

### Strengths

Among many of my strengths, I feel two DT aspects have improved over the course of this quarter, playspace and distancing. Building off of my education of the playspace last quarter, I have been able to apply my knowledge of the playspace to a new realm of understanding. AUS (2019) describes the playspace as a “mutual agreement between clients and therapists in a play process that what is happening between them is both real and pretend, an objective face and a subjective fantasy” (p. 1). I have applied this knowledge into the playspace of a group when doing improvisational activities. A playspace can occur between any group of people, no matter what they are doing. I witnessed a new playspace grow in my improvisation class through various activities that pushed boundaries and stretched individuals’ levels of comfort. I am more confident in my ability to create a playspace and notice the different guidelines needed for safety.

I view distancing as another one of my strengths as a future drama therapist. Healthy distancing is a continuum used to help a client remain engaged in an exercise without being flooded by emotions or no longer benefiting from the activity (AUS, 2019, p. 4). I feel strongly about my ability to distance myself from material and noticing if a client may be overwhelmed or underwhelmed. Johnson (n.d.) describes the different types of roles therapists take on to facilitate a drama therapy session. I am drawn to the leader role, which Johnson (n.d.) describes as leading the groups in activities but taking on roles within the group as needed, though the client’s decisions are the focus of the work (p. 115). I feel most comfortable in this role because I can witness while also interact with individuals as needed. I enjoy participating, so I feel I would not enjoy the therapy sessions as much if I were not participating in them. I also believe I would be overwhelmed with information if I was always participating in the activities.

### Areas of Growth

While it is important for me to highlight my strengths, it is just as important for me to take note of areas that still need growth, which are my active witnessing and play skills. I feel comforted by taking on the leader role because I am worried about what I may miss if I do not witness others in the playspace. Witnessing is being an audience member to yourself and/or someone else during a therapy session (Jones, 2007, p. 101). While being a leader in a group, I may be participating in times of need, like I did during my student facilitation. My participation resulted in not witnessing interactions or scenes. I would like to grow in my comfort and ability to witness multiple people at the same time. Jones’ (2007) words bring comfort to me when he points out that witnessing can take any form, from short terms to long terms of time (p. 102). He also says that clients benefit from other members of a group witnessing them (Jones, 2007, p. 101). It is comforting to know that I can witness for any period of time and it will still be beneficial. It is also good to know that other members’ witnessing roles are just as important as my own. As a therapist of a group, I will not be able to witness everyone at all times, so others in the group witnessing is important to keep in mind.

Along with growing my ability to witness, I also would like to improve my ability to play. In relationship to the playspace, play is the way one can creatively and flexibly interact with other people; it is a state of mind where time and space can be altered (Jones, 2007, p. 88). I am concerned about being able to engage people adolescent aged or older in play because I have not been able to practice this skill outside of the classroom environment. I hope to widen my horizons of interactions and evoke play in other relationships in my life but that is still not playing with people that I do not yet know. Maybe other classes will allow me to play with people who are not used to playing in their every-day life.

## Conclusion

I always find it crucial to write a reflection paper at the end of a quarter to revisit my abilities and note the areas where I have grown. It synthesizes what I learned over the course of the quarter and what I want to take with me as a future therapist. There are specific therapy concepts that will continue with me after this quarter, specifically Ormont (1992) and Emunah’s (1985) ideas regarding resistance, Ormont’s (1992) benefits of group therapy, and DT core processes of life/drama connection, embodiment, and dramatic projection (AUS, 2019; Jones, 2007). After learning these concepts, I realized continued strengths of the playspace and distancing, but also realized areas I can grow my abilities, active witnessing and engaging others in play. I hope to continue my learning throughout my DT career and realize the importance of revisiting personal connections, strengths, and areas of growth to remain engaged as a counselor.
