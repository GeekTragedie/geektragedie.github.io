<p class="paper-title">Murders and Manhood</p>

“If we should ever be asked as a species to bring forward one artist who has most fully expressed the human condition, we could with confidence elect Shakespeare to speak for us” (Greenblatt 1). Stephen Greenblatt expresses many people’s love of Shakespeare with this quote. With Shakespeare’s success, it is interesting to note that his play *Macbeth* is well-known for its poor performance history. Brett Gamboa convincingly argues the play’s failure is due to the script, not the producers of the play. One of Gamboa’s arguments connects to the script “depriving Macbeth of his death scene” (37). Macbeth is only shown killing once in the play, and is deprived of killing others throughout the play. Jerold Ramsey states another trend in *Macbeth* discord: “One of the organizing themes… is the theme of manliness” (285). Even Macbeth’s wife dares to ask Macbeth, “Are you a man” (3.4.70). The *Oxford English Dictionary* defines “manly” as, “courageous, strong, independent in spirit, frank, upright…having those qualities…associated with fighting men” (“manly, *adj.*2a.”). Ramsey continues discussing the difference between manliness and humaneness in *Macbeth.* There is a connection between the claims made by Gamboa and Ramsey’s that can be discovered. Exploring the ideas of manhood in Shakespeare’s *Macbeth* through the murders helps support the idea that Macbeth does not fit the definition of manly to an audience.

The first series of deaths in *Macbeth* are King Duncan and his guards being murdered by Macbeth (2.2). While this murder could be viewed as strong or could be “associated with fighting men,” there are two reasons as to why this is not true (“manly, *adj.*2a.”). The first way these murders are not seen as manly is because the audience does not see them happen on stage. Though Lady Macbeth tells the audience Macbeth is doing the committing the murders, the audience does not see this happening. In the introduction to “Violence, Politics, and Gender in Early Modern England,” Carole Levin and Joseph Ward state that “men were far more likely than women to engage in violent crimes in early modern England” (4). Audiences understand this and relate violence as being associated with manliness. Brett Gamboa argues that “depriving Macbeth of his death scene” is one area where *Macbeth* falls short of connecting with its audience (37). The audience cannot relate the murders directly to Macbeth because they cannot see Macbeth commit the murders. The audience needs to see Macbeth commit the violent crime. When Macbeth comes on stage to tell his wife the king is dead, the daggers are what the audience sees first. As Tomoko Wakasa says in their master thesis *Swords in Early Modern English Plays*:

> The daggers and blood visible to the eyes firstly let the audience know the fact that he \[Macbeth\] has truly ‘done the deed’ (2.2.14)… By smearing the real daggers with Duncan’s blood, he gives the physical form of the vision of the dagger not ‘sensible / To feeling’ (2.1.6-7). (110)

The audience learns first from the daggers than from Macbeth of the deaths. The daggers give the vision of the violence, not Macbeth. The audience makes more of a connection to the daggers and violence than to Macbeth. The daggers have done the deed as much as Macbeth has, which takes away the idea of manliness from Macbeth.

Macbeth’s reaction to the murders also does not portray manliness. Macbeth enters the stage with the daggers and the first lines spoken are “Who’s there? What ho?” (2.2.8). These lines convey that Macbeth fears being caught for the deeds he has done. Instead of being strong and confident, he is seen as scared. Macbeth then asks if he was too loud when he murdered the king and guards then asks who is in the other room (2.2.14, 21). Macbeth’s fear quickly turns to paranoia. Lady Macbeth tells her husband to “Consider it not so deeply” (2.2.33). Lady Macbeth must calm down her husband and then tell him to return the daggers to the bodies or else they will be caught (2.2.51). Through Lady Macbeth controlling the situation, she is being stronger than her husband, fitting the definition of manly more than Macbeth. By not showing the murders being committed, but showing Macbeth panicked by the act he committed and his wife taking over to ensure they do not get caught, the play and Lady Macbeth deprive Macbeth of being viewed by the audience as manly. While manly is associated with fighting and Macbeth commits three murders in this scene, through his reaction and his wife’s actions, these murders do not portray Macbeth as manly.

Macbeth is seen by the audience committing one murder himself in the play and this is when he kills Young Siward. While this action may be viewed as manly, it does not connect to an audience as much as one may believe. Young Siward enters in 5.4 with Malcolm, Macduff and Siward, but is never referred to, nor does he say any lines. The audience has no connection to Young Siward, so Macbeth killing this character in 5.7 does not move the audience to perceiving this action as manly. The exchange between the characters is also very short and Young Siward is not certain who Macbeth is (5.2.5). Young Siward attacks and is killed by Macbeth. Macbeth killing a character that he does not have a connection to does not show that he is strong or courageous. This death also appears so late in the play, that the audience has already understood Macbeth’s character before this action happens. With the lack of connection between Young Siward and the audience and Young Siward and Macbeth, this short exchange murder does not change the perspective of an audience to believing Macbeth is manly.

Macbeth is the cause of other murders in the play, however, he does not physically commit these murders himself. In 3.1, Macbeth orders three murderers to kill Banquo and Fleance (3.1.114-25). While Macbeth claims the murderers need to do this deed, “Masking the business from the common eye / For sundry weighty reasons” (3.1.123-4), it is not courageous for a man to not kill for himself. By not killing Banquo and Fleance himself, Macbeth does not hold up the idea that he is a “fighting man” (“manly, *adj.*2a.”). When the murderers go to kill Banquo and Fleance, they successfully kill Banquo, though this murder is not seen on stage (3.3.22). The stage directions read “*\[The Murderers attack. First Murderer strikes out the light\]*” (3.3) and after the murder has happened, the third murderer asks, “Who did strike out the light?” (3.3.22). By the murder not being visible, it makes the death less impactful to the audience. Brett Gamboa discusses the previous productions of *Macbeth* saying that many people blame the productions themselves as failures to the text (32). Gamboa compellingly argues that it is not “practitioners fail\[ing\] the play” but instead says “how the play may be failing them” (32). Throughout the article Gamboa shows how audiences cannot connect to Macbeth. The scene of the murderers killing Banquo is set up as though the audience will see the killings, but because the light is taken away right before the act is committed, the scene builds-up for the audience into seeing nothing. This scene does not meet audience’s expectations, making the death more of a disappointment. *Macbeth* disappointing the audience in this scene aligns with Gamboa’s argument of the play “failing” its’ audience. By not meeting this expectation, the disappointment then falls upon Macbeth, the one who ordered this action to happen in the first place, and adds to the audience’s views of him as a man. Macbeth does not fit the definition of “upright” by failing to meet the audience’s expectations (“manly, *adj.*2a.”). While the murderers successfully kill Banquo, they fail to kill Fleance (3.4.16-7). Due to the murderers failing to kill Fleance, Macbeth also fails to kill him, showing him as weak and failing to be manly. Macbeth could have gone to kill Banquo and Fleance himself, but hired others that failed to do it. From Macbeth’s failure to have Fleance killed and the death of Banquo not being shown on stage, the audience does not physically see anything manly from Macbeth.

Another set of murders Macbeth orders others to commit are those of Macduff’s family. While already stated, Macbeth not doing the killing himself is unmanly; however, Macbeth ordering the death of women and children itself is also not manly. In 4.2, Lady Macduff’s death is not seen on stage and is not committed by Macbeth physically (4.2.82). This murder makes Macbeth seem even less manly because it combines lack of shown violence, as seen with King Duncan, with the murders not being committed by Macbeth, as seen with Banquo. While this murder pulls double duty against Macbeth’s manliness, Lady Macduff’s son’s murder provides new evidence towards Macbeth not being manly.

The first murder shown on stage happens late in the play and is the murder of the Macduff’s son in 4.4. The scene reads:

<pre class="verse-quote">A MURDERER. <em>[stabbing him]</em>        What, you egg!
Young fry of treachery!
SON.                 He has killed me, mother,
Run away, I pray you!
      <em>[He dies.]</em>
            <em>Exit [WIFE] crying ‘Murder!’ [followed by</em>
                        <em>MURDERERS with Son’s body].</em></pre>

Due to the scene where the audience anticipates Banquo’s murder, but is then deprived of it, this sets up the expectation for the murder of the Macduff family. The audience does not expect to see the murders on stage, however the murder of the son, a young child, is seen, breaking this expectation. With the lack of shown violence the whole show, the audience seeing this murder has a larger impact, especially adding in the fact that the victim is a young child. Children are viewed as innocent beings, and the fact that Macbeth causes the death of innocence is not courageous or strong. The death of a child also can be viewed differently from Shakespeare’s period. Laura Spence’s dissertation entitled *Women Who Murder in Early Modern England, 1558-1700* states that a “statute passed in 1624, entitled ‘An Act to Prevent the Destroying and Murthering of Bastard Children’… implies that the mothers murdered or hid the deaths of their child straight after birth” (25). The fact that the government had to create a regulation regarding this subject means that these deaths were an issue in this period. Spence continues on to say there were more “recorded cases of infanticide” than “cases of child murder... However… there were many more pamphlets published throughout this period concerning \[child murder\]” (25) specifically against mothers. Through publicizing women killing their children, the murder of children is viewed as a womanly act. By Macbeth ordering the murder of a young child, this murder is viewed as womanly because the public views this issue of child murder relating to women. The Macduff’s son’s death is, overall, not seen as manly as Macbeth does not commit the murder himself and the deed itself is viewed by the public as womanly. This death has a large amount of impact on the audience because it is the first murder shown on stage and it is so late in the play that it comes unexpectedly. The murder victim is also a child, which chiefly impacts audience as well. Through the impact of a murder that is viewed by the public as womanly, ultimately the audience does not view Macbeth as manly.

Through the analysis of the deaths in *Macbeth,* one can see how the murders play a certain role in an audience perceiving Macbeth as fitting the definition of manly. Macbeth directly causes most of the deaths in the play, not including his own or Lady Macbeth’s. “\[D\]epriving Macbeth of his death scene” (Gamboa 37) disconnects Macbeth from the murders and helps the audience contribute the murders to the daggers, the object first scene by the audience to help them know the deed has been done. This lack of shown violence disconnects the audience to the idea that Macbeth is manly. The only murder physically seen committed by Macbeth is in 5.4 when Macbeth kills Young Siward, of which the audience has no real connection to. By the scene being short and there being a lack of relationship between Macbeth and the audience to Young Siward, this murder does not show Macbeth as being courageous. Finally, the first murder be shown on stage, caused by Macbeth, is Macduff’s son. The murder of children, in Early Modern England, is connected to women and thus showing Macbeth as the opposite of manly. In *Macbeth,* the theme commonly discussed of manliness can be viewed differently by looking at the murders committed. The way in which people die in Shakespeare’s plays can help enhance the understanding of the overall theme and should be considered when analyzing a play.
