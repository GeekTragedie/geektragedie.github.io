To develop my skills as a counselor, I took the course Counseling Theories and Practice: Part II, which focused primarily on more recently developed theoretical orientations of counseling. Through this course, I developed more skills as a counselor by exploring and practicing various theoretical orientations. While exploring theories, I considered my own beliefs of wellness and suffering to find orientations that best fit my worldview. To me, wellness includes authenticity to the self by acknowledging the feelings in the here-and-now. After this course, I hope to continue my education about these theories in my future work as a counselor and integrate these theories into my future practice. The purpose of this paper is to synthesize how I have developed through this course and how I plan to incorporate the course information into my work as a counselor with real clients.

## Development as a Counselor

Through this course, I have found and developed many skills that I hope to hone more as a future counselor. There are three crucial skills that I want to integrate more into my future practice as a counselor: higher session engagement, mindfulness, and social justice approach.

### Session Engagement

In previous quarters, I found myself not engaging or talking in sessions as often as I did this quarter.  I am unsure as to why this occurred but have found more of my own voice this quarter. My engagement may have increased because I developed more of a rapport with my client through continuing work from the previous quarter. Having more rapport with my client allowed me to be more comfortable using interventions and offering new perspectives to situations. I have grown more confident in my common factor skills and was able to incorporate these more easily into my role as the counselor. By becoming comfortable with common factors of counseling, I have been able to differentiate nervousness about being a counselor from discomfort with a theoretical orientation. It was easier for me have higher engagement than previous practice sessions because I found orientations and interventions that I was comfortable facilitating, specifically mindfulness-based approaches.

### Mindfulness

Mindfulness is a tool I plan to integrate into my work with future clients. According to Kabat-Zinn (2003), the founder of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, “An operational working definition of mindfulness is: the awareness that emerges through paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally to the unfolding of experience moment by moment” (p. 145). Mindfulness became my go-to intervention when I did not know what to do in a session. This orientation felt right in my body as a counselor and was beneficial to my client during multiple sessions. I found mindfulness to aid clients’ transition into a therapy setting to focus on themselves; the United States can be fast-paced, and mindfulness helps bring clients into the present. I am a future drama therapist, and in drama therapy sessions, heightened emotions are likely to be expressed in a session. I can offer mindfulness to my future clients to aid their transition from heightened emotions in session back into their everyday, fast-paced life. As a counselor, I also can benefit from using mindfulness at the beginning and ending of my sessions to remain present and note possible countertransference.

### Social Justice

I consider the social justice orientation as crucial to my future work as a counselor. The course had one class period devoted to exploring the social justice orientation. According to Griffin (2007 as cited in Bertelsen, 2019c) social justice therapy “focuses on understanding social power dynamics that result in some social groups having privilege, status, access while other groups are disadvantaged, oppressed, denied access” (p. 2). While I already knew this counseling element was important to me, I deeply appreciated the orientation’s approach to how a counselor brings the clients’ identities into session. Before this course, I had not yet found the language that was most comfortable to address identities in sessions, so having a demonstration and being shown various ways to approach client identities was imperative to my ability to integrate this theory into my practice. I may want to explore specific client identities that are less important to my client, which may hinder the client’s therapeutic process. By increasing my use of the social justice orientation, I hope to decrease countertransference that may come from my interest in a client’s identity. I need to be mindful of my privilege as a counselor that I am in a position of power, and the client may not feel comfortable addressing concerns regarding my approach. I need to have space for the client to be comfortable addressing issues they may have with me and being open to criticism. I am curious about how I can create a space where my client feels comfortable sharing their concerns about my approach. Would it be as simple as asking a client how they feel the counseling process is going?

One element from the social justice orientation class that I am taking with me is how obvious my place of privilege is to the outside world. The “Social Justice Counseling Exploration” handout invites students to reflect on “how obvious is this identity to someone who only sees you or does not know you well?” (Bertelsen, 2019b, p. 3). Although I hold a lot of target identities, I need to be mindful that they are not visually noticeable, and my clients are not going to know. My clients may project their systemic frustration onto me as I am in a position of power and present as a less targeted population. I will need to be mindful of my self-disclosure for this fact – am I disclosing out of discomfort or am I disclosing to increase the therapeutic relationship? Do I want to remain a clean slate for the client to project on or do I want to present as myself?

Finally, I feel the need to note my sexual orientation dilemma because this quarter I found out I am asexual. Nothing like finally figuring this out when at 25 and married, right? This identity will consistently show up while I work with clients struggling with sexual experiences in session. With my asexuality, I have difficulty understanding the importance of sex in others’ lives because the act has no meaningful connection for me. I need to ensure I allow the space to talk about sex, because I know it is important for other people to talk about, and it is a topic that many do not feel comfortable sharing about. I also need to be careful if my client is getting too close to me as a counselor, because I constantly miss social cues regarding sexual advances and have other people point them out to me later. In a counseling setting, I do not have someone watching interactions to note this danger to me. I need to continually use supervision to ensure healthy boundaries are maintained in my counseling space.

## Theoretical Orientation

In this course, I found the Gestalt theory resonated with me and the Gestalt therapy approach felt natural to practice in my pseudo-session. There are no words to express my excitement in finding a theoretical orientation that felt natural to my drama therapy mindset. When reading Gestalt theory in the textbook, I very much resonated with the core groundwork of the theory that originated in Ancient Greece: “the whole is different or greater than the sum of its parts” (Sommers-Flanagan & Sommers-Flanagan, 2012, p. 195). I have always connected with the Socratic method of questioning “why,” which I connect to understanding someone as a whole. I must have a deep connection to ancient Greece due to my love of theatre and connection to ancient Greek philosophy. It is important to me to learn a person’s background so I can create an overarching idea of who the person is, because I do not believe one part of a person’s identity fully represents the whole person. With my drama therapy background, I resonated with bringing feelings into the here-and-now. Garrett (2007) writes that the first stage of Gestalt sessions is to have the client bring issues they are having into the session; instead of reflecting on a past experience, the client’s feelings in the moment are discussed during the session for exploration. I know others disagree, which could be a whole paper on its own, but I believe feeling in the moment is crucial to healing wounds and learning about the self.

Working through Bertelsen’s (2019a) “Gestalt Techniques: Self-Awareness Self-Assessment” really helped me solidify my feelings towards Gestalt Therapy. The Gestalt approach has techniques I would be very comfortable facilitating (here and now, role reversals, rehearsals, and playing the projection). There are Gestalt techniques that I actually have already facilitated in non-therapy theatre settings (empty chair, hot seat, making the rounds, and exaggeration). While filling out the questionnaire as a client, I asked myself the difference between being uncomfortable and being challenged. I appreciate that the approach has techniques that would challenge me as a client through being a little uncomfortable. When using Gestalt therapy as a counselor, I will need to ensure my clients understand the therapeutic nature of these techniques.

Gestalt theory fit me well in my pseudo-sessions as both a client and a counselor. As a client, my counselor gave me the opportunity to think differently about a conflict I was facing. Gestalt theory brings clients into their immediate awareness with how they are feeling in the moment in the session (Sommers-Flanagan & Sommers-Flanagan, 2012). This approach was helpful to me as a client because I tend to neglect how I feel in the moment. By exploring feelings in the moment and externalizing the feelings, I was able to create a touchstone moment that I can return to in my daily life when necessary to remind myself how it feels to be anxious, calm, and productive at the same time.

As a counselor, I had the privilege to facilitate Gestalt interventions that largely benefited my client. Gestalt’s here-and-now approach came natural to me combined with my core mindfulness nature. I invited my client to personify a piece of themselves and talk to it. The client responded well to this exercise and was able to then reverse their role to gain another perspective of themselves. Together, we integrated the different parts of themselves together to create a whole.

### Future Development

Realizing Gestalt theory resonates with me a lot, I need to research how to best use this theory with diverse client populations. As stated earlier, social justice is crucial to me, and I need to make my Gestalt orientation approachable to all of my clients, even those who may not feel completely comfortable with the interventions. Sommers-Flanagan and Sommers-Flanagan (2012) have a nice call-to-action to Gestalt therapists to increase the research available when working with clients from diverse backgrounds: “many clients from diverse cultural backgrounds are reluctant to engage in emotionally focused, experiential therapy. The emotional focus can be inconsistent with collectivist cultural values and with cultural rules about emotional expression” (p. 218). Gestalt therapy can come off as abrasive, pushy and sometimes unethical, given the discussions of classmates’ feelings towards this approach. Given these diverse perspectives of the approach, a high number of clients may not be comfortable doing Gestalt therapy. I want to maintain cultural humility through my work by increasing my knowledge regarding Gestalt therapy with diverse populations.

With my base of mindfulness, I also want to increase my knowledge on how Gestalt therapy incorporates mindfulness practices. I would not be surprised to find research integrating the two orientations because both disciplines focus on the here-and-now. Due to mindfulness-based practices being rooted in Zen-Buddhist teachings, I wonder if collectivist cultures that are predominantly Buddhist would be more open to my integrated mindfulness-based, drama therapy, Gestalt therapy approach.

## Conclusion

Over the course of the quarter, I have gained insight into how I view my role as a counselor. My theories course allowed me to explore engaging with my client more in sessions. I learned that mindfulness and social justice aspects are very important to me. After multiple theory courses, I finally found that my theoretical orientation will be Gestalt therapy, and I was privileged to practice this in sessions as a client and therapist. Now that I have found my orientation, I now need to educate myself on how to apply this theory with culturally diverse populations and how to integrate it with my mindfulness, drama therapy self.
