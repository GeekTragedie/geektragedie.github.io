<p class="paper-title"><strong>My Root Influences</strong></p>

While learning to become a counselor, I learned the importance of understanding myself and where I have come from to best be present and serve my future clients. A lot of my world understanding has been molded by modeled behaviors of others or my life experiences. I use Blake’s (2009) adaptation of Hays’s (1996) ADDRESSING model to view traits I hold as an agent (Christian, white, heterosexual, and US born) or a target (trauma experienced, gifted, female from a rural area) group. As a future clinician, I want to remember, dissect, and understand how these important aspects of myself influence my perspectives on suffering, wellness, and helpfulness. In this paper, I will support my current definitions of struggle, health, and helping to aid me in my future career as a counselor.

## Suffering

My view of suffering comes from my experience with trauma. My brother [was struggling] before his death from suicide. My experience was different than some others’, because [we had our last days together with my brother] before we [learned] he would not make it and we surrounded him with love as he passed. I understood suffering differently after witnessing my brother struggle, [our last days together] and the funeral, and watching others’ grieving processes over the years. My trauma experience of watching my brother pass made me view personal struggles in a new light, realizing that no one can share the exact same experience.

My definition of suffering is: Experiencing an individual or series of event(s), interaction(s), or emotion(s) in life that expands beyond an individual’s current, normal level of challenge, understanding, or comprehension. After my brother passed away, I realized that everyone’s level of struggle cannot be compared. What is difficult for one person will not translate to another. [Each member of my family has reacted in a different way.] All of us experienced the same event and have struggled in different ways. I believe I have dealt with my brother’s death in a healthy way and no longer struggle with the loss, but that cannot be said for the rest of my nuclear family. All people are different, which means what they classify as a struggle may not be a struggle to someone else.

I also learned that the way people deal with struggle or suffering is unique to every individual. An indication of struggle can be physical manifestations, such as shortness of breath, crying, or shaking, but a person’s struggle can go unnoticed if physical characteristics are absent. Someone close to a person may notice behaviors or interactions not being consistent with that person’s norm. However, the best way to know when someone is experiencing hardship is to ask and interpret the response.

Aside from my brother’s death, suffering and pain have been crucial to my development in life as I have been met with many challenges. Without these experiences in my life, I do not believe I would have entered counseling or attempted to understand myself to the degree I do now. I would be less confident, less empathetic, and probably less happy. I know it is corny; it is important looking at the silver lining and positive outcomes from hardship. It helps with connecting to others, coping with situations, learning about the self, and creating a positive outlook. I had many silver linings after my brother passed: my family connected deeper with my partner; my brother is not suffering; I am closer to my mother; my divorced parents were in the same room together and hugged; I learned how to cope with [my own struggles]; I found my self-confidence. While I miss my brother dearly every day, I know he would not want me suffering. My life would be miserable if I focus on only my pain, and some people in my family let the grief take over them. I watch them go through the motions of life day after day with no joy or hope, and I am sorry for them that they have not decided to take action yet. Joy and hope are two aspects that I feel are important in life for wellness.

## Health and Wellness

Growing up, my view of health was derived from my age (youth), ethnicity (Euro-American), sexual orientation (heterosexual), and gender (cisgender female). My mother modeled and verbalized her and society’s expectations of what health should look like; she ran every day, went to bed early, woke up early, commented on her healthy eating, and took me to the dentist and doctor for checkups regularly. She also always plated my food, instructed me when to work out, and commented on my clothes and size. After doing counseling work in college, I realized her behavior and messages were not healthy and had to reevaluate my perspective on health and wellness.

My current definition of wellness is understanding personal needs and morals and discovering a purpose of the self. While physical health is important in life, I believe thriving and wellness are less about physicality and more about mental stability. Can a person find joy? A deep sense of happiness, not superficial. Can a person find hope? A reason to continue. Does a person feel of value? A strong self-confidence. Personal needs on the mental side can be monitored by changing the physical actions, so physicality plays a role in wellness, but it is not possible to know if someone else is healthy based on their physical being.

## Helpfulness

Many of my moral standings come from my white, heterosexual, rural South Dakota, Christian background, which, specifically in the Pacific Northwest, can be a scary statement to make. Observing Midwestern hospitality and friendliness, though it can be faked at times, has shaped the way I view helping others. I knew most of the families in my hometown and if someone was going through a hard time, everyone knew and was there to support in any way possible. From experience, the aid can be overwhelming, but ultimately the family feels supported and gracious to the community for their boundless support.

I define helpfulness as doing an action or saying something that can benefit another, and that action or verbiage is taken by the other party in a positive way. It is not the intent of a deed that classifies an action as helpful, it is the receiver that makes the distinction. Someone may offer advice or do an action specifically to benefit someone else, but it may not be helpful, such as telling someone to leave their abusive partner or buying a sandwich for a person experiencing poverty. These acts are not considered helpful unless the other party agrees that it is. From another perspective, someone may be talking or doing their job and help someone without knowing. The intent of the original action may not have been to help, but the result was helpful because the person received it in a positive way; for example, sharing a story from a vacation or losing a dollar bill. It is the receiving of actions that deem them helpful, not the intent. Someone can go through life hoping to help others, but they will never know unless they are told they are aiding. So, if something is helpful to you, it may be worthwhile to the other person if you tell them their action, intentional or not, was beneficial.

## Conclusion

Approaching my course regarding counseling theories and practices, I hope to find a technique that best supports my views on suffering, wellness, and helpfulness. Struggling and wellness can only be classified by the individual having the experience, while helpfulness is decided by the person receiving an action or spoken word. I want to revisit this helpful analysis later in my academic career to reevaluate my beliefs on these terms, because as people go through life, their own view of words changes.
