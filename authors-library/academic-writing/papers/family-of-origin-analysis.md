## Family of Origin Analysis

While the training to become a therapist is challenging, it is important for students in this field to understand where they have come from and how their past has influenced them.  Kerr (2008) promotes counseling students to understand their family of origin (FoO) so the students have “awareness of emotional blind spots… a greater understanding of potential biases” (p. 107) and can decrease their risk of countertransference (p. 109).  By exploring my FoO and the interactions therein, I hope to be more equipped to becoming an influential counselor.  This paper analyzes my FoO with certain events and specific interactions that may have influenced my family members while I was seventeen years and younger.  By analyzing my family’s structure, relationships, key life events, and multigenerational patterns, I will have more freedom to notice my family’s actions and have the choice to continue these patterns in my own life.

## Family Structure

### Maternal Family

My maternal grandparents were high school sweethearts who graduated from the same small-town high school and were married young.  The two of them had three children, all daughters, in this order: my mother and then my two aunts.  When the girls were young, the family moved overseas for my grandfather’s work.  The family lived abroad for several years, in more than one country, before returning to their hometown.

When going back to school in their hometown, my mother met my father, who was a year older.  They dated until my father graduated and went to college at which point, they decided to separate for a year. Soon after resuming their relationship, my mother became pregnant. Pressured by my father’s family, they decided to get married to avoid having a child out of wedlock.  After the wedding, my parents moved to a college town. My mother continued her education degree and my father started his medical training, and soon after, they gave birth to me.  The couple stayed together until my dad decided to take a job in another city without consulting my mom who did not want to move.  This situation was the tipping point in the unhappy marriage, and my mother decided to file for divorce where my mom received primary custody of me.

Two years after divorcing my father, my mom met my stepdad; they got married when I was five years old.  When my stepdad joined my family, we became a blended family.  Blended family is defined by Nichols (2008) as “separate families united by marriage; stepfamilies” (p. 489).  As my stepdad’s family joined mine, I became more acquainted with his FoO. My stepdad’s parents had five children, and he was the second.  Due to health issues, my step-grandfather later passed away.  Though these members were added to the family and I spent holidays with them, I never truly became close to anyone in the family.  Soon after my mom and my stepdad got married, my mom gave birth to my half-brother, Thomas; about a year later Daniel followed.

Returning to my maternal grandparents’ children, my aunt, the middle child, met her husband during high school.  They were married before moving out of state, where they both attended college.  After finishing school, they returned home to settle down and work before giving birth to their three sons.  These three boys are the youngest on my mother’s side of the family.

My other aunt, the youngest child, was the last of her sisters to get married.  She married a man with three children of his own, adding them to the family.  The two of them added two more children to their family.

### Paternal Family

My paternal grandparents met while they were in college and got married soon after.  Together, they had two children: my aunt and my father.  No one in my father’s family moved outside of their hometown until the children graduated high school.  In high school is where my father met my mother. They were wed, had me, and then got divorced.  My father was devastated when my mother filed for divorce and more torn when my mother won primary custody of me.  This agreement means he was able to see me one weekend a month, half of the summer, and on various holidays. My dad dated women for many years, but never settled down, leaving more time for he and I to grow close.  While he thought he found the right woman, they moved in together and got engaged, the relationship did not work out.  Thus, my dad and I were alone again.

My dad eventually found the love of his life, my stepmom, after he went back to school to advance his medical degree.  While my stepmom also became pregnant out of wedlock, my father’s family did not pressure her to marry into the family; she married in voluntarily.  My stepmom and my father were married when I was in high school, and this marriage also added my stepmom’s family of origin into my family as well, creating my other blended family.  My stepmom brought into the family her parents and her siblings and their families.  Shortly after the marriage, she gave birth to my other half-brother.  I was lucky enough to accept the offer of being the godmother to my young brother.

My father’s sister, my aunt, found her husband quickly after graduating high school.  They married, and years later they had their first daughter.  After many more years of waiting, the couple were blessed with fraternal twins.  The next year, the family had my youngest cousin, the final addition to this family.

## Family Relationships

### The Divorce

The divorce between my parents created a lot of conflict in my FoO.  My dad’s side of the family teamed up against my mom; hurtful words were said in the attempt to make my mom stay.  My father’s family has always appeared pleasant and caring towards others, so it shocked me to hear they were so cruel towards my mother.  My family’s actions can be understood by applying McGoldrick’s (1995) explanation of how families react to change (p. 86).  McGoldrick states “there is no life without change and no change without disruption… family members may try to cling to ‘the way things were’” (p. 86).  Complementary to McGoldrick’s discussion of change, Nichols describes family homeostasis: “families as units that resist change” (p. 28).  My dad’s family felt as though my mom was ruining the family homeostasis; they did not want change to happen, so they tried to prevent the change.  Their way to cope was to shame my mom for causing the family homeostasis to become unbalanced.

### Paternal Family

After my parents’ divorce, I had a close relationship with my dad.  Whenever I visited him, it was usually the two of us spending time together.  When my dad was promoted at work, he had more responsibilities and less time off.  My dad called upon my grandma, his mother, to help take care of me when he had to work.  My grandma became my maternal figure when I was visiting my dad.  I spent a lot of time growing up with her and viewed her almost as a second mother.

### Maternal Family

**My mother’s nuclear family.**  My mother and her sisters all got along together well.  Bowen’s System Theory (as cited in Nichols, 2008) discusses sibling rivalry as children fighting with each other as a result of trying to gain more appreciation and attention from their parents (p. 130).  Sibling rivalry did not happen between my mom and her sisters and the reason for this can be explained using Sulloway’s (1997) theory on family niches.  He states, “siblings are motivated to exploit unoccupied niches because they stand to gain something in the process: greater parental investment” (p. 95).  My mother and her sisters all found their niches in the family: my mother, sports; my middle aunt, visual arts; and my youngest aunt, writing.  Hoshino (2018) explains that centripetal families “focus their energy inwards” towards each other (J. Hoshino, personal communication, October 24, 2018).  My mother’s family was centripetal as they spent many years in different countries where other people did not speak English.  The sisters were only able to communicate with each other, so they became close.  There were also close relationships between the daughters and their parents.  However, this parental relationship pattern did not apply to my mother and me.

**My maternal family.**  The conflict between my mother and me was not always present; during my childhood, I was close to my mother.  We would write bedtime notes back and forth, go for walks, and read books.  This relationship continued though the transition of adding my stepdad to the family system.  Without a definitive reason, conflict eventually arose between my mother and me, and I can use the theory of family niches and sibling order to speculate the cause.

Two theories that apply to my conflict with my mom are McGoldrick’s (1995) discussion of sibling order and Sulloway’s (1997) discussion of family niches. McGoldrick explains many only children feel “very close attachments to their parents” (p. 224).  As I was an only child for six years, I was accustomed to getting her attention.  I was an only child on my dad’s side for seventeen years; so, I was accustomed to receiving all his attention.  It was not until I was 11 and my brother Thomas started school that I noticed my mom giving more of her attention to his education and less attention to me.  Sulloway (1997) states siblings “report greater parental discrimination than parents report” (p. 88).  Bowen (as cited in Nichols, 2008) states that “each child… becomes highly sensitive to the amount of attention he or she receives in relationship to siblings” (p. 130).  The combination of Sulloway and Bowen’s theories can help me better understand the conflict that arose from my mother giving more attention to my brother.  I may have felt as though my mother was showing preference towards Thomas and less preference towards me. I then let out my anxiety from this conflict on my mother.

Bowen’s System Theory (as cited in Nichols, 2008) also discusses sibling rivalries, (p. 130) but the rivalry part of the theory does not work when applied to my siblings and I due to my personal role in the family.  Bowen relates the lack of attention from the mother to result “in fighting and resentment” between siblings (p. 130).  This described reaction did not happen in my family and can be explained by applying a theory created by Napier.  Napier believes parental children “take on major household and childrearing responsibilities” (p. 2).  I felt very motherly towards my brothers, so I did not take out my anxiety by creating conflict with my brother, as Bowen’s theory predicts; instead, I discussed my frustration with my mother to my brother in an unhealthy way.

When I decreased my anxiety regarding my conflict with my mother by discussing it with my brother, I created an emotional triangle.  Bowen’s triangulation theory (as cited in Kerr, 2008) can be explained “when two people are in conflict, one person attempts to draw in a third person to stabilize the system” (p. 98).  I had a conflict with my mother, and I used Thomas as a third-party member to verbalize my conflict.  I tried to get my brother on to understand my side of the conflict and get him to feel the same way I felt.

## Key Life Events

### Before Birth

As previously discussed, one series of critical events in my maternal family was my mother’s family moving overseas, from one country to another, and then back home.  These moves caused the family to be centripetal as there were not many people outside of the family that were able to speak English and the family did not know other languages well.  The movement of the family has impact today as my mother’s side of the family is still centripetal.  Weekly contact between multiple family members through phone calls and text messages is normal.  The whole side of my mother’s family also gathers for multiple holidays and birthdays throughout the year.

### Early Childhood

During my parents’ divorce, my paternal grandma and Dad created a triangle with my mother.  As stated earlier, triangulation happens when two people are in conflict and one member of the party brings in a third-party member to help decrease their anxiety about the situation (Bowen as cited in Nichols, 2008, pp. 127-128).  To decrease my dad’s anxiety caused by the conflict with my mom, he allied with my grandma for support.  My grandma agreed with my dad, blaming my mom for their conflict in their relationship.  My dad’s side of the family then discontinued contact with anyone on my mom’s side.  Hoshino (2018) defines emotional cutoff as “the process of separation… or denying importance of parental family” (p. 2).  My dad did not deal with the emotions of the divorce in a healthy way but instead separated himself from the situation.  Due to not dealing with these emotions, my dad was not able to have a healthy, steady romantic relationship for twelve years.

### Childhood

I remember one unhealthy relationship my dad had when I was a child which had a major impact on my personality.  My dad started dating a woman who had two children, one my age and one two years younger.  My dad and his girlfriend got engaged when I was around 10 years old, and she and her kids moved in with my dad.  While my dad was working, he would leave me with her and her kids.  Unknown to my father, she was struggling, causing her to stay in bed most of the day.  I took care of the house and her children.  As stated earlier, Napier (as cited in David, 2018a) shares that children who take on “household and childrearing responsibilities” are considered “parental kids” (p. 2).  Napier continues to say that parental children “often work much too hard for their own good… have trouble setting limits on how much they are responsible for” who are “at risk for being exploited by others” (p. 2).  My dad’s relationship with his fiancée impacted my personality, causing me to have many traits listed above.

Another critical event in my childhood was my mother marrying my step-dad.  I had to learn how to balance a new family system, as we became a blended family.  I also then had to become accustomed to not being an only child when my brother was born.  As a parental child, I was very motherly towards my brother; I never wanted to put him down and always wanted to spend as much time with him as possible.  I was close to both of my brothers, finding pride and joy by helping teach them about the world.  As my brothers grew older and I started my adolescent years, we started to find our places in the family.

### Adolescence

**Nuclear family sibling niches.**  During my adolescent years, my brothers started to show more interest in specified activities, finding their proper niches in the nuclear family.  Sulloway (1997) shows that “among siblings, dramatic differences are common place… because siblings cultivate distinct niches within the family” (p. 93).  My siblings and I have different interests: me, theatre; Thomas, soccer; and Daniel, technology.  My brothers and I had different niches in our family, so our parents did not compare us to each other, and we excelled in our given field, not needing to fight for recognition.

My siblings and I also support sibling birth order theories.  McGoldrick (1995) describes the characteristics of the oldest child as “overresponsible… self-critical” but unable to “handle criticism from others well,” and “leads others” (p. 211-212).  McGoldrick also states that oldest daughters “may be saddled with the responsibilities of the oldest child without the privileges or enhanced self-esteem” as it is appeared to be their duty as a female (p. 214).  As the oldest daughter, I identify with all the characteristics McGoldrick lists.  It also supports me identifying as a parental child.  My brother Thomas and I have had conversation regarding his status as a middle child.  McGoldrick explains that middle children “run the risk of getting lost in the family,” are “the best negotiators,” “can stimulate competitive feelings,” (p. 221) and “feel somewhat lost and unappreciated” (p. 222).  Thomas has expressed feeling these characteristics as a middle child.  While I have not had a conversation with Daniel regarding his thoughts as the youngest sibling, I noticed two characteristics that emerged. McGoldrick states the youngest child “has more a sense of ‘pairing’” (p. 214).  I know Daniel felt very close to Thomas and viewed him as a best friend.  McGoldrick also describes the youngest as making “remarkable creative leaps leading to inventions and innovations” (p. 214).  While Daniel has not yet invented or innovated, he is very interested in computer science and innovative technology.

## Multigenerational Patterns

### Responding to Stress

One multigenerational pattern that can be seen on both sides of my family is how members respond to stress.  McGoldrick (1995) states “characteristic ways of coping with stress are usually learned… there is a tendency to fall into certain types of discommunication” (p. 86).  In my family, not talking about certain topics to decrease stress has been learned by multiple members.  My family avoids politics, feelings, and moral topics as to not cause disruption in how people behave or feel.

An example of the lack of discussion is with my paternal grandma, who dislikes the people married into the family: my step-mom and my uncle.  While she has not directly stated she does not like them, she has told me how she feels my step-mom has stolen my dad from the family and that my uncle is lazy and should get a job to support his family.  My grandma’s opinions have not been discussed verbally, but they are rather apparent, and members of the family know about them.  As McGoldrick (1995) rightfully states, “families live in a ‘cold war’ – no battles, just chronic tension” (p. 105).  My family feels my grandma’s opinions during holidays, but no one talks about it; they all sit there with the tension in the air.

### Gender Roles

Another pattern that can be seen on both sides of my family are gender roles.  My family members have fit into the traditional gender roles of their culture’s society.  The women are viewed as the ones who take care of the household chores and cooking, while the men make money and do yard work.  This trend can be seen with my grandparents, my parents, my aunts, and blended families.  While this tradition may appear to be normal for families, McGoldrick (1995) discusses that following these traditions of society also run in the family: “culture has often prescribed… appreciation of the men;” (p. 174) “women have been valued mainly for their caretaking roles” (p. 190).  My family has followed the rules of society and hold the expectation that women are to be caretakers.  With society’s views of women changing, my generation may take a different stance on gender roles to better follow the change in societal expectations.

### Family Niches

One final pattern shown on both my father’s and my mother’s sides has been discussed throughout this paper: lack of sibling rivalry.  My grandparents have told me of their fond relationships with their siblings and how, growing up, they had gotten along.  My maternal grandma still helps her brother today when he is struggling, and my paternal grandpa often goes fishing with his brother.  My parents are close to their siblings and call each other for parenting advice.  My cousins, as of right now, all get along and play games together, considering their siblings their best friends.  Sulloway (1997) describes siblings needing to find their specialization in order to achieve equal attention from their parents (p. 96), and he states “siblings… go out of their way to be different” (p. 95).  When looking at the interests of my family members in comparison to their siblings, this theory holds true.  All the siblings in my family have different interests setting them apart.  My paternal grandma loves teaching while her sister enjoys engineering; Mom enjoys sports while one of my aunts prefers art and the other, reading; I like theatre, Thomas plays soccer, and Daniel goes to computer science camp.  My family has a way of finding the members finding their interests in relation to their siblings to prevent conflict.

## Conclusion

There are many encounters in my life in which my FoO may have impacted me.  It is important for me to understand my FoO when hoping to become a therapist, so I can be a beneficial counselor.  It is important to map out family structure, understand family relationships, analyze key life events, and track multigenerational patterns in the family, so it can be up to my discretion whether I choose to continue the patterns passed on through the generations of my family.
