# Tamagawa 2015 Lesson Plans: A Summary

**Degree / Program:** BFA in Theatre  
**Course / Context:** Tamagawa University summer English and culture program, Dakota State University, with colleagues  
**Date:** August 3–14, 2015  

---

<p class="piece-note">A summary of the lesson plans written with fellow instructors for a two-week visit by students from Tamagawa University in Japan. The full plans also held long background readings copied from reference sources, worksheets, the students’ names, and the partner university’s feedback on an earlier year’s program. Those are left out. This page describes how the plans were built and what they covered.</p>

## The Program

Twelve Tamagawa students spent two weeks in Madison, South Dakota, living with host families and taking English classes at Dakota State University (DSU). The program began with placement testing (Michigan and Accuplacer) and ended with post-testing and a “Japan Night” that the students hosted for the community. Most days had a morning of English study tied to a “Topic of the Day,” then an afternoon field trip or activity that let the students use what they had just learned.

Each plan gives a time and length for every activity and lists the copies and materials needed. Instructors take turns leading the grammar and pronunciation lessons. A shared class Facebook page carried short writing assignments between classes.

## How the Lessons Work

- **Getting to know each other.** On the first day the instructors introduce themselves and set a relaxed tone. Students should say when the teacher talks too fast or too slow, keep a steady sleep schedule after the long flight, and speak English in class, using Japanese only to help a classmate understand the instructions. Students decorate desk signs that show their interests and write down three things they are most excited to learn about the United States and the Midwest.
- **Grammar in small pieces.** Each grammar lesson is short and comes back the next day: sentence structure (subject, verb, object; transitive and intransitive verbs; conjugating “to be”), then nouns, verbs, articles, adjectives, prepositions, relative clauses, and conditionals. Pronunciation notes target sounds the students found hard, such as final “l,” “r,” short “i” (seat versus sit), and n/l, sh/j, b/p, and f/v.
- **Turning it around.** Students get chances to teach the teacher. One lesson ends with a ten-minute writing prompt, “If I traveled to Japan, where should I go and why?”, before everyone shares.
- **Vocabulary games.** Before the Sioux Falls trip, students review clothing and zoo-animal words with *Last Man Standing* (toss a ball and name a new word in the category), *Pictionary*, and a Jeopardy-style game.
- **Presenting what they saw.** At the zoo, each student picks an animal and gives a five-minute report the next day on its name, color, diet, habitat, behavior, and why they chose it. The instructor models it first with a short talk on the American bison.
- **Culture tied to the outings.** Background lessons come right before the matching trip: Plains Indian life, the buffalo’s traditional uses, and the winter count before Pipestone; how South Dakota became a state, why Pierre became the capital, and how the three branches of state government work; Hutterite colony life before the farm tour; and Laura Ingalls Wilder’s years in De Smet before the homestead visit. Discussion questions check the reading (“Was the reading difficult? Did you need to rely on a dictionary?”) and ask students to compare with Japan (“What is the capital of Japan? Why is it important to have a capital?”).
- **Listening and speaking.** Partners each get a tongue twister to practice over the weekend. A Monday listening quiz asks about the weekend with host families. Two-person role-play cards cover everyday problems: asking for tennis lessons, getting out of a party (which is secretly a surprise party), calling a wrong number to order pizza, asking for a borrowed book back, and returning sour milk.
- **Practical American life.** Short asides explain things like tipping at a restaurant (with the math worked out for a $15 bill), idioms the students heard and didn’t understand, and local words like “desperado,” Texas hold ’em, and pheasant season.
- **Writing after every trip.** After each outing, students post a paragraph (sometimes with a photo) on the class Facebook page about what they learned, and near the end they write about what they have learned about American culture so far.
