An important idea a counselor should consider is the theory they use when working with clients. There are many theoretical approaches a counselor can choose from, and it is important the counselor choose an approach that fits their own values. While working with a pseudo-client in class, Adlerian theory resonated with me because of its connection to the client’s social context. For this paper, I introduce key concepts of Adlerian theory, including its view on suffering, wellness, and the role of the counselor. I then make cultural considerations for Asian clients I may work with in the future. I then analyze my own use of the theory in my therapy session with my new-found knowledge of Adlerian theory.

## Adlerian Theory

Adlerian theory has its own base ideas of how a client may experience suffering, wellness, and the counselors’ role. The theory believes a person cannot be fully understood outside of their social context (Watts, 2017); a person learns how to comprehend outside information from their environment growing up: “the child sees the world in general as paralleling his home environment and eventually the wider world on the basis of his initial perceptions” (Watts, 2017, p. 143). Therefore, a counselor must assess the client’s early life in order to best understand how the client is viewing their present world. The counselor then views the client’s perception of the world to understand the client’s present lifestyle. To create an understanding of a client’s lifestyle, it is recommended that counselors assess “self-development, spiritual development, occupation, society, and love” (Sharf, 2016, p. 129).

The counselor also needs to understand the client in relation to their present social connection in order to help the client connect to their present surroundings (Yee, Stevens, & Schulz, 2016). Another form of assessment done by Adlerian counselors is level of social interest which involves three stages: “aptitude, ability, and secondary dynamic characteristics” (Sharf, 2016, p. 130). Through these elements, counselors can draw on the client’s feelings of inferiority or superiority in relation to others (Sharf, 2016). If a client feels inferior to those around them, they may be discouraged in their abilities, causing mental health issues.

### Suffering and Wellness

Suffering, from an Adlerian perspective, is based on the client’s lifestyle choices and amount of discouragement and hope. Yee et al. (2016) state that “Adler believed that mentally healthy people are people who care for the growth of society as a whole” (p. 92). In order to achieve this, a counselor needs to view the client from a holistic perspective and take the client’s environment into consideration (Watts, 2017, p. 143). This view and understanding can give the therapist insight into the client’s lifestyle.

A client’s lifestyle is created during childhood, and Adlerians believe issues may arise if the understanding from childhood is no longer serving the client in the present (Watts, 2017, p. 147). For example, a client with multiple personalities may have grown up using the other personalities to understand the world. However, in adulthood it is causing issues with memory loss and dissociation. These issues arising in the client causes distress and the client may be discouraged with their ability to handle this, once helpful, coping strategy.

Adlerian theory views suffering as a client being discouraged with their abilities to handle their life stressors (Bitter, Robertson, Healey, & Jones Cole, 2009). To continue the example, a client with multiple personalities is discouraged with understanding and using their alternate personalities in their everyday life. With the idea that discouragement causes client suffering, Adlerian theory uses “encouragement as the remedy to being discouraged” (Yee et al., 2016, p. 93). In this case example, Adlerian therapists would encourage a client’s ability to use and manage their alternate personalities in their life. An overarching goal in therapy is to help clients understand their worth through encouragement (Watts, 2017).

Adlerian therapists look at wellness from a social context and lifestyle perspective. 	To encourage social interest and wellness, Adlerian therapists do not like to use diagnostic labels with their clients. Watts (2017) states “clients are not sick (as in having a disease) and thus are not identified or labeled by their diagnoses” (p. 146). Many counselors view labels as causing demoralization, there is something wrong that needs a cure (Watts, 2017), and Adlerians “prefer to avoid diagnostic labels altogether” (Bitter et al., 2009, p. 22). As a counseling strategy, Adlerians use relabeling to empower the client’s abilities and remove any blame or shame that may be associated with a clinical diagnosis (Bitter et al., 2009). It is recommended that the client and counselor collaborate if a diagnostic label is to be used in the therapy (Bitter et al., 2009, p. 25). The counselor does not see themselves as an expert, so constant collaboration is crucial.

### Role of the Counselor

Adlerian therapy puts a lot of emphasis on the therapeutic alliance between the client and counselor. As the counselor is not viewed as the expert in the relationship, the counselor’s role is to be an encourager (Yee et al., 2016). A counselor must trust the client’s abilities and encourage them to trust their own capabilities (Watts, 2017, pp. 145-146). To help a client understand lifestyle, it is recommended that a counselor “model social interest” in the relationship through “congruence, empathic understanding, and unconditional positive regard” (Watts, 2017, p. 146).

## Cultural Application

There are considerations Adlerian counselors should make when working with Asian or Asian American clients. Asian-Americans are made up of many diverse backgrounds “representing 43 ethnic groups” (Carlson & Carlson, 2000). Therefore, therapists should keep in mind the specific background the client is coming from and its relation to Adlerian theory. For example, while the theory has an emphasis on birth order, due to governmental regulations, some Chinese clients may not have siblings (Sun & Bitter, 2012). Sun and Bitter (2012) recommend counselors consider birth order for Chinese clients by examining “the client’s childhood relationships both in the extended family and also in larger social settings” (p. 237) because they may not have siblings. Another difference is that South Korean clients may be connected to their culture’s view of *jeaong* (Sun & Bitter, 2012). There is no translation of this term into English, but its definition “is a combination of empathy, sympathy, compassion, emotional attachment, and tenderness;” without the element of *jeaong* in the therapeutic alliance, it is believed that no healing or human connection would be achieved (Sun & Bitter, 2012, p. 242). When a counselor is working with an Asian American client, it is important to know which cultural background in Asian the client identities with.

Elements of Adlerian theory can complement Asian cultural values. Many Asian cultures emphasize collectivism which is embraced by Adlerian theory’s focus on community and lifestyle (Sun & Bitter, 2012; Carlson & Carlson, 2000). Both Adlerian theory and collectivist cultures “assert that human growth is characterized by and accomplished through a productive engagement with others, rather than a decline into self-absorption or living in a self-centered manner” (Sun & Bitter, 2012, p. 236). An Adlerian therapist can help Asian clients embrace themselves as an individual while still supporting and contributing to the community, fulfilling their social role and expectations.

While Adlerian theory may be compatible with Asian cultures, therapists will still need to modify their approach to their clients. Asian clients may expect the counselor to take on an expert role in therapy sessions, which contrasts a typical Adlerian approach (Carlson & Carlson, 2000; Sun & Bitter, 2012). Carlson and Carlson (2000) recommend Adlerian counselors tailor their approach through “reeducation as a means of helping clients to deal with problems” (p. 220). This approach may help empower the clients to brainstorm solutions in the therapy sessions; however, solution-focus is another area of conflict for therapists working with Asian clients. Carlson and Carlson (2000) discuss the difficulties of exploring childhood and family dynamics with this population because clients have trouble understanding the importance of these elements to the present. These clients also would like to finish therapy rather quickly (Carlson & Carlson, 2000), so it is recommended that the therapist use a solution-focused Adlerian approach to help grow the therapeutic alliance by adhering to the clients’ preferences (Sun & Bitter, 2012).

On top of the lack of evidence-based research, it is difficult to find academic articles regarding the application of Adlerian theory with Asian clients (Carlson & Carlson, 2000; Sun & Bitter, 2012). Most of the research found synthesizes Adlerian theory and Asian cultural customs but does not provide application of the theory with clients. There needs to be a call to action for counselors to research the benefits of Adlerian Theory with this population. Non-Asian counselors should make space and give a voice to support this research and help increase cultural humility in the counseling field.

## Personal Assessment

When looking at my application of Adlerian theory, there are elements I did well and items that could be practiced more. There are four steps to the therapy process: establishing a relationship, assessment, insight, and reorientation to a new lifestyle (Carlson, n.d.). I practiced this theory in my third session with this client and there were elements of Adlerian theory I believe worked well. Adlerian therapists emphasize a strong therapeutic relationship (Watts, 2017; Bitter et al., 2009; Sun & Bitter, 2012). Through my previous sessions and this one, I believe I have created a strong therapeutic alliance with my client. She trusted me with the technique I brought to our session and was not resistant. She also [shared openly with] me, which is a sign of trust.

While I may have used Adlerian interventions listed in Sharf’s (2016) text, I do not feel I incorporated enough of the second therapy step (assessment of client’s childhood) into my session. Sperry (2018) wrote an article arguing that Adlerian therapy needs a more core strategy to the therapeutic process in order to become an evidence-based practice. Part of this argument stated that Adlerian therapists are too integrative with their intervention strategies and need to be more specific (Sperry, 2018). With this idea in mind, I do not believe I truly performed the way an Adlerian counselor would because I focused on interventions and goal setting instead of on assessment. Many Adlerian therapists emphasize early recollections in order to understand core beliefs (Sharf, 2016). There is also a large emphasis on the role the client played in their childhood in relation to others (Bitter et al., 2009). I did not attempt to ask my client about her role in her household growing up. While I inquired about a belief, there could have been more client-centered exploration and discovery regarding [that belief].

While I may have missed the assessment stage, I may have still offered my client the third stage, insight. By using some reorientation and reimagining, I asked my client what her life would look like [without those beliefs]. This reframing offered my client the ability to see what changes she may want to make in her life to be more empowered.

After my research regarding Adlerian theory, I believe the roots of the theory complement my personal ideas of wellness. When I took a course on systems perspectives, I was drawn to understanding an individual in relation to their systemic connections, but I do not want to do family therapy. I found connection with Adlerian therapy and its idea that a person cannot be understood without considering their social context. One decision a person makes consists of layers of beliefs that have years of environmental support and systemic repercussions. Therefore, I want to work from a base that is always thinking about social context.

## Conclusion

Similar to the lifestyle of a person, there are many elements that make up Adlerian theory. The base of the theory includes assessment of a client’s childhood and an analysis of their lifestyle and social interest. These ideas offer a particular way of viewing client suffering and wellness and what role the therapist plays in the therapeutic relationship. I practiced Adlerian theory with a client and found the theory to fit my ideas of wellness. While I may not have practiced the theory perfectly, there are elements I found empowering to the client and areas that I can improve my skills to be more successful.
