<p class="paper-title"><strong>My First Twelve-Step Meeting</strong></p>

I have found that hearing the stories of others’ experiences of hardship increases my motivation to be a better person and role model. On Friday, May 17, 2019, I attended my first Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) meeting(s) at [two local churches]. I was impacted by hearing the happiness and excitement AA members had for life and others. I am confident in referring and encouraging future clients to attend twelve-step programs after witnessing members talk about how their lives have been impacted. In this paper, I reflect on my experience attending these AA meetings, to include observations I made, and then state how this experience will inform my future work with clients.

## Unattended Meeting

The first meeting I attended was at [a local church in the morning]. As I pulled into the parking lot, parents were dropping off their children at Preschool, and I did not know where to go. I started to enter the education wing to ask someone where I should go, but there was no office to direct me, so I walked back to my car to look up a different meeting. While going back to my car, I saw there was a back-parking lot, and as I continued driving, in front of a small cabin unattached to the church, I saw a sign: “AA Spoken Here.” I parked my car and walked into the cabin to find one older woman reading a newspaper. She looked at me unexpectedly, and I introduced myself as a counseling student. She gave me the speeches read at the beginning of meetings and the AA approved literature given to members as they go through the program: *Daily Reflection*, *Alcoholics Anonymous*, and the twelve-step book. She also informed me of the local AA meetings held that day. After sharing stories about ourselves, I left to attend a different meeting.

### Analysis of Experience

I see positive and negative sides to attending this particular AA meeting. I appreciated that the leader was approachable, positive towards AA, and knowledgeable about the surrounding areas and steps of the program. She was also very open to talking about her experience with others and curious as to my own experiences. If I had a client with social anxiety, I would consider referring them to this meeting because I know it would be smaller. I would need to consider how my client may relate to the leader. If my client is looking for more of a fellowship or community, this meeting may not be ideal for them.

Some other negative aspects of this meeting were the location, time, and the size. The meeting took place in a small cabin with no windows that personally felt odd for me to locate and approach. The time of the meeting aligns with the start of a preschool time, so I was nervous driving through parents dropping off their children, though it may be convenient for one parent who drops off their child and then goes to AA. The time listed online was incorrect; the meeting was one hour in length instead of one and a half. Having two people attend the meeting was awkward; there were less shared experiences to connect to and discuss. Once our time was up for the meeting, the leader seemed hasty about leaving, so I went to a coffee shop for an hour between meetings to reflect on the first meeting and prepare myself for the next.

## Largely Attended Meeting

### My Experience

Coffee in hand, I pulled into [a second church] for their noon AA meeting and knew right away this meeting had higher attendance. A small group of women were outside chatting and smoking and a small group of men held a door open and welcomed people. I walked into a basement room lined with chairs and, because I was early, I sat and observed those around me. There was high energy in the room as laughter echoed off the walls. People welcomed each other with handshakes, hugs, and emotionally connected greetings. The walls displayed the twelve-steps and twelve-traditions for all to see, and in front of these posters were two desks with members that seemed like officers of a meeting.

Exactly at noon, a woman introduced herself and started to read the first sheet of the meeting. Two other sheets were read containing the steps and meeting goals, but I was unable to understand what any of what was being read because there were constant side conversations happening. In the first readings, I believe the leader stated something about a first step meeting happening elsewhere but was unable to fully understand what she said. The voices quieted as the readings stopped and the leaders asked for new members, visitors, and anniversary celebrators to introduce themselves. Then a woman read that day’s reflection, told her sobriety story, and called on specific members in the group to speak to her story. After specific members spoke, the meeting was opened for anyone to speak, and others shared. At exactly 1:00pm, everyone stood up, formed a circle holding hands around the room, and said a prayer. Following the prayer, everyone disbanded, talked in groups, and left. I walked back to my car, hearing the immense laughter behind me, and left holding the stories of the anonymous.

### Analysis of Experience

Two aspects of this meeting stand out to me as being very positive: atmosphere and mechanics. It was encouraging to feel the energy from the AA members before, during, and after the meeting. The atmosphere was welcoming, nonjudgmental, and pressure free. There was a large emphasis on fellowship that made this meeting welcoming. Due to the variety of ages, it was rather easy to find someone I could relate to while stories were being shared.

The mechanics of this meeting can help add to the positive atmosphere present. The location was easy to find because I was able to follow groups of people to the meeting doors; however, if I had been a little later, I may not have found the room because there was no signage, which is also understandable for anonymity purposes. There were diverse stories shared, people of all ages, and comfortable seating availability. During the meeting, sponsors raised their hands and the sponsor coordinators, separated by sex, were recognized for anyone that was new. I also appreciated the smaller group for anyone approaching the first step. New members can meet each other and go through the same items together, which helps create community and experiential connection. Through the positive atmosphere and mechanics of this meeting, I would comfortably refer a client here based on my experience.

Though I thoroughly enjoyed myself, I could see drawbacks referring a client to this type AA meeting. The meeting was well attended, but if I had a client struggling with anxiety or attention deficit problems, the large number of people accompanied by the noise and side conversations may be overstimulating. Due to the noise and side conversations, I was unable to hear any of the sheets that were read at the beginning of the meeting; so, if there was a person going to the meeting for the first time, they would not have been able to hear the steps or traditions being read. They also would have felt out of place when everyone recited prayers together as a group. I also noted that no one approached me before or after the meeting to talk with me. If I had a client that struggled with social interactions or starting conversations, they may feel uncomfortable approaching someone mid-conversation. After reflecting, I notice that this meeting was not culturally diverse. Most of the group members were white and based on the sponsors separating by sex, I am curious as to how a gender queer client may feel when attending this meeting. While there are few drawbacks I notice from this meeting, the few noted are key elements to not refer a client to this meeting.

## Connection to the Themes

After attending the two meetings, a few key themes were noted that coincide with the information I have learned in my counseling curriculum. Many people spoke about the friendships they have gained through AA. It was beautiful to listen to members talk about the true friendship and laughter share with each other. They expressed how important their sponsors were in their lives and how important their support was in their recovery process. Witnessing these stories gave new meaning to Lewis’s (2015) idea AA being a “mutual help groups:” “mutual is quite fitting given that the trust of AA is about providing mutual support between and among members” (p. 46). I felt jealous of the members sharing how close they are and how they knew deep down that someone was always there to support them no matter what.

Two themes particularly stood out for new AA members; the first theme was well stated by a member: “If you think all of this is shit, join the club.” Many people agreed that they started out believing that the steps and a higher power would not work. They encouraged new members to continue with the program and get a sponsor, even if they might think it would be useless. The second theme advised for new members was “do not focus on the differences in stories, focus on the similarities.” Members said focusing on the similarities is what helps you learn that you are not alone in your struggles. You can learn more about your own struggle by noticing the similarities in others’ stories. You know you can make it through if others who are like you have made it through. As a future counselor, I appreciated hearing this idea, so I can some day share this advice with a future client that may be struggling to connect with others. This advice not only applies to AA, it applies to anyone who may feel isolated in life.

Members’ recovery stories consisted of two heavy struggles; one, they were going to die if they did not change and two, they may have to cutoff friends or family to change. Lewis (2015) states, “addictive use of alcohol is destroying… lives” (p. 46). Members spoke about their realization that they were going to die if they did not cease using substances. Many members owe their lives to AA. A second struggle came from the realization that you cannot control others’ actions, no matter how hard you tried. Families have created roles for themselves and a homeostasis that include a loved one’s addiction (Strong, 2019). There can be resistance to changing a family’s homeostasis, which can cause relapse to happen once an addict becomes sober (Strong, 2019). The AA members learned that, no matter how hard they tried, they could not change other people in their lives. They have had to sacrifice important relationships in their lives during their recovery process. All the themes I noticed in the meeting aligned information I have been taught throughout my higher education, but hearing these truths come from the mouths of real people completely changed my view of the twelve-step program completely.

## Conclusion

After this experience, I now believe AA is a successful program for those struggling with alcohol addiction. I was moved by the success stories of those who have made it through the program and fully believe in the supportive, unjudgmental nature of the group. As a counselor, I feel it may be beneficial to know how active the AA meetings are in my client’s area. I may also want to know the exact location of where the meetings are held so I can describe the place to clients that may feel anxious about attending the meetings. I feel more confident about referring a client to a meeting and my abilities to describe what meetings contain and what are offered to AA members. I appreciate the opportunity to attend an open meeting, see the structure, and hear the personal stories.
