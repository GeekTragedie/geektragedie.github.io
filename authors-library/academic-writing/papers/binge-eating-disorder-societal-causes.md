<p class="paper-title"><strong>Binge Eating Disorder: Societal Causes</strong></p>

Between two and seven percent of people in the United States are or have been diagnosed with binge-eating disorder (BED) (Comer, 2015, p. 358). BED is diagnosed when a person has “recurrent episodes of binge eating… marked distress regarding binge eating… the binge eating occurs, on average, at least once a week for 3 months… the binge eating is not associated with the recurrent use of inappropriate compensatory behavior” (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). In comparison with other eating disorders, “individuals with binge eating disorder reported a significantly longer mean lifetime duration of illness” (Harrison et al., 2006). There are multiple explanations for the causes of this disorder: “childhood experiences, parental depression, vulnerability to obesity, and repeated exposure to negative comments about shape, weight, and eating” (Fairburn et al., 1998). Many of these leading factors are linked to stigma from society against those who are overweight. With this in mind, one of the main causes of binge-eating disorder is the stigma against overweight people. The stigma starts a chain reaction that leads to low self-esteem that triggers binge episodes, but there are steps that can be taken to help overcome the stigma and BED.

One of the causes of BED is stigmas against those who are overweight. Ebneter, Latner, and O’Brien (2011) claim that there are two stigmas against obesity that affect BED: “the belief that people’s fortunes or misfortunes are deserved, and the belief that obesity is controllable.” They are saying people believe that those who are obese deserve the obesity and have the ability to control it. Due to these beliefs, the researchers believe that these factors allow people to have “stigmatizing attitudes without feelings of guilt” (Ebneter, Latner, & O’Brien, 2011). With people not feeling guilty about their beliefs, it allows the stigma remain a social norm. The stigma against obesity that helps cause BED is the “perceptions of controllability” (Ebneter, Latner, & O’Brien, 2011). These are due to people believing “obesity is caused by lack of self-discipline” causing them “to blame obese people for their condition and stigmatize them accordingly” (Ebneter, Latner, & O’Brien, 2011). People that are not overweight put the blame on those who are overweight because they feel they can control this. The people not overweight blame others for their lack of ability to have good self-discipline. The negative ideas towards people who are overweight cause them to feel isolated and have negative thoughts about the way they look. These stigmas can cause a chain of events to happen in those who have BED.

The one effect from the stigmas against obesity is self-discrimination. Rudolph (2015) claims that “stigmatized obese individuals tend to internalize the pervasive weight stigma, which might lead to self-discrimination.” It also shows significant correlations between “experiences of weight stigma, depressive symptoms and implicit self-esteem” (Rudolph, 2015, p. 368). This study shows that the stigma against obesity makes the individuals that are obese internalize their feelings and discriminate against themselves. This self-discrimination creates lower self-esteem, a trigger for binge-eating disorder.

There are multiple triggers to binge eating that can be caused by the stigma of people that are obese. According to Simon Sherry (2009) “4 putative binge triggers [are] interpersonal discrepancies, low interpersonal esteem, depressive affect, and dietary restraint.” If one is self-discriminating, they are not going to have good interpersonal communication because their lack of self-esteem will cause them to lack interpersonal communication skills. This low self-esteem will cause interpersonal discrepancies and low interpersonal esteem. The inability to communicate efficiently with others will cause someone with binge-eating disorder to then binge. Some of the poor interpersonal exposures that someone with BED may face are “negative comments about shape, weight, and eating” (Fairburn et al., 1998). When exposed to these negative comments, it may cause someone with BED to binge. When people with BED experience negative conversations with others, it can trigger them to binge eat. The interpersonal triggers for binge eating can be explained by the relationship between food and the person suffering from binge-eating disorder. If someone encounters a negative comments, as stated above, they may have “emotions such as anger, fear, or sadness” (Johnston, 2004, p. 1459). Johnston explains that the relationship between the person with BED and food can be “characterized by the use of food as a drug to soothe disturbing emotions” (Johnston, 2004, p. 1459). If someone with BED has a poor experience interacting with an individual, they may turn to food for comfort.
Another binge trigger caused by societal stigma is dietary restraint (Sherry, 2009). Due to the fact that America is so oriented around one’s physical appearance, many people believe they should be on diets. There are multiple “body mass index charts, worksheets, handouts and posters” (Johnston, 2004, p. 1459). Not only are people being pressured by people in society to be on diets, physicians are also culprits for enhancing eating disorders. Johnston (2004) writes the following:

> Diet clubs and exercise facilities are filled with people who for most of their lives have been trying to control their bodies without ever being diagnosed as having an eating disorder. Their physicians are as oblivious or deluded as they are. (p. 1469)

If physicians are not willing to acknowledge eating disorders people suffer with, then people are going to have a more difficult time overcoming their disorders and lead a healthy life. Johnston discusses how society needs to shine light on these disorders and help people work through them instead of continuing to stigmatize those who are suffering (Johnston, 2004). With the pressure from society for everyone to be on a diet, there is also negative feelings towards breaking a diet. Attached to the idea of self-discrimination, if one breaks a diet rule, it can also trigger a binge (J. Puckett, personal communication, March 21, 2016). With society’s constant focus on diets, it is causing those with BED to binge more often.

When looking at society and its effect on those with BED, there are certainly steps people can take to can help. Johnston believes people are overlooking those with eating disorders. People should talk more directly about it and stop being passive about the problem (Johnston, 2004). Addressing the fact that those who have problems with obesity do not deserve it and that it is not always controllable (Ebneter, Latner, & O’Brien, 2011). When addressing these ideas to the public, it may be met with resistance because these ideas are used as a safety net for people feeling guilty. However, it is important for people to understand or feel guilty for these negative feelings towards those who are obese and what they have caused in those who have BED. While abolishing these stigmas, it may also lower the amount of “negative comments about shape, weight, and eating” said to those who have BED and this may, in turn, help improve the interpersonal relationships between those who have the BED and society (Fairburn et al., 1998). With enhancing these relationships, it will help lower the amount of binge triggers (Sherry, 2009). If society takes a step away from people simply being skinny and dieting, it may also decrease the triggers for those who binge due to breaking their diets (Sherry, 2009). People should be looking for the next steps in helping decrease the number of people with BED. While it may be a relatively new disorder, that does not make it any less real.

Overall, if society decreases their stigma against those who are overweight and accepts them for who they are, there will certainly be a decrease in binge episodes for people who have BED. The first step in decreasing BED is understanding the stigmas that society has against people who are overweight. Then people need to look at the different triggers for binges in order to finally discover ways to help people who suffer from BED. People may even be able to help so greatly that the amount of people in our population with BED will fall to a significant low.
