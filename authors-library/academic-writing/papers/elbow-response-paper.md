<p class="paper-title"><strong>Elbow Response Paper</strong></p>

As a writing consultant, I have been in numerous conversations with other writers regarding their writing processes and have realized most people have their own form of the writing process that has worked the best for them. Writing is a personalized activity and, while people may have similar processes, no two people are going to write the same exact way. Peter Elbow (1998) in his book *Writing with Power* argues everyone should use the same two-phase process he has created to be the most effective writers (p. 6-12). While Elbow appears to believe his two-phase approach is best for the writing process, my experiences with writing and creative arts leads me to disagree with his theory.

The first idea where I disagree with Peter Elbow’s argument is regarding brainstorming freely. Elbow (1998) believes in “the power of brainstorming” (p. 8), and I fully agree that brainstorming is a very helpful stage in the writing process; however, I disagree in the way Elbow describes brainstorming should be completed. Elbow (1998) discusses that one should “first write freely” (p. 7) to create their ideas so “no one is allowed to criticize *any* idea or suggestion that is offered” (p. 8). I agree that creating ideas is important, but, from my experience, keeping these ideas to myself has not been helpful. I discuss my ideas with trusted friends and writing consultants, so they can help me eliminate, what Elbow (1998) states are “stupid, impractical, or useless” ideas (p. 8). Overall, these discussions help me save time and effort. While it may be beneficial for some to use free-writing to create ideas, I have found brainstorming aloud with other trusted people to be a more valuable use of my time.

Another idea in which I disagree with Elbow is in the area of organization. Elbow (1998) claims “when people think to much during the early stages [of writing] about what they want to end up with, that preoccupation with the final product keeps them from attaining it” (p. 7). Elbow (1998) does not believe people should think too much about what they want to say in the beginning stages because he believes it hinders the creativity (p. 7). With my experience as a writer, I completely disagree. I always use a very detailed outline in my writing because it keeps my mind on track as to what I am supposed to be writing about. I keep my paper very organized from the beginning. Dr. Raimondo Genna (personal communication, Spring 2016), my Devised Theatre professor and chair of the theatre department, taught me that adding rules and barriers to what you are doing can actually help creativity thrive more. So, while Elbow (1998) believes adding too much structure early on can diminish the creative side of the brain (p. 7), my past experience in writing and theatre leads me to disagree with this claim.

One final area that leads me to disagree with Elbow’s two-phase process deals with the critical creative person. Elbow (1998) states that “creativity is strong only if critical thinking is weak, or vice versa” (p. 9). While this phenomenon may be true for some people, I believe his statement is too generalized. I believe I am a critical creative person given my background in theatre. When taking on the responsibilities of a director, it is important for me to be both creative and critical at the same time. I have had to be critical of what the audience is going to view and what the technical directors are able to accomplish. If a director leads the actors or their design team to be too creative, the production will not be a success. It is important for the director to be both organized in what they want accomplished, but also creative in their viewing of the production. Through my experience as a director, it makes me disagree with Elbow’s (1998) argument that one cannot be both creative and critical at the same time (p. 7). Though, maybe fellow directors and I are simply what Elbow (1998) describes as “rare [people] who [are]… both highly intuitive and highly organized” (p. 10).

While Elbow (1998) is attempting to “direct [his] book to a very broad audience” using his two-phase approach to writing (p. 6-7), my experience has led me to disagree with his theory. I disagree with Elbow’s view on brainstorming, his take on too much planning in the early stages, as well as his view of the critical creative person. While Elbow’s two-phase approach does not work for me, it may work for some people. I encourage everyone to find their own personal approach to writing they feel fits them the best.
