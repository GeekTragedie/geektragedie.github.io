<p class="paper-title"><strong>LGBTQ Community Representation in Education</strong></p>

Imagine you are a cisgender, heterosexual female.  You are walking down the street, and someone says, “He looks like someone I know.” You go to school and there are only male bathrooms.  You and your boyfriend want to order food at a restaurant, but the waiter refuses you service because their religion does not believe in heterosexuality.  Sexual minorities and gender minorities face these select few problems (wrong pronoun use, no bathroom inclusiveness, and refusal of service) almost every day.  There are shown reasons as to why the United States has prejudice against the LGBTQ+ community, socially and institutionally.  One way to decrease prejudice and discrimination in the United States is to include sexual minority (SM) and gender minority (GM) education in high schools.

## Background

Before discussing how to implement the discussion of SMs and GMs into high school education, it is important to understand the differences between the two groups, because most of the time they are linked together under LGBTQ.  Math and Seshadri (2013) define sexual minorities as those who “are a group whose sexual identity, orientation or practices differ from the majority of the surrounding society” (Abstract).  The definition of sexual orientation is “how a person characterizes their emotional and sexual attraction others” (Glossary of LGBT terms, 2016, para.  5).  Some examples of SMs are gay, bisexual, pansexual, and asexual.  A key term to keep in mind when considering SMs are heteronormativity, “the assumption that everyone is heterosexual and that heterosexuality is superior to all other sexualities” (Glossary of LGBT terms, 2016, p.  3).  An example of heteronormativity is parents asking their children if they have a girlfriend if they are a little boy.  Another term to consider is homonormativity, which is the assumption that there is one specific way for sexual minorities to act and live their life and putting heterosexual ideals onto a sexual minority couple.  An example of homonormativity is when it is assumed in a lesbian relationship that one of the women is the dominant role.

Gender minorities are also members of the LGBTQ community that need to be defined and understood when considering applying GM into education.  GMs are people whose gender is not the majority gender, cisgender.  It is important to understand the different between sex and gender.  Sex is the biology someone has while gender is how someone identifies themselves.  Gender identity is defined as “A person’s internal sense of being a man, woman, both, neither, or another gender” (Glossary of LGBT terms, 2016, p.  3).  Some examples of gender minority identities are agender, bigender, trigender, pangender, gender flux, gender fluid, and transgender.  Gender expression is separate than gender identity; gender expression is “the way a person acts, dresses, speaks, and behaves (does not necessarily correspond to the sex at birth OR gender identity)” (Glossary of LGBT terms, 2016, p.  2).  One must know what cisnormativity is in order to understand GM perspectives as well; cisnormativity is “the assumption that everyone is cisgender – their gender aligns with their sex assigned at birth” (Glossary of LGBT terms, 2016, p.  3).  Examples of cisnormativity are toys and clothes sections in stores and bathrooms that are not gender inclusive.

## Reasons for Prejudice

There are multiple prejudice types that enhance prejudice against the LGBTQ community.  Social learning theory enhances the prejudice though families teaching their children, peers teaching their peers prejudice, schools enhancing cisnormativity and heteronormativity in education, churches teach that the bible frowns upon SMs and GMs, and the media lacking representation of GMs and SMs.  Stereotyping increase prejudice through homonormativity and assumptions of sexual orientation and gender identity.  Right Wing Authoritarian personality types increase stigma in two ways.  They have a high stigma against SMs and GMs.  People who have a right wing authoritarian personality and are a SM experience high self-stigma because of their sexual orientation.  Social dominance theory shows that those who are minority groups are stigmatized because of their minority status.  There is certainly political conservativism through discrimination laws that do not support GMs and SMs (please see Appendix A).  Religiosity certainly largely impacts prejudice against this community through multiple religions teaching that SMs are against their religion.  There is also prejudice due to the lack of openness to experience.  There is a study done by Dr.  Cindy Struckman-Johnson about homophobia on at the University of South Dakota.  The findings of this research was that the Midwest is sheltered and many students on this campus have not met a SM or GM (Struckman-Johnson, 2017, p.  2).

## Problems this Population Faces

People are prejudice against the LGBTQ community both on an institutional level and individual level.  This prejudice affects the community in multiple negative ways.  First, it is important to look at the employment laws and policies, or lack thereof, in the United States.  20 states have laws that prohibit discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity.  Two states prohibit discrimination based on sexual orientation only.  Six states prohibit discrimination against public employees based on sexual orientation and gender identity.  Five states prohibit discrimination against public employees based on sexual orientation only.  17 states have no discrimination laws in place to protect LGBTQ people for employment.  For more information and a visual, please see appendix A.  The lack of laws protecting the LGBTQ community show the institutional prejudice society has.

Not only does the United States lack in laws to help the LGBTQ community, there are also individual stigmas that are held against the community: enacted stigma, felt stigma, and internalized stigma.  Enacted stigma is “the overt behavioral expression of sexual stigma by individuals” (Herek, Gillis, & Cogan, 2009, p.  34).  These stigmas are direct behaviors done against a community.  Some examples of this stigma are hate crimes and employment discrimination.  Felt stigma is “an individual’s knowledge of society’s stance towards” them (Herek et al., 2009, p.  34).  These are a person’s feelings of society not accepting them and possible behaviors that can evolve from this feeling.  Some examples of this stigma are being scared of attending a class for the fear that a professor will call you by the wrong name or not holding your significant other’s hand in public.  There is also internalized stigma that a person feels due to society.  Internalized stigma is “an individual’s personal acceptance of… stigma as part of her or his own value system and self-concept” (Herek et al., 2009, p.  34).  An easier understanding this idea is the negative sense of self as an LGBTQ person based on stigma.  An example of this concept is internalized homophobia.  All of these stigmas take a toll on LGBTQ members and there are large side effects.

A large effect of stigma and prejudice that the LGBTQ community faces are mental health issues.  Among these mental health concerns are higher counts of anxiety, depression and suicide attempts (Raifman, Moscoe, Austin, & McConnell, 2017).  According to Raifman et al.  (2017), heterosexual adolescents have a suicide attempt rate of 8.6% while LGB adolescents have a suicide attempt rate of 28.5%.  However, these rates have decreased since same-sex marriage laws were passed in 2015.  It is important to emphasize that passing the law legalizing same-sex marriage decreased the percentage of suicide attempts for *all* adolescents significantly by .6%.  The percentage of LGB adolescent suicide attempts also went down significantly by 4% (Raifman et al., 2017, p.  351).  The researchers assume that the reason for the significant drop in overall suicide attempts for adolescents is because society is more accepting of all people, which makes adolescents more comfortable in their environment (Raifman et al., 2017, p.  350).  Based off of these results, it is important to society to decrease the prejudice against the LGBTQ community.  One way to do this is by being more accepting in high schools.

## Fixing Prejudice in High Schools

### Why High Schools

It is important to understand why society needs to face the prejudice against the LGBTQ community through high school education.  As discussed above, adolescent suicide rates decreased when the United States passed same-sex marriage laws, showing that society is more accepting.  To support that SM information should be in, adolescents are in Freud’s psychosexual genital stage and trying to figure out their sexual orientation (Arnett, 2016).  Arnett (2016) also shows that adolescence is when sexual feelings are beginning to emerge.

As far as GM education in high school goes, research shows that children know they are a GM as early as age 2.  Many children figure out that they are a GM between the ages of 2 and 5 (Genderkinderen, 2013).  To support that GM information should be implemented into high school education, Erik Erikson states that adolescents, ages 12 to 18, are in the developmental stage of identity versus role confusion (Arnett, 2016).  This stage is when people are finding who they are, this includes a person’s gender identity.  If someone does not fulfill this stage, Erikson believes that they will have difficulty with their identity throughout their life.  This is why high schoolers should learn about GMs because if they identify as a GM, then they will be more successful in their life.  For the overall well-being of adolescents, especially in the LGBTQ community, it is important to include SMs and GMs into our high school education.

### School’s Problems

While it is important to educate student about LGBTQ people, it is also important to understand how exclusive our high schools are.  There are many problems in schools that the LGBTQ community faces.  One general issue LGBTQ students face are anti-bullying policies, or lack thereof.  While there are 20 states that have laws that address harassment and bullying of students based on sexual orientation and gender identity, all other states are exclusive to protect this community.  Two states have laws that prevent school districts from protecting LGBT students and eight states have laws that restrict the inclusion of LGBT topics.  For more information on these laws and states, please see appendix B.

**Sexual minority issues.**  SM issues can be seen starting with teachers and staff.  Teachers and staff normally make assumptions about who is dating who and what students’ sexual orientation are, without the proper education on SMs.  Many teachers do not talk about SMs either because they may consider it a political issue that they should not discuss in class.  The lack of education for teachers is very important.  It is important for teachers to know the struggles that SMs face in order to protect their students.  There is a case in Sioux Falls, SD that a girl in high school was caught by the school kissing another girl in the bathroom.  The school called the parents to tell them that their daughter was caught kissing another girl in the bathroom.  The girl’s family was very religious and ended up beating the girl because of her behavior.  While the girl was taken away from her parents and put into foster care, she has to live with this trauma for the rest of her life.  It was not the school’s place to out the girl to her family, and if the school understood SM struggles, maybe they would have been more sensitive towards the issue.

There is little sex education in schools that is for SMs.  Most of the sex education teaches abstinence only; while this has its own issues, many other education programs only teach heterosexual education.  SM sex education is also important to teach in school.  This issue is also seen in textbooks where the information for SMs is very limited.  If there is a SM in a school, many people ask that one person to explain their sexual orientation for others due to the lack of information from schools.  These explanations can get old and discriminatory, depending on the questions asked.  Prom is also an issue for SMs because mainstream advertising has heterosexual couples and many proms are envisioned as heterosexual couples.  There are also only a king and queen royalty in prom, which keeps the idea of heterosexual couples being praised in our heteronormative society.

**Gender minority issues.**  Prom and homecoming royalty is also an issue to GMs because there are only kings and queens.  For a person that does not identify as either male or female, there is no place for them in royalty at the school.  There are also other issues GMs face in schools, such as sports.  There are male and female sports, which causes an issue to those who do not identify as either of these genders.  There have also been issues with which team and sports transgender students can participate in.  These issues need to be discussed and worked out.

GM students also are excluded when it comes to bathrooms.  The Gavin Grimm case went through the courts and set a precedent for school bathroom policies around the United States.  Gavin is a transgender male that transitioned in high school.  When he transitioned, he asked the school if he could use the men’s room and the school gave him permission.  For two week Gavin used the men’s room, until a school board meeting approached and parents came in and argued with the school board about Gavin using the men’s room.  The school board told Gavin that he had to continue to use the nurse’s bathroom, which was out of his way.  Gavin sued the school.  The court case was appealed all the way to the Supreme Court, until President Trump revoked President Obama’s definition of Title IX.  The Supreme Court then sent the case back down to the appellate courts who ruled in favor of the school.  While the court case seems as though they really were sympathetic towards Gavin’s case, they told him that he needs to find another reason to sue the school after President Trump’s revoke.  This ruling was disappointing to transgender students’ rights around the world.  It is important for GMs to feel comfortable in schools, and bathrooms are a portion of this comfort.  In order to feel accepted in public, a person should have a place to use the restroom.

There are more issues in schools than bathrooms that GMs face.  Names on class rosters are one.  Many names on rosters are names that are assigned to students at birth.  If GM student is in class and their name assigned at birth is read off, they are automatically outed to the class as a GM.  Many textbooks only discuss gender binary issues.  There are boy and girl specific schools.  Puberty talks for students are always split between boys and girls, but what about students that do not fit into the gender binary? Same as SM, many teachers also do not talk about GM issues because they see it as a political issue.  If the teachers are not educated, then how are students supposed to learn about themselves and those around them? Not discussing the LGBTQ community does not prepare students for life outside of high school and does not prepare students to have educated knowledge on political issues.  It is important to inform students about issues and let them critically think about the issues themselves before making decisions.

## Proposed Solution

For the multiple issues the LGBTQ community faces, it is important to decrease prejudice against this population.  It is important to educate society about LGBTQ issues, so including SMs and GMs into the high school education system will help decrease prejudice based off of the social learning theory.  There are two groups of education that can be implemented into high schools to help decrease the prejudice using multiple levels of prejudice prevention theories: general implementation and LGBTQ Education Week.

### General Implementation

To decrease prejudice against the LGBTQ community, there are three aspects of general implementation schools should use: education of teachers, sex education, and class introductions.  The first step to general implementation is educating the teachers.  The teachers should know about the struggles the LGBTQ faces and a general history of the community.  They should know the information covered in the background section above.  They should also be taught to teach students about the LGBTQ community and how to work with students that may be transition or have transitioned.  The teachers should also learn the different, non-linear stages LGBTQ students go through.  It is important for teachers to know these stages so that they can teach these stages to students, but also help students through the stages and give students information and support.  It is also important for teachers to understand that the stages are non-linear, because not all SMs and GMs will go through the stages in the same order if they go through all the stages at all.  If someone knows the stages and thinks they are linear, if they do not go through a certain stage or goes through the stages out of order, this can cause a person distress that they are not “transitioning correctly,” when there is no right or wrong way to transition.

**Non-linear stages.**  It is important for teachers to know the non-linear stages for GM people, SM people, and SM couples.  The first non-linear stages a teacher should know are those of GM people.  Ashley Austin (2016) researched GM transitions and lists six non-linear stages that GM people go through.  The six stages teachers should know are “moving from uncertainty to knowing, finding me, recognizing self in others, explaining work, struggling for authenticity, and evolving self-acceptance” (Austin, 2016, p.  221).

There are different non-linear stages teachers should know for SM people are for transitions and couples.  The non-linear stages that teachers should know for SM people’s individual transition, as defined by Dr.  Vivienne Cass in Kenneady and Oswalt’s (2014) article, are: prestage, assuming one’s sexual orientation; stage 1, identity confusion; stage 2, identity comparison; stage 3, identity tolerance; stage 4, identity acceptance; stage 5, identity pride; stage 6, identity synthesis (pp.  231-232).  The non-linear stages teachers should know about SM couples, because they are different than heterosexual relationships, are defined by Macapagal, Greene, Rivera, and Mustanski (2015) as the following: discernment (meeting and talking); initiation (dating, having sex, using condoms, and getting tested); negotiation (relationship agreements); cohabitation (living together); commitment (introducing their partner to their family or friends, engagement and marriage, having children) (pp.  312-314).

Another general implementation a school can do is including SMs into their sex education, as discussed above.  This may start by teaching the teachers SM sex education and updating textbooks to include SMs.  It is the all about implementing the information into the class room.

Implementing a class introduction policy would protect students that are a GM for being outed in classes.  How this would work is that students would introduce themselves in classes at the beginning of the year saying their name and pronoun so that all students felt safer in classes and more included.  This limits the possibility of a teacher outing a GM student and also allows students the opportunity to know that there are other pronouns besides he and she.  If a student identifies as another pronoun, then the teacher can have a moment in class to teach the other students on how to be respectful to the GM student’s pronouns.   Schools, overall, should implement these ideas of educating teachers, updated the sex education, and including class introductions, into high schools.

### LGBTQ Education Week

Using the multiple prejudice theories, high schools should implement an LGBTQ education week into their schools so that students learn more about SMs and GMs.

**Monday.**  The first day is very oriented around the social learning theory.  This day is dedicated to teaching students about sexual minorities.  The day will start out by teaching the student different sexual orientations, as listed in the background section.  This history also includes the history of SMs in the DSM.  Teachers will then teach students the history of SM people, so they can understand the struggle.  Teachers will also educate the students that sexual orientation is not a choice to anyone.  Teaching discrimination laws that include and/or exclude SMs is also important.  When teaching these laws, teachers should not try to persuade students one way or another; just teaching the facts is the teacher’s job.  This discussion will help the students’ critical thinking skills, so they can gain make up their own minds.  Teachers should also teach the non-linear stages of SM people and couples on this day.

**Tuesday.**  Tuesday, using the social learning theory of prejudice, will be dedicated to GM education.  Explaining the difference between sex and gender, examples of GMs, and cisnormativity is important on this day.  Teachers will teach the history of this group and their struggles in society faces.  It is also especial to show the research that upholds that gender identity is not a choice.  A teacher can also teach students how to use pronouns that are not he and she, so that when students get out of school, they can respectfully use the correct pronouns of those they meet.  Continuing education on the discrimination laws and non-linear stages of GM people will also happen on this day.

**Wednesday.**  This day of the week is dedicated to perspective taking.  All students will be given a SM and GM identity and have to walk a day in the shoes of this minority member.  On this day, the bathrooms can be inconveniently located on a far side of the school.  The students can also take a test as a member of the LGBTQ community.  The test can be a standardized test, college application, or psychological survey that is strictly exclusive of the LGBTQ community.  At the end of the day, students can sit around and discuss the struggles they experienced that day.  An exercise to do once the day is through is have all the students write down the privilege they have as a majority member.

**Thursday.**  The fourth day is dedicated to contact hypothesis.  The school should bring in a guest speaker to talk to the students about being a member of the LGBTQ community.  Some examples of guest speakers could be a researcher, community member, professor, or professional LGBTQ speaker, such as Ivan Coyote.  By doing this, the students are exposed to a member of the community and the struggles this person may face.  This can decrease prejudice through understanding that speaker’s perspective and hearing their story.  LGBTQ students can meet someone they can identify with, which helps with their stages of development.

**Friday.**  This is another day dedicated to perspective taking.  The school can bring in a performance of a show that shows the struggles of an LGBTQ member.  The performance can be brought in from the community or done by students.  An example of this is the Washington Pavilion’s DAPA program in Sioux Falls, South Dakota.  These performances are done by high school actors around the area.  The actors travel to various schools and perform plays that highlight certain struggles, such as drugs, alcohol, and bullying.  The program could show LGBTQ members’ struggles as well.  The students can identify with actors that are their act and better understand the situations.  The school could also bring in a play or perform a play that discusses LGBTQ struggles, such as *Next Fall* or *The Laramie Project*.  While these shows may need to be censored for a high school audience, the overarching storyline is the important part.  The day can also show videos of LGBTQ members talking about their experience and struggles so that students are more exposed to members of the community.  What is the most important on this day is to highlight the happiness the LGBTQ community has.  While the week may seem very negative and teach about the hardships, it is important to teach the good things.  Show that those who are in the LGBTQ community are truly happy and love who they are, even though they go through all these struggles.  The struggles are worth it.

## Conclusion

Overall, it is important for high schools to include LGBTQ information into education.  There are many reasons why society is prejudice against this population and the LGBTQ community faces a lot of struggles that can have dire outcomes.  To decrease the prejudice, high schools should be more inclusive and teach students more about the LGBTQ community.  There are many reasons why high school is the primary time to teach people about the LGBTQ community and there are many things schools should change in order to be more inclusive.  A good solution for schools to implement is to change their general information and include an LGBTQ education week.  Generally, schools should educate their teachers more on the LGBTQ community so they can help their students.  Schools should also change their sex education programs to include SMs and change the way they do class introductions.  The LGBTQ education week should emphasize the prejudice prevention tactics of the social learning theory, perspective taking, and contact hypothesis.  This implementation can both help LGBTQ adolescents with their identity and decrease the prejudice of other students.  The LGBTQ community is struggling.  more people in society should show respect to people for who they are, not try to shut them out.
