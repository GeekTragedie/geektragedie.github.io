<p class="paper-title">Sandra Clark Journal Analysis</p>

Sandra Clark’s essay, “*Macbeth* and the Language of the Passions,” takes the humoural theory and uses it to analyze Macbeth’s actions throughout the play. Clark explains the humours and how they were viewed during the time Shakespeare was writing. Clark then applies this theory to examples in *Macbeth* where Macbeth experiences these humours. Audiences during the early modern English period were familiar with the humoral theory, and, because of this understanding, Clark concludes that audiences may have viewed Macbeth differently and “could still empathise with \[Macbeth’s\] guilt and terror” (309).

Sandra Clark’s argument is very well thought-out and understandable. Clark’s reading of the play seems very critical. Her description of Macbeth’s physical difficulties of “sleeplessness” and the fact that he is “fevered” are two examples discussed in the essay (305, 306). The physical ailments are caused by imbalanced humours and then can cause other imbalances, for example “humours in \[Macbeth’s\] stomach, causing vapours to ascend to the brain” (305). The main connection to the play is that Macbeth does not have the opportunity to control his humours or try to cure himself. Clark states that “Macbeth is bitterly away that he has polluted his mind” (306). Even though Macbeth is aware of the issue, “his mind and body have been taken over” (307). Macbeth cannot control the problems he is having because his body has taken over. The audiences in early modern England would understand this takeover idea and possibly empathize with Macbeth, having possibly dealt with the same basic idea.

While Sandra Clark’s main argument is valid, the evidence used could have been more directly connected to the text. The theory used was the humoral theory; however, there was more talk about the physical ailments. While these ailments still apply to the theory utilized, there should have been a more direct relation to the humours. With the humours well explained, the vocabulary for the humorus should have been employed more throughout the essay. This usage would have made it easier on the reader to understand the humours’ connection and understand the evidence.

There is evidence in the play that can provide a more direct relation to the humoural theory that could have been used. There is a lot of talk about the brain in both the essay; however, the humour that directly links to the brain is phlegm, which is rarely discussed. The idea of fire is also discussed multiple times. The humour that connects to fire is choleric, which could have been more directly discussed in the essay. Another piece of evidence that could have been utilized is the talk of summer in the play and how summer also connects to choleric and fire. Banquo tells Duncan that the king is “this guest of summer” (1.6.3). With the king being a guest during the summer, that means the play takes place in summer. The choleric temperament is most persistent in the summer. This connection could be another supporting piece of evidence for Clark’s argument.

In Sandra Clark’s article, “*Macbeth* and the Language of the Passions,” she argues that Macbeth’s actions can be understood if readers understand the humour theory behind Macbeth’s actions. While Clark provides a lot of evidence, there could have been more direct points and evidence used. Audiences during the early modern English period may have been more empathetic towards Macbeth because, to them, he was sick with a disorder. With this idea in mind, it is important to think of the audience during the time period of which a play is written to fully understand how a playwright may want a character to be comprehended.
