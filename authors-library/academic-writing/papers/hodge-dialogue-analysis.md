## Choice of Words

There are particular words used in this script to heighten the stakes of certain situations. There are words used that are not necessarily used in modern speaking language anymore, but the meanings of the words portray a deeper meaning than words that can have similar meaning. The words used in the script help the audience connect with the characters better and help them more fully understand the sorrow and the agony Kreon is going through. It is through words such as “burdened,” “bitterness,” and “calamity” (Sophocles 1391, 1409, 1497) that Kreon portrays the high stakes of his situation. The audience is also better able to understand how guilty Kreon feels in his situation through the words used: “appease,” “wretched,” and “warp” (1430, 1464, 1507).

## Choice of Phrases

The choice of phrasing in the scene is used to emphasize words at the end of a line. This idea is discussed in the section regarding lines and speeches. Phrasing is also used to use alliteration in some instances. While there is an example of this idea in the sound of dialogue section, it is also shown when the messenger is telling Kreon about his wife’s death. She states: “so she could suffer the same wound” (1470). The use of the ‘s’ sound creates the idea of a snake, symbolizing evil. This phrasing give the audience an impression of something evil in the midst of what is happening. Some phrasing is also used to emphasize ideas. One example of this emphasizes Kreon’s desire to die when he repeats this idea in a speech, “Let it come, let it happen now--/ Let it happen, let it come” (1483, 1487). By repetition, it shows the audience how much Kreon’s desire is felt. It more portrays his agony and suffering.

## Choice of Images

In the dialogue, there is use of imagery multiple times throughout the scene. The first image created is through Kreon’s lines: “a god struck my head / an immense blow” (1410-1). This line shows the audience how large of a role the gods have in the play. This image is also an example of the violence of the gods, explaining how the gods punished Kreon for his acts violently. The next image used is also in with Kreon’s lines when he states that his happiness has been “stamped out” (1415). Using this imagery personifies Kreon’s happiness as if it were a fire, letting off light. The happiness is taken away, violently, and Kreon is left in the darkness with no way to look around. One more example of imagery is when Kreon shows to Hades that they have flooded his “harbor” (1430-1). This, again, shows the importance of the gods to the people in the play. It also shows how much death has happened in the recent time. Floods have a certain destructive nature to them, so the fact that he uses this imagery in a time of people dying helps portray the feeling of sorrow.

Another image used is by the Messenger when he states that Kreon’s “hands are full of sorrow” (1418) and Kreon “bear[s] its full weight” (1419). While sorrow does not actually weigh anything, this image shows how sorrow can make you feel down and heavy, depressed. The messenger is also in charge of telling Kreon how the queen died. In order to show the full effect to the audience, because there is little violence on stage, the messenger needed to say all the details of what happened. The messenger, then, tells the story of how Eurydike killed herself.

The leader uses imagery in his dialogue when he says, “It’s best to say little/ in the face of evil” (1481-2). Evil does not have a face to talk to. The leader is trying to show others what Kreon is dealing with and how to cope with it. To continue with this imagery, the leader later tells Kreon, “No human being/ evades calamity/ once it has struck” (1496-8). This line gives the image of someone attempting to get away from a struggle, as if it were a living thing, but cannot.

The imagery in this scene shows the audience how the people are feeling in a more elaborate way so that the audience can better understand the feelings and situations happening in the lives of the characters. It also shows how much effect the gods play in the lives of the characters. Many images are personified to help show how important the feelings are, as if they were living objects having a mind of their own.

## Choice of Peculiar Characteristics

I believe the scene makes good choices for characteristics for the characters. Kreon’s dialogue matches up with that of someone grieving. His use of language helps elaborate his sorrow. Him talking about his guilt for his son’s death also shows his polar attitude. He was blindly proud, but has changed his personality to accommodate for his actions.

I feel as though the leader’s dialogue shows the speaks more towards the audience and explaining ideas. He is there more as a know-it-all, as if he was able to predict these events were to happen. The leader seems to be wise in his ways and believe that Kreon deserves the pain he is suffering. The leader seems to hold an arrogant manner as well, as he tells Kreon information.

The messenger seems hesitant to tell Kreon the information. She knows that Kreon is above her, however, she fully believes that Kreon deserves what came to him when telling her tale. While she believes he deserves this, she also eases into her information more. She is less harsh and slowly builds up her you-deserved-this attitude.

## The Sound of the Dialogue

The dialogue of the various characters sound different, which helps develop their characters. The lines of Kreon are more drawn out and have longer vowel sounds. The consonants also sound less harsh in comparison to the other characters. For example: “Oh, what errors of the mind I have made!” (1395). In this line, the majority of the vowels and consonants are as explained above. The leader has a harsher sounding to his dialogue when talking to Kreon. There is the use of alliteration to influence this harshness: “took it far too late” (1407). The messenger’s tone comes across as more of a story telling tone, which is understandable considering that is their role in the play.

## Structure of Lines and Speeches

The script is written in verse and the way that it is written is good for showing which words are to be emphasized. The words at the end of a line are more important, most of the time, than those that are in the middle. With this information, it is important to see how each line ends to find more meaning in the scene. A good example are the lines Kreon says: “You all see it—the man/ who murdered, and the son/ who’s dead. What I did/ was blind and wrong!” (1398-1401). In these lines, it shows how much Kreon is emphasizing how *his actions*, what *he did,* was what *wrong* and how his actions killed his own *son.* While the line talks about murder and death, the more important information is that Kreon’s actions caused the wrongful death. The rest of the scene can be read in this manner and it unfolds different meanings.
