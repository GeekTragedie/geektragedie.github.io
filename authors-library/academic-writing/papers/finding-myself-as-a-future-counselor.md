<p class="paper-title"><strong>Finding Myself as a Future Counselor</strong></p>

Honestly, at the beginning of the quarter, I did not believe I would learn anything from the Communication and Counseling Skills course that I could apply to myself as a counselor. I soon realized how wrong I was. I was quickly infected by my professor’s enthusiasm for the subject matter, fascinated by the techniques, and enjoyed my taping sessions with my clients. Throughout this course, I have grown as a counselor because my view of the profession has changed, I have new insight to my abilities, and I can set goals for myself as a future counselor.

## Counseling Profession

My view of the counseling profession has changed over the course of the quarter as I learned counselors do not advise and more focus on the therapeutic relationship to help the client grow. I was relieved when I learned counselors do not give advice, but instead help the client find what is best for them. The client is the expert of their own experience. Throughout their work, counselors need to maintain cultural humility – “a lifelong commitment to self-evaluation and critique, to redressing the power imbalances in the [therapist-client] dynamic, and to developing mutually beneficial and non-paternalistic partnerships” (Sajnani, Tomczyk, Bleuer, Dokter, Carr, & Bilodeau, 2016, p. 144). Even if a counselor is working with a client from a similar cultural background, they should practice cultural humility to be reminded that no two people are the same; each person has their own background and their own issues. The counselor’s job is to listen to the client’s struggle and guide the client to find the best solution for their individual struggle. Knowing the client is the expert will help build a strong therapeutic relationship.

I learned that the key to the counseling profession is to build a therapeutic relationship: “a professional relationship involving a good working alliance, trust, honesty, and the promise of confidentiality, which focuses on the needs of the client and involves compensation for the helper” (Young, 2017, p. 321). The therapeutic relationship has been found to be the most influential aspect of the client’s success in counseling (Young, 2017, p. 33). Before this course, I believed choosing the right technique was the important aspect of healing for the client. I am good at creating relationships with people and holding space for others to explore and feel safe. I am not the best at helping people solve their problems by telling them what to do. I gained confidence in my ability to become a counselor after learning that I do not have to give advice and that the key to helping is creating a therapeutic relationship.

## My Role as a Counselor

Throughout the course, I have learned my strengths as a counselor, as well as struggled, discovering the aspects I need to continue growing.

### Growth and Strengths

I have grown many skills throughout this course, as well as embraced my strengths. The most important take-away I have from Communication and Counseling is knowing I have the strength and abilities to become a counselor. Young (2017) explains that self-esteem is separated into efficacy – feeling one can perform a specific task successfully – and self-worth – a general feeling that one is good enough to live in the world (p. 215). I struggle with low self-esteem, both in terms of efficacy and self-worth. However, my efficacy increased through my positive feedback, both from the professor and my observers. At the beginning of class, I was nervous for recording because I worried I would not help the client and possibly say something to harm them. This nervousness decreased as I was encouraged by my professor to speak more in the session and then received positive feedback from others that I was holding space well and connecting to my clients.

I was challenged after the first recording to speak more during my sessions, though I noticed one of my strengths is silence. Young (2017) states one of the problems helpers face is “letting the client talk too long” (p. 114). I had this problem at the beginning of the quarter because I was not confident in what I would say to the client. After being told I have a strength in using silence, I started to be more mindful of the silence I used. I chose to use silence as a tactic, but only when it would be beneficial to the client. I challenged myself to talk more in the sessions and it worked. I can understand the client’s struggle and reflect their feelings back in a way that portrayed my empathy, thus building the therapeutic relationship. Receiving positive feedback from the client in these moments strengthened my self-efficacy as a counselor.

### Struggles

While I am proud of my growth, I struggled with countertransference with clients. I experienced a lot of countertransference with my last two clients. After receiving feedback from my observers, during my second and third sessions, I learned I handled my countertransference well. Through my countertransference, I developed self-care tactics, such as Young’s (2017) suggestions of “becoming a client” and “keep a personal journal” (p. 5), to differentiate my feelings from the struggle of the client. I gave myself enough time to reflect on the sessions and finish my homework in a timely manner. I will need to continue counseling, as most therapists do (Young, 2017, p. 5), to continue to work through countertransference that arises with clients.

However, during my final recording, I was unable to hold back a physical response to the client’s struggle. Two days prior to the session, my husband and I had an argument where he blamed me for the issues in our relationship. While my husband and I talked through this issue, his comment still hurt, and I was trying to work through it. Preparing for the session, I thought I was going to try and focus on empowering the client through what was discussed the previous week, as encouraged by Spangler (personal communication, March 11, 2019). I learned to not have such expectations in the future. The client ended up discussing [something close to what I was working through] in the session, which hurt me again like my husband had, and I was unable to control my automatic response. After my physical response, I lost my confidence for a moment, struggled to find words to express what had happened, and think about what would be best for the client. I am looking forward to having more practice with self-disclosure in future class-practice sessions, which an observer encouraged me to do. The self-disclosure may have helped me in this instance.

### Continued Growth

Through learning my strengths and working through my struggles, I have discovered what skills I need to continue growing during my counseling career: beginning and ending sessions, working through countertransference, and remembering the stages of change. I want to find a way to begin and end the session that is beneficial to the client, specifically if I see the same client multiple times. I gained confidence in beginning and ending sessions with clients I knew I would see once. However, seeing the same client twice was different. I felt I led the client to talk about something that they were not needing in the moment. I also did not quite know what the best way would be to close a session. I hope to have more practice in classes to explore different tactics of opening sessions that are not the first session, as described by Young (2017, p. 73). I also hope to narrow down my closing session tactics, as I believe I used three at the end of my last two sessions.

I learned from my last two sessions that sometimes digging deeper into a client’s struggle may not be beneficial to the client in the moment. Young (2017) suggests using reflective meaning skills to help clients go deeper into their self-understanding (p. 124). In class, we were advised to discuss a level five or six struggles on a scale of one to ten. While my client believed she was talking about a lower level struggle, we dug deeper and realized the struggle was a core issue, level ten. My professor advised to move the client to a lower level issue and focus on the client’s strengths. Due to the therapeutic relationship I had built in the previous session, I was unable to keep the next session at a lower level, as the client was comfortable and wanted to discuss a deeper issue. I hope to be mindful of the time I have with future clients and create techniques to help clients with the time limits I may have.

I also need to be mindful about which stage of change the client is currently in. The five stages of change are precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance (Young, 2017, p. 246). While I may feel as though a client is in the action phase, the client may actually be in the contemplation or maintenance stage. I use this as an example because of my own personal experience in counseling. I thought I was in the action stage; I was making changes in my life and realizing what struggles I had. However, I had not even set goals for myself and the changes I made in my life were small changes in mindset. I was only maintaining the changes I had made with my previous counselor and contemplating other changes I may want to make. If I was unable to realize which stage I was in, I need to be mindful of what stage my clients are in and prepared for.

## Future Counseling Goals

I look forward to applying the skills I learned in Communication and Counseling into my future work as a drama therapist. I challenged myself in my practice sessions to use drama therapy techniques, and the attempt was successful. I see myself continuing to use mindfulness and meditation with clients in the future, as I witnessed their success through my practice sessions. These solution techniques were also suggested by Young (2017, p. 230-232), which surprised me to learn other forms of counseling use these techniques with success. Young (2017) cautions counselors to use techniques that have been researched and found successful (p. 210). I look forward to using meditation and mindfulness techniques with clients I have in the future, knowing they have been influential in other clients.

I began this course not thinking the skills would be beneficial for the work I wanted to do as a drama therapist because I would not be sitting in a chair and doing “talk therapy,” but at the end of class, I realized the skills are important for all aspects of life and should always be used in a drama therapy setting. While I may not use the direct words given in the class examples, the techniques and goals are still the same. Drama therapists should still use Young’s (2017) techniques of reflect content, feeling, and meaning in order to build a therapeutic relationship (p. 124). The use of these tactics may look different in drama therapy, but the goal is still the same. I want to continue holding space for future clients as a counselor, and in order to do this, I need to keep in mind Young’s (2017) communication skills.

## Conclusion

I learned a lot about the counseling profession and myself as a counselor through taking the course Communication and Counseling Skills. I discovered the true building blocks of the profession and gained confidence in my abilities to join the counseling field. I am excited to become a counselor and apply the skills I learned in this class to helping others. Helping skills can be applied to any social interaction, and anyone can benefit from “Learning the Art of Helping” (Young, 2017).
