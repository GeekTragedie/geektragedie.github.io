In today’s American society, many people utilize different forms of technology with screens: tablets, laptop and desktop computers and cell phones. Some college students purchase their text books online and read them on their different devices. Essays and papers are no longer written by hand and many presentations utilize projectors for PowerPoints or Prezis. With this new shift in technology, it bears the question: is there a difference between the ways people absorb, learn, and process information when it is presented to them on a screen versus on paper? In attempts to find an answer to this specific question, my fellow researchers and I decided to conduct a study using a test that explored a specific way the brain processes information on a screen versus on paper. We first looked at previous experiments and studies that were trying to find a difference between screen and paper. We then decided to look at a specific test that had to do with the way people took in information and processed it. By the information we obtained through our literature review, my group and I decided to test the following hypothesis: participants who take the Stroop test on paper are faster and more accurate than those who take it on the computer.

## Literature Review

While doing our literature review, there was no information discovered that showed any previous studies conducting the same experiment as the one we decided to conduct. Due to this fact, we focused our research towards previous experiments or observations that looked at the effects between screen and paper usage. There has been previous experiments that focused mainly on the differences between using a screen and using paper; these experiments were researching different variable along with these two mediums. After researching screen and paper differences, we researched the way to conduct the Stroop test on paper and screen so we could be efficient. We also looked at what the Stroop effect was testing.

### Screen vs. Paper

Richard Picking (1997) in his article “Reading music from screens vs paper” discusses how he conducted an experiment focusing on the way participants read “music text from the computer screen” and compared this “to paper in a laboratory controlled study” (p. 72). His findings showed that the participants “showed a significant preference for animation over paper;” however, “the medium of paper performed better overall than the static screen representation” (Picking, 1997, p. 72). This experiment used 19 subjects and the author discovered that subjects who were using paper performed better than those on a screen. This study helped us see that those using paper were more accurate than those who use screens.

M. Kopper, S. Mayr, and A. Buchner (2016) conducted a study on whether reading from a computer screen or reading from paper makes a difference in general. In this experiment, the proofreading speed and performance was equal for the screen and paper; however, there was more eyestrain on the individuals who used screens (Kopper, Mayr & Buchner, 2016, Abstract). To test this idea of eyestrain, the experimenters extended the amount of time used on a screen and reduced the screen’s luminance, but this did not change any of the results. Repositioning the screens did change the amount of eyestrain and also increased the proofreading speed (Kopper, Mayr & Buchner, 2016, Abstract). Although the results did show an increase with the use of a screen, the experimenters stated that the paper “seemed to enable unimpaired reading without evidence of increased physical strain” which is, overall, better for the participants (Kopper, Mayr & Buchner, 2016, Abstract). Through this information, we learned that doing an activity on a screen creates eyestrain. While the other studies researched still show that learning on paper is better than on screen, this study can give an explanation as to why.

A study conducted by Rakefet Ackerman (2011) looked at the effect of metacognitive regulation of text learning on screen versus on paper. In their research, the participants were given “texts of 1000-1200 words in one of the two media” (Ackerman, 2011, p. 18). They also were given “metacognitive prediction-of-performance judgements with respect to a subsequent multiple-choice test” (Ackerman, 2011, p. 18). While the test performance of those who were given a fixed study time showed no difference between screen and paper, those who were able to regulate their own study time performed worse on with screen than paper (Ackerman, 2011, p. 18). While this may be due to the amount of distractions caused by a computer, this study showed that those who use study on screens are less likely to perform well. This study helped enhance our idea that those who use screens will have a poorer performance.

Through the research previously discussed, our group believes that those who take the Stroop test on a computer screen will be less accurate and slower than those who take it on paper. With this belief, our group came up with the following hypothesis to test: participants who take the Stroop test on paper are faster and more accurate than those who take it on the computer.

### Stroop Effect

Raymond De Young (2014), an Associate Professor of Environmental Psychology and Planning at the University of Michigan, published a website detailing what the Stroop Effect is and what it measures. De Young (2014) states that “the effect is related to the ability of most people to read words more quickly and automatically than they can the name of the color.” Due to this fact, people who are told to read the color of the ink will still read the word written on accident (De Young, 2014, para. 8). The Stroop effect has to do with “the interference that automatic processing of words has on the more mentally effortful task of just naming the ink color” (para. 9). This task is showing how the brain takes longer to perform a task if it cannot simply use what is automatically given.

De Young’s research was based on J. Ridley Stroop’s (1935) original study titled “Studies of Interference in Serial Verbal Reactions.” The reason for the difficulty with the third test is that there is “interference in serial verbal reactions” (Stroop, 1935, p. 659). Stroop (1935) listed the effects of his test of reading the color of the ink:

> 1\. It decreased the interference of conflicting word stimuli to naming colors but did not eliminate it. 2. It produced a practice curve comparable to that obtained in many other learning experiments. 3. It increased the variability of the group. 4. It shortened the reaction time to colors presented in color squares. 5. It increased the interference of conflicting color stimuli upon reading words. (p. 659)

With this definition of the results of the Stroop effect, we were content with knowing this test looked at the way the brain processes information and were confident in choosing this test in our experiment.

## Methods

To test this hypothesis, we performed an experiment with 56 participants (17 male and 39 female) from the University of South Dakota’s Learning and Memory class in the spring of 2016. The participants were all students in their sophomore, junior, or senior year of college. The participants were tested in their Learning and Memory Lab. There were four labs; two labs were given the paper Stroop test while the other two labs were given the screen Stroop test. All participants were asked to fill out information forms that asked the participants about their sex, age, year in college, and ethnicity. Before distributing the information sheets, students were asked if they were color blind. Due to the fact that the Stroop test deals primarily with being able to distinguish between colors, if a student was color blind, they were excused from the study.

### Experimental Breakdown

The students taking the paper version of the test were instructed to fill out the information sheet. The participants were split into groups of two, told to pick one person to participate first, and then asked to take out a device to time themselves. They were then given a packet face-down with three sheets and instructed to not to turn the packet over until instructed to do so. Once everyone was ready we asked participants to read the first page from left to right without being timed. After this, we instructed them to turn to the second page and read the color of the text as their partner timed them and then document how long it took them to get through this task and how many mistakes were made in the process. The participant was then asked to read through the first page again untimed. Following this the participants were asked to read the ink color of the text on the final page while the partner took note of the number of error made and how long it took the participant to fulfill this task. Once this step was finished, the participants were asked to switch partners and were talked through the same process with the second partner.

The participants doing the screen version of the test were ran through a very similar method as the paper group, only had some more specific instructions with the methods of using the computer program. Participants were asked to split into groups of two, take out a timing device, and then told to choose one person to go first. They then logged onto a computer and were asked to open up the Google Chrome browser and zoom their browser to 175%. Next, they were asked to go to the following URL: https://www.math.unt.edu/~tam/SelfTests/StroopEffects.html. Once this website opened up, the participant was asked to scroll to the bottom of the page. Once everyone was caught up, the participants were asked to read the third table given on the website from left to right, untimed. They were then asked to read the color of the text in the first table on the website while being timed and judged on their accuracy. Following this, the participants were asked to read the third table again untimed. After this, the participants were to be timed and marked for accuracy while reading the color of the text in the second table. Once this task was fulfilled, the participants were asked to switch partners and were walked through the process again with the second partner.

### Statistic Methodology

We then took the data accumulated and used the following formulas to analyze the information: S = √[Σ(x − M)<sup>2</sup> / (N − 1)] and t = (M − μ) / S<sub>m</sub>. S stands for standard deviation while t stands for a standard t-test. The significance level used in this experiment was a 0.05 significance level. After finding our data, the numbers were then used to find the bell curve for both the amount of time taken by participants. The information was then analyzed.

## Data

Using the data taken, we found two things: the mean and standard deviation. Using this information, we then ran a t-test with a significance level of 0.05. It is important to note the amount of participants in each group; the number or participants who performed the paper test is 31 and online is 25.

### Means and Standard Deviations

When looking at the amount of time it took for participants to take the test, test one had an M = 16.23 seconds and an S = 1.19 seconds. The online test 1 had an M = 18.6 seconds and an S = 3.64 seconds. This shows that it took more seconds to perform the online test and the online test was more spread out. For test 2, paper participants had an M = 38.42 seconds and an S = 1.09 seconds. The online participants had an M = 37.08 seconds and an S = 6.04. This shows that those who did the test on paper were a little slower than those who took it online; however those who did the online test were more varied than those on paper. When looking at the amount of errors performed by participants, neither of the groups had any errors during the first test. During the second test, the M of errors for those taking the paper test was equal to .85 words and the S = 1.09 words. Looking at the data from the online test, M = .78 words and S = .19 words. This indicates that the people who took the test on paper made more errors and were more varied on said errors than those who took the test online.

### t-Test

Using the data drawn from our experiment we found that in paper test 1, t = -0.626; in online test 1, t = 0.324. Paper test 2 found the following information: t = 0.569 for time and t = 0.25 for errors. Online test 2 found t = -0.119 for time and t = -0.21 for errors. These tests were ran using a significance level of 0.05.

## Analysis and Discussion

The information provided did not support our hypothesis. We believed that the paper test would be faster and more accurate than the online test. The data given shows that the online test was actually faster than the paper test by 1.34 seconds. Looking at the errors, the paper test actually also had more errors than the online test when analyzing the harder test, test two. Both of these tests, however, were not found to be statistically significant on any level and, therefore, is not particularly important when compared to the population.

## Conclusion and Future Study

Overall, my research team and I conducted a study based on the hypothesis that participants who take the Stroop test on paper are faster and more accurate than those who take it on the computer. Those who took the test on paper were found to be slower than those who took it on the computer and those who took the test online were more accurate than those who took it on paper. This is the opposite of what we had hypothesized. None of this information was statistically significant and there could have been outside effects impacting our results. For future study on this phenomenon, I would be more consistent with the people composing the experiment. I would also do a double sided t-test to see if there is any change between screen and paper at all. When looking at working memory, maybe to further research we could look at different tests of working memory. It would probably be more beneficial to have an equal amount of people in each group and have a larger amount of participants. I think this would be an interesting study to continue looking into.
