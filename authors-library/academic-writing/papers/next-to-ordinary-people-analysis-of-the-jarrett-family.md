I witness a family of three taking a picture together; the photographer asks the teenage son to move between his parents. The son grimaces and moves towards his mother. After the photo, the father asks to take a picture of the mother and son. The mother objects, but the father continually insists, so the mother asks to take a different picture as the father fumbles with the camera. The son listens to his mother trying to avoid this picture being taken repeatedly until he explodes with anger from this constant rejection. This describes a scene from Redford’s (1980) film *Ordinary People*. In order to understand this short interaction of taking family pictures, one needs to know the family’s background. In this paper, I systemically analyze the fictional Jarrett family in the movie *Ordinary People* to include a background of the family system, analysis of the mother-son dyad, and treating this dyad using Bowenian theory. While this paper uses Bowenian theory, it finishes with a discussion of my preferred method of systemic theory approaches.

## Background

Before addressing treatment strategies for the Jarrett family, it is important to understand the individual members of the family and the system’s structure and process. In the following section, I view the Jarrett family members from a multicultural lens using Blake’s (2006) Addressing Model. I also take into consideration the individual’s strengths and outside support systems. After discussing individuals, I analyze the family’s structure, process, and presenting problem from a cybernetic standpoint.

### Individual Family Members

**Calvin.** The father of the Jarrett family system is a heterosexual, cisgender male of upper middle-class socioeconomic status and is employed as a tax attorney. Calvin has experienced trauma; he suffered the loss of his eldest son, Buck, and found his son, Conrad, attempting suicide. Calvin is a hardworking father and attorney whose strengths include emotional expression, reaching out to understand others, and caring about his family members. His outside support system is a friend from work, and I am curious as to his family of origin.

**Beth.** Beth, married to Calvin, is the mother of the Jarrett family system. She is a heterosexual, cisgender female of upper middle-class socioeconomic status. She too has experienced trauma from her sons’ experiences. I believe Beth is unemployed and keeps herself busy, running errands for the family and upholding her social agenda. Gender roles in a family tend to be passed down through the generations (McGoldrick, 1995, p. 53), which seems to be the case when observing Beth and her mother taking on society’s expected female caretaking role in the family. Beth has taken on the responsibility for housework, cooking, and shopping. She also cares for the social status of the family and saving face.

Beth is particular in the way she expresses herself and what she shares with others. She appreciates family privacy regarding the trauma she experienced regarding her two sons. Her strengths consist of specific coping mechanisms, self-control, organization, and loyalty. Beth has surrounded herself with multiple support systems, such as her friends, her parents, and her brother (Ward) and his wife (Audrey).

**Buck.** Buck was the older son of the Jarrett family who passed away during a boating accident. Buck had a close relationship with his mother and a poor relationship with father, who pushed him to succeed. He was a star athlete on his high school’s swim team, evidenced by multiple athletic awards. His support systems included his friends, swim mates, and swim coach.

**Conrad.** The younger son in the family, Conrad is a teenage heterosexual, cisgender male of upper middle-class socioeconomic status who does not believe in God. He experienced trauma, as he was with Buck during the boating accident; he is also currently grieving the loss of a friend due to suicide, Karen. Conrad attempted suicide and spent time at an inpatient hospital, where he met Karen, for four months where he experienced electroshock therapy but did not leave continuing any medications. He experiences nightmares causing sleeping problems. Due to his time in the hospital, Conrad is retaking his junior year of high school where he was on the swim team. Before the hospitalization, he was a straight-a student, though may be currently struggling academically.

Conrad has many strengths to be incorporated into therapy. He has developed coping mechanisms to get through the day and has self-control enough to not attempt suicide again, showing immense courage. He can view life’s silver linings, knows when to reach out in times of need, and is willing to work on his emotional expression. Outside of the family, his support systems consist of his friend (Dicky), girlfriend (Jeannie), previous swim coach, and individual psychologist (Dr. Berger). I am curious as to other school-related supports he may have and his relationship with his grandparents.

### Family Structure and Process

The Jarrett family structure has specific family rules and relationship boundaries that control the way the family members interact with one another. Overt rules are verbally stated to family members (Shamoon, 2019). The Jarrett family overt rules include keeping your room clean, brushing your teeth, telling others in the system where you are, and not lying. Families also have covert rules, which are not directly said, but understood by the family members (Shamoon, 2019). Covert rules in the Jarrett family include, do not talk about Buck or the funeral, do not talk about family issues with people outside of the family, do not talk about being in therapy, and do not express your emotions.

Beth and Calvin have an enmeshed couple relationship with diffused boundaries. According to structural theory, relationships with diffused boundaries tend to become enmeshed; these types of boundaries do not allow individualism between the members, demand loyalty, and is threatened by disagreements (Gehart, 2018, p. 137). The individuals in this dyad have difficulty identifying themselves outside of their relationship. They spend most of their time together and are threatened by their arguments and disagreements regarding Conrad. The dyad cannot see their couple relationship separate from their parenting relationship.

Beth has close relationships with her mother and her brother with clear boundaries. These boundary types are healthy and allow room for personal identity (Gehart, 2018). Beth is able to converse with her mother about family matters and can trust her brother to unjudgementally and supportively spend time golfing with her. Beth understands her role and the others’ roles in these relationships.

Beth had a close relationship with Buck, unlike Calvin’s poor relationship with Buck. Based on family discussion and flashbacks, Beth identified strongly with Buck. She viewed him as strong and capable. She was able to converse and laugh with Buck, in contrast to Calvin. Conrad states that Calvin would yell at Buck and push him to succeed. Conrad also states that Calvin would ridicule the sons for ruining their boat during the accident. I am curious what Conrad and Buck’s relationship was like.

Calvin has a normal relationship with Conrad with clear boundaries in contrast to Beth’s conflicting relationship and rigid boundaries with Conrad. Calvin inquires about Conrad’s health, and Conrad feels open to discussing his mental health and emotions with Calvin, evidence of clear boundaries. Beth and Calvin’s relationship is rigid. Rigid boundaries allow independence, emotional disconnect, few repercussions, and “have excessive tolerance for deviation” (Gehart, 2018, p. 137). This dyad rarely interacts with each other, only talks about facts, and lacks consequences for not following the rules of their relationship.

**Positive feedback loop.** The nuclear family has a particular positive feedback loop to keep their family at homeostasis. A family’s homeostasis “refers to the unique set of behavioral, emotional, and interactional norms that create stability for the family” (Gehart, 2018, p. 48) and a positive feedback loop includes behaviors that can lead to a new homeostasis, however negative feedback returns a family back to their original homeostasis (p. 49). The Jarrett family starts their loop at homeostasis, which includes a lack of communication and emotional expression regarding past experiences. Conrad provides evidence of this homeostasis when he says he misses the hospital because “no one hid anything in the hospital.” Beth also asks Calvin to not discuss Buck’s funeral. Conrad provides positive feedback by breaking family rules: not eating, quitting the swim team, talking about Buck, and not telling his parents where he is. Beth reacts to these actions by yelling or becoming frustrated, followed by Conrad withdrawing from conversation. Calvin goes to Conrad to try to understand Conrad’s point of view. When understanding is not met, Calvin withdraws from Conrad, providing negative feedback. Calvin returns to Beth who also does not talk about the situation and yells at Calvin for siding with Conrad. Negative feedback continues as Beth withdraws from Calvin, neglecting to talk about the issue or emote emotion. This final step brings the family back to homeostasis of not communicating or expressing emotions.

**Misguided solutions.** The family has attempted to solve the presenting problem, though these attempted solutions have failed. Strategic family therapy identifies more-of-the-same solutioning which are solutions family members have tried multiple times, but they only make the presenting problem worse (Gehart, 2018, p. 95). When Beth and Conrad are in an argument, neither of the two discuss the problem or how they feel about what is bothering them, and the two withdraw from each other. These moments are examples of “terrible simplifications” which is when “action is necessary but none is taken” (Gehart, p. 95). After the verbal argument regarding Conrad quitting the swim team, neither Conrad or Beth apologize or discuss why they were angry at each other. Taking action in this moment may have been necessary.

Beth has an expectation for Conrad to feel better and act normal, which is another example of more-of-the-same solution. Paradox solutioning is when “action is taken at the wrong level” and people tend to “demand attitude or personality changes and are not content with behavioral changes” (Gehart, 2018, pp. 95-96). After the hospital, Beth and Beth’s mother expected Conrad to have been better than he presented. Beth states her disappointment about Conrad’s behaviors saying that “he provokes people” and “people don’t want to be with him.” Beth’s expectations of Conrad changing are unreasonable and not at the correct level of change.

## Client System

When treating the Jarrett family, I would start therapy by treating a parent-child subsystem first before treating the entire family. In structural family therapy, subsystems are small units that make up the entire family system, such as couples, parents, or siblings (Gehart, 2018, p. 144). The subsystem to be addressed in this critical application analysis is the parent-child dyad between Beth and Conrad. Due to Conrad’s history of attempting suicide, I believe it is crucial to ensure he is in therapy. According to McGoldrick (1995), parent-child relationship are difficult to navigate, and this specific relationship “is the most difficult one [parents] will know” (p. 173), and Western culture “often prescribe distance between… mothers and sons” (p. 174). With this information in mind, I believe addressing the mother-son relationship in this family is important.

I also chose this particular subsystem because, as stated before, it is conflictual with rigid boundaries. These two have created coping mechanisms for their anxiety that is creating issues elsewhere in the family system. This dyad is important to address due to the constant triangulation, lack of differentiation causing projection, and evidence of emotional cutoff.

## Bowenian Treatment Approach

Many systemic approaches can be beneficial for this family subsystem, but I am taking the Bowenian approach. Issues key to this dyad are best explained through Bowen’s theories and the interventions appear applicable and approachable to Beth and Calvin.

### Defining the Problem

**Triangulation.** Both Beth and Conrad turn to Calvin to discuss their issues with each other, triangulating Calvin. Brown (1999) defines triangulation as a dyad relationship using a third person or activity to regulate the anxiety in their relationship. According to McGoldrick (1995), “the classic parent-child triangle involves the child or children siding with one parent, while the other is the outsider” (p. 174). In the Jarrett family, Beth is the outsider, as Conrad identifies with Calvin often during times of family arguments. Conrad tells his father to talk to Beth for him: “why don’t you tell her to stop it,” “she hates me, don’t you see that,” and “Tell her I’m sorry. I can’t talk to her, because it doesn’t change anything. It doesn’t change the way she looks at me.” Beth tells Calvin that Conrad is manipulative and demanding attention. She also believes Calvin is constantly siding with and thinking about Conrad. These triangles continue the problem in the family, as Beth and Conrad do not talk about their issues to each other, they bring in a third party to dissolve their anxiety towards each other.

**Low differentiation and projection.** There is low differentiation in Beth as she defines her role and self through others and suppresses her own emotions to maintain the family’s homeostasis. Differentiation is a continuum of “a person’s ability to separate intrapersonal and interpersonal distress… also described… as the ability to balance two life forces: the need for *togetherness* and the need for *autonomy*” (Gehart, 2018, p. 265). Beth reacts to Conrad’s behavior with anger and frustration instead of responding or attempting to understand. When Ward and Audrey tell Beth they want her to be happy, Beth becomes angry and denies that she is able to be happy due to the loss of her son. Beth also talks often about escaping reality by traveling to Europe, London, and Houston or going to the movies and the theatre. Instead of facing her emotions and what she wants, she attempts to separate from them.

Beth’s lack of differentiation creates a projection of family’s issues onto Conrad, who in turn has become symptomatic. According to Gehart (2018), “parents ‘project’ their immaturity onto one or more children, causing decreased differentiation in subsequent generations” (p. 270). Brown (1999) adds that a child developing behavioral or emotional problems is a sign of low differentiation being projected onto a child (p. 96). Conrad shows signs of emotional problems though his attempted suicide and nightmares. He also shows signs of low differentiation as he allows other people to define who he is: Beth, the hated child; Calvin, the invisible child; Dr. Berger, another client; and friends, the crazy one. These labels are barriers for Conrad understanding and expressing his own feelings; though he has a breakthrough in therapy when he is able to express his anger and shame resulting from Buck’s death. Without Beth approaching her low differentiation, it will be difficult for Conrad to be mentally and emotionally healthy.

**Emotional cutoff.** Beth has withdrawn in the parent-child dyad enough that it has created an emotional cutoff. Emotional cutoffs occur when “a person no longer emotionally engages with another in order to manage anxiety; this usually occurs between children and parents” (Gehart, 2018, p. 270). During a conversation with Dr. Berger, Conrad states that his relationship with his mother is only “surface level.” Due to the family’s positive feedback loop and lack of emotional expression, Beth has emotionally cutoff Conrad, evidenced by two of Conrad’s attempts for connection. Conrad tried to talk with his mother about a memory with Buck, but Beth diverted the conversation to talking about the neighbor’s dog. Conrad also hugged his mother and received no emotional response.

### Role of the Therapist

This dyad subsystem may benefit from the therapist using second-order cybernetics. In this type of cybernetics, the therapist becomes part of the family system and is able to influence change by this role (Gehart, 2018, p. 53). Beth and Conrad have difficulties talking to each other, so having them start therapy by talking to me can be beneficial while their relationship begins to heal. As the dyad becomes more capable of talking with each other, I can decrease my role in the system and direct the conversations to be between the clients.

### Treatment Goals

**Address chronic anxiety.** One treatment goal for the Jarrett family is to address their chronic anxiety resulting from the Buck’s death and Conrad’s suicide attempt. Gehart (2018) says that chronic anxiety arises in the family if members do not deal with their emotional reactions after a crisis, loss, or conflict (p. 268). McGoldrick (1995) addresses loss in a family and says people carry images “of funerals and of the death” with them as “part of [their] heritage and identity” (p. 127). Beth and Conrad have not been able to address their emotions regarding the death of Buck and his death is being carried around with the individuals wherever they go. As their therapist, I want to decrease this anxiety and encourage the dyad to think about and discuss their emotions about this loss.

**Increase differentiation.** Another treatment goal for the parent-child dyad is to increase differentiation in the individuals. Increasing differentiation will help the individuals address their emotions and define who they believe they are. Exploring themselves will allow them to interact with others in positive ways while maintaining their sense of self.

### Interventions

**Emotional cutoff.** An intervention that would benefit this subsystem is decreasing the emotional cutoff between Beth and Conrad. I hope to address the emotional cutoff to help treat the chronic anxiety and increase differentiation. I would ask Beth and Conrad to talk about their emotions regarding each other, the death of Buck, and Conrad’s suicide attempt. These questions can help the individuals differentiate as they are considering their own feelings instead of someone else’s. This dyad can benefit from a therapist witnessing their conversations to help prevent their relationship from remaining, as Conrad states, “surface level.”

**Detriangulation.** Decreasing the emotional cutoff will also lead to the detriangulation of Calvin in the family system. To detriangulate this dyad, it may be beneficial to invite Calvin into one therapy session to ask him about the triangulation that happens in the family system. Asking the head of the triangle can help me understand the situation outside of the therapy sessions. Brown (1999) says the first step to detriangling is to help a person “recognise both subtle and the more obvious ways they are ‘triangled’ by others” (p. 99). Calvin can be an asset to the therapy if he can recognize that he is being triangulated and is able to regulate his triangulation while Conrad and Beth work on detriangulating.

After understanding the triangles, I would bring the triangulation to the attention of Beth and Conrad in hopes that they will work on communicating directly with each other instead of turning to Calvin. Brown (1999) recommends that family members notice their triangulation and “plan ways of communicating” with each other to detriangulate (p. 99). I can use second-order cybernetics to help the family learn ways to detriangulate by having the family members talk to me about each other and then turn these items back to the other person. For example, I can welcome Conrad to say “my mother hates me” but then will request Conrad address this directly to Beth. Another example can be Beth telling me that Conrad manipulates the family for attention. I can ask Beth why she feels this way and then ask that she tell Conrad this concern, both addressing emotional cutoff and detriangulation.

### Change Predictions

I predict second-order change will be key to the Jarrett family’s systemic health. Second-order change happens “when a system restructures its homeostasis in response to positive feedback and the rules that govern the system fundamentally shift” (Gehart, 2018, p. 49). Through addressing chronic anxiety, increasing differentiation, and detriangulation, the family will need to function differently. Their overt and covert rules will change to create a new form of homeostasis that includes increased communication and emotional expression. Talking about family stressors should become more regular and expected in the family system.

## Preferred Theoretical Orientation

Applying Bowen’s model to the Jarrett family helped me grow more appreciation for the theory, though I still do not feel it is my preferred theoretical orientation. While I was very interested in narrative therapy, I need to learn more about it to comfortably say it is my preferred approach orientation. For [GeekTragedie] theory, I would like to combine strategic and structural theory together.

### Strategic Theory

I am very drawn to strategic theory because I see it as unique and creative; I have an appreciation for trusting the system and restructuring communication processes. Gehart (2018) states, “systemic therapists respect the family as a system… They have a deep, abiding trust that the system can reorganize itself without the therapist forcing change” (p. 91). I find beauty in this deep level of trust and respect for the family system. Part of being a therapist is creating relationships with clients. Trust is an important factor in relationships, so I appreciate that it is a key aspect of this theory.

As a drama therapist, I had fun thinking of new ways to change the process of communication between members of a system. Ross’s (1988) article discusses a woman who starts to act like Lucy Ricardo at home to restructure her family. Gehart (2018) gives the example of a family needing to move their argument into the bathroom; she also has the reader imagine a couple set up their bedroom like a court room in order to argue. I am drawn to this technique because these examples are fascinating, evidence based, and creative.

### Structural Theory

I am drawn to structural theory’s reframing and enactment interventions. Reframing can help a person view a situation from a different perspective (Hannah & Brown, 2019). Part of the reframing process is thinking creatively about the issue at hand. Two types of reframing are emphasizing strengths and using metaphors (Hannah & Brown, 2019). In my work as a future counselor, I want to be able to look at the strengths in my clients and help them view the world through these strengths. I also will be using a lot of metaphors in my work as a drama therapist, so reframing is going to be an important skill for me to use.

As a future drama therapist, it is not surprising that I am drawn to enactments: “the therapist prompts the family to reenact a conflict or other interaction” in the therapy session (Gehart, 2018, p. 138). I feel practice in a different environment can be beneficial for any circumstance. The best way to learn a skill is to apply it, and enactments allow clients to practice what you are asking them to do outside of the therapy room. Enactments also allow me to learn how the couple interacts, which can give clues to how I can approach treatment with the clients.

### [GeekTragedie] Theory

I have a unique worldview that will influence my future role as a therapist and my preferred theoretical orientation. In terms of my role, I feel most comfortable using first-order cybernetics, where the therapist does not become part of the family system in treatment (Gehart, 2018, p. 53). Drama therapist Johnson (n.d.) discusses the different roles a therapist can take during a drama therapy session. My style aligns most with being a “leader,” which Johnson (n.d.) describes as a therapist leading exercises, though may join the group at various times by “stand[ing] in the circle, crawl[ing] on the floor, and tak[ing] on a role in the story. Nevertheless, the leader remains somewhat removed from the total participation and attempts to keep the focus on the clients’ experiences” (p. 115). While I would like to stay in this role, I know I will have to take on others for the benefit of my clients, but I find comfort knowing my personal preference.

In the leader role, I firmly believe my think-positive, silver lining attitude will be noticed through incorporating my trust in the system and reframing ideas. I am a relationship-based person and find hope in noticing strengths. I want to bring these aspects into my future therapy sessions to benefit my clients and increase hope and motivation. This mentality can also help me develop strong relationships with my clients, as they will have someone supporting them.

With my leader role and positive outlook, I hope to incorporate the systemic items I discussed above from strategic and structural theory. I am interested in combining the idea of interrupting the process and enactments. It would be beneficial for clients to practice modifying their process for communication in the therapy room. If a couple is arguing about finances, I would like to put them into a scene where they can still argue about the finances but change the way in which they argue. For example, the couple argues in different roles, like an elderly couple, or they argue in poetry form or they pretend they are on a beach. I would enjoy being creative with the couple to see what ideas they can come up with and relate to. As stated before, I believe bringing these techniques into the therapy session will be beneficial practice.

## Conclusion

The goal of this paper was to approach the problems faced by the Jarrett family in the movie *Ordinary People* in a systemic manner. I discussed the background of the family, including the family structure and processes. When starting therapy for this family, I would approach the parent-child subsystem between Beth and Conrad first. Through the lens of Bowenian theory, this dyad struggles with triangulation, low differentiation, projection, and emotional cutoff. To approach these issues, I would address the chronic anxiety and attempt to increase differentiation through decreasing the emotional cutoff and detriangulation. While I used the Bowenian approach in this paper, I do not feel this theory is my preferred theoretical orientation. I want to approach therapy through a combination of strategic and structural therapy methods. With my systemic base of knowledge, I hope [GeekTragedie] Theory can empower future clients and allow for a healing therapeutic relationship.
