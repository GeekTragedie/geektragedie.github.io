<p class="paper-title">Acting Gay with AIDS in America</p>

Acquired immune deficiency syndrome, more commonly known as AIDS, is a wide-spread problem in today’s society.  In 2011, there were 34 million people around the world that were diagnosed with AIDS (AVERTing HIV and AIDS (AVERT)).  In the United States alone, more than 1.2 million people have HIV/AIDS and one in seven are not even aware that they have it (U.S.  Department of Health & Human Services).  HIV/AIDS is known to have a more serious impact on “gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men” (U.S.  Department).  Some men struggle with the idea of being gay and believe that society does not see gay men as men who have power.  This is particularly true during the time of Ronald Reagan’s presidency when AIDS became a huge problem in the United States.  However, Reagan’s action, or lack thereof, caused gay men during his presidency to question whether or not they should embrace their disease or not (San Francisco AIDS Foundation (SFAF)).  The United States has a stigma towards people with AIDS.  This stigma along with Reagan’s presidency causes gay men during this time to act differently.  Through Tony Kushner’s play, *Angels in America:* *A Gay Fantasia on National Themes*, one can see how Ronald Reagan’s presidency and America’s stigma towards AIDS influenced gay men’s actions during this time period and this caused them to perform in certain ways.

AIDS is not a disease that one can simply hide the fact that they have it the entire time.  It is not a disease that one can take pills to get rid of.  It is not a disease that one can live a normal life while having.  In order to have AIDS, one must have HIV.  There are four different stages people with HIV/AIDS go through and each stage makes the disease more noticeable to society.  The first stage the victim may have fevers, swollen glands, rashes, muscle pain, and/or headaches (U.S.  Department).  The next stage, called the clinical latency stage, is where the person may not show any symptoms at all, but as the disease spreads and becomes worse, there are many physical symptoms that arise (U.S.  Department).  The person may have rapid weight loss, extreme tiredness, memory loss, prolonged swelling, diarrhea, and/or “red, brown, pink or purplish blotches on or under the skin or inside the mouth, nose, or eyelids (U.S.  Department).  The final stage of HIV, which many people may or may not reach, is AIDS (SFAF).  AIDS is when someone has HIV and also has “a very low count of CD4 cells.  CD4 cells are part of the immune system, and are attacked/destroyed by HIV” (SFAF).  These are the three stages once someone has AIDS; however, there is always the stage or process of getting HIV.

AIDS/HIV can be contracted through the following bodily fluids: blood, semen, vaginal secretion, and breast milk (SFAF).  One can come into contact with these things through sharing needles, touching others’ blood, breast feeding, and sexual contact (vaginal, anal, or oral) (SFAF).  Once one is contracted with HIV/AIDS, it cannot be cured.  “There is no procedure or medication which has been scientifically proven to reliably eliminate the virus from a person’s body or reverse the damage to the immune system” (SFAF).  Some peoples’ bodies take to the disease and better than others, but “gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (MSM)… are most seriously affected by HIV” (U.S.  Department).  A 2011 study showed that “MSM accounted for 65 percent of new HIV infections” (AVERT).  This disease is a well-known problem in the United States, but why has it become such a problem and what is its history?
AIDS dates back in medical journals to 1981, the first year Ronald Reagan was president (SFAF).  By the end of that year, “there [was] a cumulative total of 270 reported cases of severe immune deficiency among gay men, and 121 of those individuals [had] died” (U.S.  Department).  The following year the first AIDS clinic was created in San Francisco and in the year after that the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention created an AIDS Hotline for the public to call and gain information about the disease.  In 1985 the US Congress distributed 70 million dollars for AIDS research and it was not until September of that year that President Ronald Reagan finally mentioned AIDS as being a priority in a letter to congress (U.S.  Department).  It was not until May of 1987 that Reagan publicly addressed AIDS and made it a Presidential Commission (U.S.  Department).  By the time Reagan had addressed AIDS publicly, twenty-one thousand Americans had already died from the disease (SFAF).

It surprises people to learn that there were close friends of the Reagan family that were well-known AIDS activists.  Actress and friend of the Reagan family, Elizabeth Taylor, is the founder and large supporter of the Elizabeth Taylor AIDS Foundation, created in 1991.  She organized the first AIDS fundraiser in 1984 (Taylor).  Another friend of the Reagan family, Rock Hudson, actually died of AIDS in 1985 (SFAF & Taylor).  Despite one of his close friends dying of AIDS, Reagan did not discuss AIDS until two years after Hudson’s death.

Reagan’s lack of action has been disputed; some take it as Reagan only taking the advice of his advisor, Gary Bauer, but most take Reagan’s lack of action as careless and inconsiderate (Cannon).  Lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender (LGBT) activist and playwright Larry Kramer, takes the latter side and was quoted by Carl Cannon as saying:

> Ronald Reagan may have done laudable things but he was also a monster and, in my estimation, responsible for more deaths than Adolf Hitler… He is one of the persons most responsible for allowing the plague of AIDS to grow from 41 cases in 1981 to over 70 million today.  He refused to even say the word out loud for the first seven years of his presidency and when he did speak about it, it was with disdain.  (Cannon)

This poor time during Reagan’s presidency is not addressed as often by society because Reagan was seen as a great president.  His neglect of talking about AIDS can be overlooked, as some might say, because “anyone willing to engage in a public discourse about AIDS does so at his or her own risk” (Piggford).  Whether or not this is due to Reagan’s presidency is unknown.  Reagan “promised to cut the budget, reduce taxes, trim bureaucracy and revive America” (Troy).  Reagan was able to put the economy back on its feet after the Great Depression and wanted to rid of the idea of a “Big Government” (Troy).  This helped Americans embrace individuality and the idea of a larger America.  Reagan was seen as such a great president that when Barack Obama ran for president in 2008, he claimed that he wanted to “replicate the Reagan Revolution’s impact” (Troy).  To try and make up for lost time, President Reagan has been quoted saying, “We mustn’t allow those with the AIDS virus to suffer discrimination… We must firmly oppose discrimination against those who have AIDS.  We must prevent the persecution, through ignorance or malice, of our fellow citizens” (Bowen).  However, actions speak louder than words.  Because Reagan was seen as such a great president, outside of the world of AIDS, this only reinforced how Americans viewed AIDS.  By Reagan not acknowledging the AIDS epidemic early on in its process, Americans followed suit with his beliefs and ideas and this helped cause a stigma towards AIDS.

While many groups in the United States are trying to educate the population about AIDS, many people still feel uncomfortable with and around people who actually have the disease (AVERT).  “The National HIV/AIDS Strategy admits ‘addressing stigma and discrimination is perhaps the biggest challenge we face’” (AVERT).  According to a United States 2011 survey, “45 percent [of people were] uncomfortable if their food was prepared by someone HIV-positive” (AVERT).  This is one example of the HIV/AIDS stigma in society.  Due to the fact that AIDS is not transferable through anything except the previously listed ways, it is not possible for people to get AIDS through food.  AIDS has been around for many years, so the disease itself is not new information.  By society’s stigma to AIDS and MSM, it becomes less surprising that gay men feel as though they need to act or perform a certain way.

Being gay brings its own stigma and prejudice onto a person.  In 1991, four years after Ronald Reagan spoke publicly about AIDS, a professor in psychology at the University of California at Davis published an article titled “Stigma, Prejudice, and Violence Against Lesbians and Gay Men” (Herek).  In this article, Herek discusses the different attitudes heterosexuals in the United States have towards gay men and women.  Herek states that “roughly two-thirds of Americans condemn homosexuality or homosexual behavior as morally wrong or a sin” (Herek 60).  After a national survey of 113 lesbians and 287 gay men, Herek discovered “5% of the men and 10% of the women reported having been physically abused or assaulted in the previous year because they were gay.  Nearly half reported experiencing some form of discrimination” (60).  Due to these findings, it is no wonder why gay and lesbian people would want to hide their sexuality from those around them.  Americans should have the freedom to do as they please.  Gay people need to be able to pursue their happiness, like the rest of society does.

One of the most recent discriminations against gay people was when Indiana Governor Mike Pence passed a law “that would allow businesses in the state to cite religious objections to refuse to serve gay people” (Neuman).  This law outraged many protestors, not only in Indiana, but from around the United States.  A group of about three thousand protestors gathered outside the Indiana Statehouse chanting, “Pence must go!” and holding signs that read “No hate in our state” (Neuman).  Many people believe this gives business owners the legal right to discriminate against gay and lesbian people.  The Governor Pence “declined to answer the question” to whether he feels this is true or not (Neuman).  Not only did discrimination happen to gay and lesbian Americans during Reagan’s presidency, but it is also happening today.  These political acts in government really sway the way society feels towards different minority groups, like gay men and women.  This makes minority groups feel as though they need to act in certain ways.  
Tony Kushner’s play, *Angels in America: A Gay Fantasia on National Themes*, demonstrates how these stigmas affect gay men and make them act a certain way.  There are multiple examples in this play of how the characters perform gay.  This can be seen through Joe’s actions and beliefs.  Joe does not consider himself gay; however, he is mistaken for it.  This mishap happens because he stays in a bathroom while another man, Louis, is crying.  Louis is glad Joe stays and says, “the other just opened the door, saw me, and fled.  I hope they had to pee real bad” (Kushner, 1477).  He proceeds to call those who fled, “Reaganite heartless macho asshole lawyers” (1477).  Joe claims that he is not being fair and that he voted for Reagan, only to be labeled by Louis as “A Gay Republican” in disbelief (1477).  Because Joe stays and by the way that he sounds, Louis mistakes him for being gay.  Although this later becomes true, this becomes a key component in how the play demonstrates the performance of gay.

To some people’s belief, one can sound gay by sounding more feminine.  Some things are determined by what they are not (Leonard).  For example, one knows the color orange because it is not any other color, red, blue, etc.  Therefore, feminine is determined by the fact that it is not masculine.  Masculine men are perceived as strong, powerful, and unemotional.  So, when Louis perceives Joe as gay, he is looking at his personality for what it is not.  Joe does not sound masculine and is not unemotional, therefore, is not masculine, making him feminine.

Louis’ observation makes Joe question his sexuality and when he goes home and is questioned by Harper of his sexuality (Kushner, 1481-82).  Later he finally comes to terms with his sexuality and realizes that he has been hiding it from himself and calls his mother to tell her the news (1503).  This call plays a large role in how being gay is performed.  The Mormon Church, as Harper tells Prior, “[doesn’t] believe in homosexuals” (1479).  When Joe tells his mother Hannah, “I’m a homosexual” it is a huge deal, specifically because his mother is also Mormon.  By others not accepting Joe being gay, this affects the way Joe feels as though he is supposed to behave.  Hannah tells Joe that they “will just forget [that] phone call” (1503).  Joe is expected to not follow his own sexuality and follow the Mormon religion.  This affects his performance of gay because he is not supposed to be gay at all.  This makes him hide the fact that he is, or at least society is trying to make him hide this fact.

There are also examples in this play of how the characters view AIDS and how the stigmas and symptoms affect the individuals.  Roy, a very popular, powerful lawyer, is diagnosed with AIDS.  Because most people who get AIDS are MSM, Roy believes that, by telling society that he has AIDS, he will no longer be a man of power.  He will be looked upon as gay and, therefore, be feminine.  When Henry breaks the news to Roy that he has AIDS, Roy responds with:

> Homosexuals are not men who sleep with other men.  Homosexuals are men who in fifteen years of trying cannot get a puissant antidiscrimination bill through City Council.  Homosexuals are men who know nobody and who nobody knows… AIDS is what homosexuals have.  I have liver cancer.  (1487)

Through these lines, it is obvious that Roy does not want to look to the world as though he has AIDS.  However, the symptoms are not easy to hide, as previously stated.  Roy is worried that others will find out he has AIDS and label him as a homosexual, adding the stigma of both AIDS and gay onto himself.  Roy does not believe homosexuals have power, so he refuses to consider himself as gay.  In other words, being weak is feminine.  Roy is not strong and not feminine, so he cannot be homosexual.  These two ideas are not incompatible.  Women can be strong and powerful, but to society, gay men are not strong enough to do things, such as be a lawyer.

Roy’s performance of gay is to hide the fact that he is, from himself and the world, so that he can stay powerful.  Society puts this label on men who are gay as being more feminine, and this is the reason.  This causes Roy to continue having sex with men, but refuses to let him admit to himself and others that he is gay to prevent the stigma that comes with it.  AIDS is considered a syndrome that only gay men have, because AIDS comes with this label, Roy refuses to accept the fact that he has AIDS as well.  This is to prevent the stigma of both being gay and having AIDS cause.  This is why, if anyone were to ask what Roy is sick with, he has liver cancer.

This particular play takes place from October 1985 to January 1986, during the time Ronald Reagan was president, but before Reagan had publicly commented about AIDS.  The play itself refers to Reagan many times, making the anger gay men and AIDS activists had more prominent.  At first Reagan is brought up on good terms when Joe says, “America has rediscovered itself...  The truth restored.  Law restored.  That’s what President Reagan’s done… He says ‘Truth exists and can be spoken proudly.’ And the country responds to him.  We become better” (1475).  Joe, performing the Mormon, married Joe that does not know his sexuality, is the one remarking this.  He has not yet reached a point in his life that he is confused with his sexuality and has not seen the horror AIDS creates.

The next instance Reagan is brought up in the play is when Louis talks sarcastically about lawyers, calling them “Reaganite heartless macho asshole lawyers” (1477).  By linking these adjectives together to describe lawyers, Louis puts Reagan in a different light.  The audience sees someone that does not appreciate all the good Reagan has done.  The audience now has the option to see Reagan through the eyes of Louis, who has gone through a lot.  Louis is in love with Prior, who is diagnosed with AIDS.  Because of Reagan’s lack of action, Louis does not particularly see Reagan as a presidential hero.

Reagan can also be seen through the eyes of a different character in the play, Roy.  Roy is a powerful, well-known lawyer who finds out he has AIDS.  He refuses to believe this fact and does not want other people to know he has AIDS.  While denying the fact he tells his doctor, “But unlike nearly every other man of whom this is true, I bring the guy I’m screwing to the White House and President Reagan smiles at us and shakes his hand” (1487).  He takes the fact that Reagan has not publicly spoken of AIDS and turns it towards himself and how he cannot have AIDS because he still is accepted by Reagan.  In Roy’s mind, if he were truly gay, Reagan would not acknowledge him in the White House, because Reagan has not publicly condoned AIDS.

The last time Reagan is discussed is when Martin and Roy are trying to get Joe to move to Washington to work on the Reagan campaign.  Martin elaborately and dramatically tells of a future plan for how Reagan can be re-elected as president, easily.  This is a trick to try and get Joe to go and work for them.  By Joe not taking this job, it is a way of him denying Reagan himself.  By denying Reagan, Joe is slowly growing to accept his own sexual identity in his own way.  The scene right after Joe denies the Reagan job, Joe talks to Louis.  The conversation also talked about Reagan, which Louis said Reagan won’t get any better.  Once again emphasizing to the audience the side of Reagan’s audience many people do not see as often.  Through these different lenses, the audience is able to see Ronald Reagan as not a perfect president for all aspects of time.

By seeing the different characters’ takes on Ronald Reagan and AIDS, one is able to see how the stigmas created by both affect the way gay men present themselves.  To prevent discrimination and/or physical violence, a gay man during this time must hide who he truly was in order to fit in and be accepted by society.  As also seen, not all this discrimination is in the past.  Our country is based on freedom and all citizens deserve the right to pursue their own happiness.
