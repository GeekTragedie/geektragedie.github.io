<p class="paper-title">Drama Therapy in Juvenile Detention Centers</p>

Academy Award-winner Tim Robbins, an actor best known for his portrayal of Andy Dufresne in the 1994 film *The Shawshank Redemption*, currently teaches an acting class to prisoners in Norco, California.  In 2013 CBS News aired a story covering this acting class.  During this story the reporter states, “More than three hundred inmates have taken the class and, in a state where more than sixty percent of inmates return to prison after they get out, Robbins says, ‘No one that has gone through the program has come back to prison that we are aware of,’” (CBS News).  As seen through Tim Robbin’s acting class, theatre helps these prisoners find a side of themselves they may not have known they had.  While many theatre companies, such as Arts in Prison and Offender Victim Ministries, help people in prison using theatre, many juvenile offenders can also find the help they need this way as well.  Juvenile detention centers (JDC) could highly benefit from a drama therapy program in their facilities. There are different things that need to be considered before implementing drama therapy into JDC, such as planning the sessions ahead of time and the students’ possible resistance to drama therapy.  Using theatre in education (TIE) in juvenile detention classes would help with the students’ resistance to drama therapy and, in practice, will also be beneficial to them.  Overall, drama therapy can be beneficial when used in juvenile detention centers, not only to the adolescents, but also to the staff.

Drama therapy, like all other therapies, does not have a specific definition.  One can try to give it a specific definition, but it is a concept that can be explained in many different ways depending on who is asked.  Robert Landy gives the National Association for Drama Therapy’s definition in his book “Drama Therapy Concepts, Theories and Practices”: “Drama therapy can be defined as the intentional use of drama/theatre process to achieve the therapeutic goal of symptom relief, emotional and physical integration and personal growth,” (Landy 62).  Although this definition does a good job of summing up the idea of drama therapy, there is more to it.  The definition simplifies the techniques used in drama therapy that are exclusively important to make it set apart from psychodrama.  While psychodrama specifically focuses on role playing, drama therapy has many different aspects of theatre to help a patient.  Drama therapy uses masks, make-up, face paint, video, autobiographical performance, photography, puppets, and, of course, theatre (Landy 147-171).  There are areas that psychodrama and drama therapy overlap, but psychodrama is more limited in its methods.

The reason drama therapy needs to be implemented into juvenile detention centers (JDC) is because the facilities currently lack the support their adolescents need.  John McCabe-Juhnke quotes Augusto Boal with his experience in prison theatre by saying, “Augusto Boal calls prisons ‘repositories of humans,’ which are like hospitals full of sick people, ‘without doctors, nurses, or even medicine: how could we expect the sick to be healed in such a scenario,” (McCabe-Juhnke 129).  In JDC, two-thirds of the incarcerated reported symptoms of high aggression, depression, and anxiety with thirty percent reporting a history of either physical or sexual abuse (Gottesman).  Fifty-three percent are not given mental health evaluations and forty-seven percent of those who have previously been diagnosed with mental issues in JDC do not meet with counselors (Gottesman).  With these startling statistics, it is surprising that action has not yet been taken to help these young adults.

Adolescents in JDC need therapy or help of some sort, but are not provided the opportunity.  “Research shows that treating substance abuse can lower recidivism rates,” but without the proper therapies offered, there is a higher chance of juveniles to relapse and return to JDC or worse, prison (Lemmer & Johnston).  A study done in Chicago measured 139 juveniles arrested for murder from 1999-2003.  Of the 139, only eight percent had no prior offence on their record, the rest were repeated offenders (Lemmer & Johnston).  Perhaps if the other ninety-two percent had received the help they needed, they would not have repeated their crimes and thus would have saved lives.  If JDC offered more options and opportunities to help those in their care, maybe more adolescents would have better tools to succeed in life.

Installing a drama therapy program in JDC would allow these facilities to offer their teenagers the tools they need and deserve.  Those who suffer from mental illnesses need help and support from others in order to pull through.  However, adolescents are not the easiest age group to work with.  Adolescents care most about their peers and push away the adults in their lives, including therapists, which makes it difficult for them to obtain the full help they need.  The adolescent years are the stage in peoples’ lives when they are trying to figure out who they are (Gallo-Lopez 81).  Because youth push away adult figures during a crucial point in their lives, it makes it that much more important to reach them.  It is vital to help teenagers while they grow so they do not grow in the wrong direction.  While it seems the youth in JDC have already gone down the wrong path in life, they are still growing and can still be shown a new focus for their lives.  However, showing them the right path proves difficult due to their resistance towards authority figures.

Adolescents see importance in feeling accepted and independence, not in being told what to do (Gallo-Lopez 83).  There are many ways to break down teenagers’ walls of resistance, like humor and cooperative activities (81 & 85).  These tactics, however, are not used in school systems, which is part of the reason why it is difficult to reach this age group.  The school systems are trying to standardize learning and an unintended consequence of this is causing the students to lose their creativity.  “They are asked to think in concrete terms, to restrain from their creative selves, to control their desire for excitement, and to keep their emotions well contained,” (84).  Because of school’s standardization technique, it can cause adolescents to resist drama therapy.  However, Robert Landy offers his own take of theatre in education (TIE) in his “goals of drama in education,” (Landy 12).  In Landy’s book he states that classes need to put more emphasis on three things, “1.  Learning about drama, 2.  Learning about learning, and 3.  Learning about thinking and speaking,” (12).  Teachers should focus on teaching their students how to ask their own questions and seek their own answers.  While they do this, they need to learn how to explain their questions and answers to others so that other can learn as well.  When students create their own questions and are able to answer them effectively in both speech and writing, they enable their minds to think in different, creative ways.

TIE helps students learn, see situations through different perspectives, and also unleashes their creativity.  An example of this can also be found in Landy’s book.  He talks about a sixth grade teacher that made his students write a paper about dumping nuclear waste.  Each student was given a different person being effected by the dumping of nuclear waste and was told to write their paper through the perspectives of that person.  In order to see the other person’s perspective, the class used research, interviews, and role playing (Landy 9).  This project taught the students about drama by them role playing their person’s character while also teaching them how to ask their own questions for their interviews, get their own answers, and then write down their answers to explain it to others.  The project met Landy’s three goals of drama in education, and was able to effectively teach in a creative manner.

Landy’s three goals were also used in a college classroom when a professor used psychodrama to teach his students.  No matter what subject the class was talking about, the professor would have the students take the roles of whomever they were learning about and be that person, feel what they were feeling.  He “tried to show that the people involved [in their topic] were no different from [the students] as human beings, and that [everyone] may learn from them and their situations by momentarily ‘being’ them,” (Black 118).  The students were able to become more active in the class’ discussions and learn that situations are “no longer a matter of ‘somebody’ out there in another state or country.  It is one of my classmates: it can happen; it does happen; it might even happen to me,” (120).  The students learn that anything in life is possible and it makes life, and their studies, feel more real.  The professor’s main goal “is to open at least one doorway, one new area of interest for each student, so that he will have to keep pursuing that interest from then on,” similar to Landy’s learning about learning and learning about thinking (120).

If JDC were to implement Landy’s three goals into their classrooms, the adolescents held there would be less resistant to the idea of drama therapy because they would have something to compare it to.  People are more welcoming to experiences when they can relate it to something else; this idea is called the theory of association.  “By recalling experiences that were similar, or that occurred next to each other in space or time, the intellect is able to give form and order to the meaningless chaos of the senses,” (Levine 91).  Unfamiliar situations can cause anxiety in a person, but any similarity in the incident can cause ease in the senses.  The idea of drama therapy may seem “uncool” to adolescents, but if they are able to make sense of the idea, the wall of resistance may be able to break down.

There are many techniques to breaking down resistance walls with reluctant adolescents; as previously mentioned, one can use humor and cooperative activities.  Because adolescents find so much importance in their peers, seeing others do drama therapy activities helps break their resistance (Gallo-Lopez).  This is seen when Julia Taylor tells of her first experience working in a female juvenile facility.  One of her assignments for her college English class at the University of Michigan was to create a creative arts workshop in a juvenile facility or prison with a partner.  The two of them were given a group from a female juvenile detention center.  The first time they met with the girls, they played a game called “I’m a Magician.  ‘I’m a magician and I turn us all into airplanes,’” (Taylor 200).  As the girls were flying around as airplanes, one of the girls caught Taylor’s eye and said to her, “we don’t do that kinda stuff around here” and she replied with “Yeah, well I guess I have a lot to learn from you all.”  The girl smiled and said, “Yeah.  I think so, too,” (200).  This game opened up the girls to each other and helped them warm up to the idea of theatre and improvisation.

Resistance walls have been broken down within inmates in prisons, which gives hope to being able to break down resistance walls in juvenile delinquents.  Juveniles are similar to prisoners in the way that both have to wear specific clothing, are split into cell blocks, are herded “like cattle,” and are kept track of at all hours of the day (McCabe-Juhnke, Juvenile and Family Services).  Due to the fact that the two facilities are so similar, it is adequate to look at the success of drama therapy in prisons to see how beneficial it would be in JDC.  The most important similarity between prisons and JDC is that both take away freedom.  Theatre allows “individuals [to] become free, free from [their] histories that keep [them] confined in a one-dimensional version of [them]selves,” (Taylor 211).  When youth and adults are put behind bars, they are “locked away from a society that has never been kind to them,” (205).  On top of this feeling of being a social outcast, “twelve percent of incarcerated youth report sexual victimization in youth facilities,” (211).  Prisoners need to feel free from everything they have to deal with in their lives, so what better therapy than one that gives prisoners that which is stolen from them?

Success using theatre in prisons can be seen, as previously discussed, in Tim Robbins acting class in California.  While interviewing one of his students, Sedro, he had no doubts that the acting class was beneficial.

> “Yesterday I got a letter from my wife and my children and they’re actually thanking me for the happiness they see in the visits…and she put on [the letter] thank you for going to church, thank you for going to school, and thank you for going to acting class.  I just wanted to let you know that all three are on the same level,” (CBS News).

Through the eyes of a prisoner himself there is evidence found that theatre raises the spirits of those incarcerated.

More evidence of theatre being beneficial for those incarcerated is seen in the Hutchinson Correctional Facility of Kansas through the eyes of John McCabe-Juhnke.  The “evidence” that McCabe-Juhnke uses “is the evidence that has been used to sustain faith for centuries: stories,” (McCabe-Juhnke 130).  He tells of a performance he presented in the prison and how hope was reestablished when drama therapy was introduced there (132).  This hope is found through the building of a friendship between two men in the prison.  They were put in a scene together and quickly through the practice process these two strangers became best friends that could work towards anything.  Their friendship was put to the test when two of the leads were taken out of their performance and the two had to work together to fill in the roles needed.  The two worked as a team to make sure each knew the lines so the show could continue.  One of the men during their reflection of the workshop said, “Inmates who have another chance at freedom should take up theatre…Doing so will open their minds to a better outlook on life.  They’re still somebody,” (138).  Theatre gave this man a positive outlook on his life and, through friendship, gave him confidence he did not have before.  Once again, theatre has been shown to give inmates the feeling of freedom.  The ability to find freedom and clarity is a tool that inmates can take with them once they leave prison.

Tim Robbins says, “People are going to get out of prison, whether you like it or not, but I would think a smart society would want people with better tools than they had going in,” (CBS News).  The tools provided by drama therapy are important in life because, as Shakespeare says, “All the world’s a stage,” (II.vii.139).  Because “all the world’s a stage,” the actors in the world, society, need to learn their roles.  These roles are a large tool for inmates to obtain before they leave prison and reenter the stage of the world.  For Landy, drama therapy helps people expand their repertoire of roles and for Emunah, drama therapy helps people expand their repertoire of situations (Pellicciari et. al. 608).  These two things are important tools for inmates to obtain before being released into the performance in society.

People play different roles in their lives, such as mother, brother, teacher, stranger, and friend.  Each role is played differently; many people do not treat their teacher the way they would their best friend.  These are simple examples of different roles people need to learn how to play.  This idea of enhancing roles has been proved in an institution for delinquent girls in Hawthorne, New York.  In this institution therapists use psychodrama to help enhance the girls’ repertoire of roles.  This program helped a tom-boy girl learn how to find her feminine side and helped her raise her confidence in possibly being in an intimate relationship with a boy instead of simply being his friend.  The girl’s self-esteem raised and she found herself being more girly, not only during the sessions, but also during her everyday life.  She had found a new role for herself, not changing who she really was on the inside, but by her wanting to change herself on the outside.  Overall, this became a huge confidence builder (Borden 86).  The learning of roles is usually developed when a person is a child through play.  A child can play with a doll and this helps them see through the perspective of a mother, therefore helping her learn the role of “mother.”  If an adolescent did not grow up learning different roles, there is a possibility of them not viewing people as human beings.  One inmate is quoted saying, “When you kill someone, you don’t see them as human beings,” (McCabe-Juhnke 130).  A way to help murderers see people and feel that their victims are human beings is by helping them cast their role in that person’s life or expanding their repertoire.

Along with broadening a person’s repertoire of roles, therapists can also help broaden a person’s repertoire of situations.  The Hawthorne institute also used psychodrama to help train the girls incarcerated there for different life situations such as job training and social situations, either current or future.  Situational job training was used by letting the girl live through a day on the job that she wished to be when she got older.  For example, Jean wanted to be a receptionist and so the therapists created scenes where she would have to overcome her disobedience towards authority figures and learn to adapt to her future occupation (Borden 86).  Another example of growing the girls’ situation repertoire were when one of their girls had a poor history with her mother and she was anxious of her mother coming to visit her.  The therapist ran through different scenarios of what could possibly happen between her and her mother.  During these scenarios, the therapist used reversal, which means that the girl, at times, was in her mother’s shoes.  Being able to see the meetings through her mother’s perspective and being able to go through different possible scenarios of her mother’s visit, the girl reported the real interaction with her mother went better than it ever had before (Borden 81).  By building situational repertoires, people are able to train themselves how to react during certain situations.  Their therapists help them focus their energy and learn the right and wrong ways of handling different circumstances that may come up in their life.

Emunah shared this same perspective of situational learning, but not only for future situations, but for past and present ones as well.  Emunah uses time manipulation and has her patients look at their life through different perspectives at different times.  For example, the patient would envision themselves in the future and answer the questions of who they are now and what they see when they look back at the past.  What do they wish that person, their past self, had done during their life (Pellicciari et. al. 608)?  This action allows the patient see themselves differently and helps them learn what they want to do about the problems within their life through the eyes of their future self.

Both role and situational repertoire building are important for anyone to learn, but especially prisoners.  While incarcerated, people are not attached to society, so they have a higher possibility of losing sense of their roles in society.  As a matter of fact, society casts them into the role of the prisoner and sometimes they feel as though they can never step out of this role.  When prisoners actively participate in drama therapy they are able to widen their horizon of roles and situations and the tools for their life are expanded in doing this.  Broadening these repertoires has not only been found beneficial to adults, but also to other age groups, such as adolescents.

By broadening adolescents’ roles, drama therapy has previously been used with success on adolescents that have been institutionalized for having an eating disorder.  The main goals of this project was to help the patients survive and to find why they have issues with food.  This workshop was especially important to the youth because they were no longer in the role of “the sick one” and could feel free to open up about their emotions (Pellicciari et. al.  611).  Through this drama therapy group the doctors and therapists were able to figure out why a boy had a problem eating sweet and salty foods through a hypothetical scene with “Mr.  Sweet & Salty” (609).  They were also able to see how a girl felt in relation to her family by playing with figures on an evolutionary scale (610).  Finally, they were also able to help a young boy see that he was not eating due to the bullying he experienced at school (610).  Not only did this drama therapy group help the doctors learn how to help their patients, some of the patients learned how to help themselves.  Ninety-three percent of the fifteen patient group were satisfied with the meetings (610).  Most of the patients from this program recommended that other people suffering from eating disorders should also take the workshop (610).  So, not only are the doctors happy, but the patients are as well.

Many other cases have shown people going through drama therapy recommending it to others or wanting others to share their experiences.  While helping men at the Hutchinson Correctional Facility, McCabe-Juhnke witnessed a man encouraging his fellow actors to go on by saying,

> “Look, guys, we’re too far along in this to give it up now.  We’re not just doing this play for us.  If we quit now, the whole drama program suffers – they might not let Mr.  McCabe-Juhnke come anymore.  Okay, so maybe I won’t want to be in drama workshop next time, maybe you won’t.  But my brother might, or your brother.  And that’s why we have to do this show.  Because it’s going to make it possible for others to have this experience,” (McCabe-Juhnke 133).

Drama therapy does not only work with prisoners through the eyes of the therapists; the prisoners and patients themselves know it works too.  Any therapy’s goal is to give their clients tools to help them live life the best that they can.  “Being in prison is not easy, [the men] do hard work” in there, but they also are human beings and, like everyone else, deserve to be treated as such (CBS News).

Before helping anyone with drama therapy, there is a large amount of consideration that needs to be put into how a drama therapy session is planned out, especially with adolescents.  Robert Landy shows a three step structure of a session: the warm up, the action, and the closure, each of which is exclusively important (Landy 125-135).  The warm up consists of either exercises or games to engage the imagination (Pellicciari et. al. 609).  The exercises do not necessarily have to be physical, but there is certainly a possibility for them to be if needed (Landy 128).  The most important aspect of the warm up is to prepare for the action and “helping clients access their role systems,” (127).  Through the warm up process, the therapist might be able find a role to work on during the session.  Loretta Gallo-Lopez gives the example of a family drama therapy session warm up called “the Hat Exchange.” Each family member was to pick out a hat from the hat box provided by the therapist.  “The first person puts their hat and states ‘I am ___,’ identifying their character with a name and a description… Examples might be ‘Bob the Butcher,’” (Gallo-Lopez 85).  The next person in line has to add on to the character’s story until the whole family has gone either one or two times with the same hat, then switches hats.  During the warm up, the therapist might see a certain character developed that they want to enhance and dig deeper into to learn more about (85-86).  The warm-up is crucial in helping an adolescent break down their resistance wall.  Without the warm up, an adolescent may not be willing to participate in the action step of the process.

A warm-up that has been proven to help, specifically with adolescents, is letting them find their own props out of a prop bin and creating a character out of them.   Placing props that an adolescent can relate to in the box is specifically important.   “Two items considered essential are sunglasses and telephones,” (Gallo-Lopez 89).   When someone puts on a pair of sunglasses, they change into another person, especially if they think the sunglasses are “cool.” Sunglasses work like masks; they make one feel disguised and hidden in someone else’s shoes.   They are also something that teenagers can relate to and do not consider “childish,” (89).   Telephones are the same way; comfortable forms that adolescents are accustomed to.   They also open up the teenager to a new conversation through talking to someone on the phone.   The teenager can be any character talking to any other character on the phone, opening up many possibilities for new situations or roles.

The next step in the session process is the action phase where the therapist has found an aspect to work on during the session; the patient might work with puppets, masks, make-up, photography, video, or create a scene.  Any of these techniques can make up the action portion of the session.  Robert Landy gives the example of a warm up that has indicated that the patient wants to work within themselves, so the therapist decides to use a video technique.  The therapist may record the patient sitting in silence for five minutes.  “Following that time, he watches the playback of his first sitting.  During this second phase, he speaks directly to his image on the monitor, addressing it in second person,” (Landy 151).  Then there is a third sitting where the patient watches himself during the second phase and then is asked to “record his impressions in a journal, noting his feelings as he watched himself address his image,” (151).  This specific example is beneficial to helping adolescents that may have issues with their self-image.  Because adolescents put so much emphasis on those around them, they can easily become self-conscious; this is especially true with juveniles because they feel as though they are unwanted in society.  By showing youth how they view themselves, a therapist is able to learn specifically what their patient dislikes about themselves.  With this knowledge, a patient can also see what they would like to change about how they view themselves.

The last step in the session is the closure, which is “not only a period of reflection, pointing to what has just happened, but also a period of hope, pointing to the work yet to be done, the journey toward healthy functioning,” (Landy 132).  In the closure, it is important for the therapist to get the patient out of the character they were previously in so they do not go in to the “real world” playing the role of someone else.  Giving adolescents hope for their future work is particularly important, especially in JDC, where hope is cast out by consistently being told what to do and feeling as though they have lost their sense of freedom.

One of the key things to do in a session is open up the patient for improvisation.  In JDC, there is consistent order and no room for creativity.  The adolescents have become accustomed to this idea and do not know how to let it go, but improvisation lets them be someone they are not and opens a whole realm of things they could possibly be.  By channeling through the possibilities, there is a way to help piece together conceivable things that are bothering the patient.  Stephen Levine builds his beliefs off of the idea that out of chaos, order can always be found.  He “mostly works in improvisatory theatre and… [says] there is always … [the need for] having faith that if you allow a creative process to take place, something will emerge that will have value,” (Levine 113).  When Julia Taylor was working with girls in an institution, she said that “the safety of the improvisation enabled [the] girls to have the conversations they didn’t know they had the words for, and speak to friends who had so often ignored them,” (Taylor 202).  The chaotic feel of improvisation can reveal answers that no other therapy does because it allows the patients to be themselves when society sometimes does not allow it.

Improvisation not only can help the patient, but it can also help the facility’s workers and the juvenile’s case worker as well.  Improvisation has been proven useful in the institution of Cedar Knolls School in Hawthorne, New York.  When the girls felt as though they were ready for parole, the case worker would ask the therapist to test the girl in an improvisational scene to see if she was really ready or not.  This was used in the case of a girl who had an emotional attraction to older men.  The girl had insisted that she was not attracted to older men anymore and that she had a boyfriend her own age.  To test that she was really ready for parole, the therapist created a scene with the girl and two men, an older one and one that was to symbolize her boyfriend.  Both the men were in love with her and she had to decide between the men.  There was also a friend in the scene that she could confide in, but during the scene it was apparent that the girl still was attracted to older men, proving that she was not ready for parole (Borden 84-85).  This process was used for other cases and proven helpful as well.

Not only has there been positive feedback from case and facility administrators, but the use of drama therapy with adolescents has also gotten positive feedback from teachers and school administrators.  ACTING OUT is a drama therapy program created, not only to help the individuals who attend the therapy sessions, but also to help the youth around the area.  The program ran for thirty-five weeks and contained youth from ages thirteen to nineteen.  There was a wide balance of gender, age, and background, but most adolescents in the group did not come from normal married parents; most of the patients were adopted, from single-parents, foster families, or blended families.  This group met, like a normal drama therapy group would, but on top of helping the individuals with their own personal issues, the youth also traveled to schools and performed for audiences their own age.  The performances’ issues have coved “substance abuse, AIDS awareness, sexual abuse, family dysfunction, social pressures, the Gulf War, date rape, homophobia, gender roles, and how the dinosaurs became extinct,” (Cossa 54).  The audience is encouraged to stop the performance to ask specific characters why they make the choices they make or offer advice.  “Many student audience members have said, ‘We’ve heard this stuff before from our teachers, but when kids our own age are acting it out it means a lot more,’” (55).  This program not only reached the youth in it, but it also helped the student audiences.  This program has gotten an enormous “positive response and validation from teachers and school administrators,” (54).  Drama therapists need to be careful, however, on how they go about performing and who performs.

Robert Landy warns, “There are dangers inherent in the transformation of improvisationally based theatre, created in a drama therapy setting, into more conventional performance for general audience,” (Landy 171).  The client might be too exposed and should only perform for a safe audience (171).  The drama therapist needs to not confuse the importance of the therapy with the idea of performance.  This is very important to consider when a therapist is working with a cast of adolescents that are very concerned about their peers and how they are viewed.  If something were to go wrong during a performance, all the therapy work that has been put into the patient could disappear.  If a group seems able and comfortable moving towards performance, then it would be safe, like that which happened with Julia Taylor’s creative arts workshop that performed their improvisational play in front of others in the institution and family members (Taylor 210-11).  The girls in the group were prepared and motivated to do well in the performance and this motivation to show what they could do to others increased their confidence.

There are many benefits JDC can achieve through implementing drama therapy into their systems.  They offer support that many of their adolescents need and do not receive.  When a drama therapist is knowledgeable about adolescent problems and the juvenile judicial system, this program would be used to its fullest capacity.  Setting up the sessions, either one-on-one or in a group setting, needs to be taken into consideration and done well, step by step.  If the drama therapist is close to the administration of the facility, case workers and teachers could also benefit from the therapy work.  There are ideas, however, that need to be considered before orchestrating the program into schools, such as possible resistance of the youth and whether or not to put on a performance.  Whether or not they believe it, adolescents need to be helped. The teenage years are the most important because this age is when youth are making drastic decisions about life and what to do with it.  Even though they push away adult figures, these figures need to reach out and help the youth to grow in the right direction.  Many juveniles need help to prosper in their life and this help could be found through drama therapy.
