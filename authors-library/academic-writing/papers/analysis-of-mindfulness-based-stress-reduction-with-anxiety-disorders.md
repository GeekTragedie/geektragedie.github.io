## Analysis of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction with Anxiety Disorders

Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) is a counseling theoretical orientation developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn (2003). To follow ethical guidelines for counseling governing bodies, clinicians need to use evidenced-based practices to ensure that they are offering the best care practices possible. To best meet this requirement, counselors should know how to analyze theoretical orientations to ensure their theoretical orientation can best suit their clients. Therefore, before using MBSR, therapists need to ensure that this theoretical orientation meets evidence-based standards that will help serve clients. There is a wide base of research regarding MBSR; for this analysis, the research has been narrowed to treating clients with anxiety disorders. The purpose of this paper is to give an analysis of MBSR for anxiety disorders to aid counselors’ decision to use this practice with clients. To analyze this orientation, multiple aspects are considered to include mechanisms of change, replicability, transportability, and economical costs.

## History of MBSR Research

Before analyzing the research, it is best to understand where MBSR has come from. The history of MBSR starts with the inclusion of mindfulness-based practices into therapy. Mindfulness teachings evolved out of *dharma* teachings, which Buddhist teachings also developed from. Mindfulness is paying attention on purpose without judgement (Kabat-Zinn, 2003). Mindfulness-based therapies emphasize “first-person experiences of suffering and happiness in human beings” (Kabat-Zinn, 2003, p. 147). Mindfulness-based clinicians believe it is important to become one with suffering (Rosenbaum, 2017). Instead of trying to distract the self from feelings of suffering, mindfulness-based therapists invite clients to feel the suffering and understand it. Through bringing awareness to the moments of suffering, a client can find points of origin for the suffering to increase understanding and therefore increase self-compassion (Chodron, 1997; Rosenbaum, 2017).

MBSR was not developed originally for the field of psychology. Kabat-Zinn (2003) first developed MBSR in 1982 for medical patients dealing with physical pain at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. It was developed to complement patients’ medical treatment and aimed to be a mindfulness model for other hospitals (p. 149). Before MBSR was used as a counseling technique, medical research supported that MBSR was beneficial to clearing skin for psoriasis patients, aided in brain and immune system changes, and decreased pain for patients with prostate cancer (Kabat-Zinn, 2003). After these successful findings, Kabat-Zinn et al.’s (1992) randomized control trial study introduced meditation-based stress reduction to psychology by applying it to clients with anxiety disorders. After the development of meditation-based stress reduction, MBSR became a theoretical orientation that was expanded to be used in counseling practices.

## Theoretical Tenets

MBSR theoretical tenets developed from mindfulness including awareness, attention, and remembering (Siegel et al., 2009, p. 18). Attention is one’s ability to focus or notice a sensation. Awareness includes the personal agency to regulate one’s ability to focus attention at any given moment. The aspect of remembering is a person’s ability to remember to practice mindfulness with intentionality. Therefore, the act of mindfulness is remembering to notice and focus one’s attention during any moment and live purposefully. Through the practice of mindfulness, it is believed that one can “eliminate needless suffering by cultivating insight into the workings of the mind and the nature of the material world” (Siegel et al., 2009, p. 18). Based on these key factors of mindfulness, MBSR then added psychological tenets to the framework.

The original mindfulness teachings have been adapted into Western psychology through the use of therapeutic mindfulness approaches by adding the qualities of nonjudgement, acceptance, and compassion (Siegel et al., 2009, p. 19). Kabat-Zinn (2003) defines therapeutic mindfulness as “the awareness that emerges through paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally to the unfolding of experience moment to moment” (p. 145). Through therapeutic mindfulness people are able to have “awareness, of present experience, with acceptance” (Siegel et al., 2009, p. 19). There is a need for therapeutic mindfulness because the more suffering a person experiences, the more they will learn to need nonjudgmental acceptance and compassion for the self (Rosenbaum, 2017). Through the practice of mindfulness, clients can learn to observe their thoughts and relate to them cognitively instead of being overcome with emotion. The ability to mindfully analyze one's thoughts allows them to create a mental model about the cause, impact, and pattern of said thoughts. (Rosenbaum, 2017).

### MBSR in Practice

MBSR has a specific way of being practiced in a counseling setting. Used either in a group or individual counseling setting, MBSR is a psychoeducation-based therapy where therapists are teachers and mindfulness is the curriculum (Santorelli & Kabat-Zinn, 2003). Therapists teach mindfulness practices to clients to aid in self-management of symptoms. The key to MBSR is the practice of incorporating mindfulness into clients’ every-day life. Mindfulness homework is assigned, and therapy sessions focus on reflecting on homework assigned (Santorelli & Kabat-Zinn, 2003). Through self-management, MBSR aims to give clients tools to continue their care after therapy.

### Role of the Therapist

Therapists have a key duty when using MBSR with clients. With MBSR, it is believed that mindfulness “cannot be taught to others in an authentic way without the instructor’s practicing it in his or her own life” (Kabat-Zinn, 2003, p. 149). Therefore, therapists using the MBSR orientation must practice mindfulness in their everyday life. Modeling can be an important element in therapy as clients can learn by the way a clinician practices (Rosenbaum, 2017). In MBSR, the therapist is both a role model and an instructor. MBSR is a counseling theoretical orientation that teaches clients how to integrate their life experiences into who they are, to include experiences of suffering, but there are specific mechanisms in MBSR that cause change in clients.

## Mechanisms of Change

Even though Kabat-Zinn et al. (1992) initially used MBSR for clients with anxiety disorders, further research needs to be conducted regarding the specific mechanism of change of MBSR for this population. In order to provide an analysis of MBSR, outside research was collected on general MBSR mechanisms of change. Elements of MBSR that have been found to cause change in clients are mindfulness, self-compassion, decentering, and body scan interventions. Evans et al. (2018) state that their “data were consistent with the premise that increased mindfulness and self-compassion predict improvements in a broad range of psychological health related outcomes, including a number of commonly experienced affective states” (Evans et al., 2018, p. 1285). This finding is rather broad and does not have an elaboration but is important to framing the change elements of mindfulness and self-compassion.

### Mindfulness

The most prevalent mechanism of change that has been researched for MBSR is mindfulness (Gawrysiak et al., 2018; Keng et al., 2012). The orientation of MBSR was based on mindfulness being a mechanism of change for clients. Based on the theoretical tenets of MBSR, mindfulness is believed to benefit through integrational suffering, nonjudgement of the self, and focused attention (Kabat-Zinn, 2003). This mechanism has been found to independently influence emotional regulation and fear of emotion (Keng et al., 2012). Gawrysiak et al. (2018) studied brain activity while using MBSR and found evidence that mindfulness specifically decreases negative brain overfunctioning. To continue to develop the influence of mindfulness, researchers recommend further analysis “identifying temporal precedence” (Gawrysiak et al., 2018, p. 533). Simply practicing mindfulness may not be the only cause of change, as there is research supporting the way in which mindfulness should be practiced.

The quality of mindfulness practice outside of sessions has also been found to be an important mechanism in MBSR. Goldberg et al. (2019) studied the influence of how well clients practice mindfulness at home. Researchers found that “practice quality may function as a mechanism underlying the beneficial effects of practice time, with improved practice quality acting as a mediator linking practice time and outcome” (Goldberg et al., 2019, p. 5). Through their findings, Goldberg et al. (2019) recommend counselors reinforce the importance of how clients are practicing their mindfulness outside of the sessions to improve positive change. It is also recommended that clinicians discourage “habitual mental tendencies” to reinforce change in cognition. Mindfulness is important to MBSR, as evidence supports mindfulness leading clients to self-compassion (Evans et al., 2018, p. 1285), which is another important mechanism of change in MBSR.

### Self-Compassion

Self-compassion has been researched as a mechanism of change for MBSR. Self-compassion has been found to aid in reducing worry, fear of emotion, maladaptive cognitive coping, and increasing “willingness to accept and experience emotions” (Keng et al., 2012, p. 276). Keng et al. (2012) notes that specifically decreasing worry has been found to positively influence cognitive behaviors in people with anxiety disorders. While anxiety disorders were not directly researched, a conclusion can be drawn through the outside research regarding these specific elements that have changed.

### Decentering

Research supports decentering clients can be a mechanism of change in MBSR for clients experiencing anxiety. According to Hoge et al. (2015), decentering is “a metacognitive capacity of individuals to observe items that arise in the mind” and “promotes disengagement from internal experiences in favor of adopting a more distanced perspective” (p. 229). Gawrysiak et al. (2018) found that decentering is a mechanism of change in MBSR for anxiety. Through decentering, clients are better able to aesthetically distance themselves from their feelings and view them through different perspectives as a witness.

### Body Scan Intervention

Body scan meditations are key to MBSR and are one of the most assigned homework practices (Santorelli & Kabat-Zinn, 2003). Body scan meditations are facilitated mindfulness that leads clients to focus their attention on feelings in the body to open awareness to the body. In MBSR, these are facilitated using recorded voices that are provided to clients (Santorelli & Kabat-Zinn, 2003). These interventions can take anywhere from five minutes to an hour. This MBSR technique has been found to be beneficial to help clients with anxiety. While body scans have not been studied as a mechanism of change in an MBSR study, researchers have individually studied the influence of body scans (Dreeben et al., 2013). Researchers have found body scans to be “positively and significantly correlated with decreased interpersonal sensitivity and anxiety” (Dreeben et al., 2013, p. 398). The amount of time a person practices body scans has also been significantly correlated to decrease anxiety (Dreeben et al., 2013). Therefore, research should be conducted to study the specific change MBSR body scan interventions have on clients with anxiety disorder because there is evidence supporting body scans being a mechanism of change for this theoretical orientation.

## Replicability

Similar to research regarding mechanisms of change for MBSR with anxiety disorders, research on replicability for this orientation with this population is also lacking. There are two ways replicability was analyzed for MBSR with anxiety disorders: adherence to the traditional protocol and formal MBSR training of instructors.

### Manualized Protocol

MBSR is lacking research with reliable sources using the same protocol methods for clients with anxiety disorders. To check for protocol adherence, researchers usually apply treatment from a structured, published protocol, handbook, or manual. The application of MBSR in these studies was difficult to standardize. The researchers used various sources as protocols for applying MBSR treatment to their samples. Some of the articles either used various sources, did not give a source, or had one source that did not match another article. The majority of the articles used the four following sources for their protocols: Kabat-Zinn (1990, six articles), Kabat-Zinn (1982, two articles), and Kabat-Zinn and Santorelli (1993) and Santorelli and Kabat-Zinn (2009, two articles).

Having reviewed the majority protocols, it is difficult to determine what protocols were followed as many of the sources did not provide step-by-step instructions. The most replicable manual is Kabat-Zinn and Santorelli’s (1993, 2009) publications; the 2009 version is an updated protocol. These authors provide a specific protocol to follow in their manual of MBSR. The articles that use this manual for their protocols are reliable replications. Kabat-Zinn’s (1990) book is used in many articles to describe MBSR; however, after reviewing the book, there is no specific protocol to follow for therapy. The book gives readers an outline of an eight-week plan to follow if they want to incorporate mindfulness into their daily life (Kabat-Zinn, 1990). This outline is not a protocol for applying MBSR in therapy sessions. While a large majority of the researchers use this book for their protocol, there is no way of verifying how the researchers applied the book’s teachings to their sample populations.

While there is a lack of reliable source material to replicate MBSR, there may be an explanation to the variance. In response to the lack of reliable protocol, Imel et al. (2008) state that the MBSR treatment they applied was:

> flexibly applied by instructors. This flexibility is regarded as essential to maintain the active ingredient of MBSR, namely mindfulness itself, and avoids what has been called ‘McMindfulness,’ which may occur if MBSR is rigidly manualized in an effort to mass produce it” (p. 738)

To support this statement, the researchers cited an article titled “Mindfulness-based stress reduction versus bona-fide active control” of which the manuscript was still being prepared for publication. This article, however, was unable to be located using database searches. Therefore, there may be research explaining and/or supporting the lack of MBSR manual, but the author was unable to find the research to support this claim.

### MBSR Supervision or Instructors

With little research supporting the protocol applied by other researchers, the author turned to the training of the instructors who applied MBSR to populations with anxiety disorders. For an instructor to meet criteria to properly apply MBSR to a population, they must finish formal training and be certified in MBSR, ideally from the University of Massachusetts Center for Mindfulness (UMCM). With this criterion in mind and only considering the information presented in the articles, four studies can be supported by the researchers or instructors’ knowledge of MBSR. Arch et al. (2013) research is supported through instructor training, but cannot support MBSR with anxiety disorders because they used an adapted MBSR for their population, though they are one of the few authors with formal MBSR training to know that their version was an adaptation.

The articles supporting replicable MBSR to populations with anxiety disorders are Goldin and Gross (2010), Hjeltnes et al. (2017), and Hjeltnes et al. (2018). Notably, two groups of authors are the same, making the application of MBSR less replicable, as fewer groups of researchers have been able to apply the same treatment. The researchers supported through training have also only applied MBSR to populations with social anxiety disorder (SAD), not other types of anxiety disorders. Therefore, two groups of researchers have supportively applied MBSR to people with SAD. These three articles also only used one-group pretest-posttest designs in their research, making their findings preliminary and non-generalizable because there was no control group to show that it was indeed MBSR that caused the change for these samples with SAD. Overall, MBSR supervision in the research did not support the replicability of MBSR to clients with anxiety disorders.

### Replicability Conclusion

There needs to be more accurately applied protocols and/or trainers before MBSR is fully supported to benefit people with anxiety disorder. Due to the lack of clear protocol in the MBSR text, it is unclear how the studies followed MBSR protocol. Further research can be done into the researchers’ backgrounds to see if they were trained in MBSR from a credible location.

## Transportability

MBSR for clients with anxiety disorders has not been extensively researched. Researchers have studied MBSR’s effects on clients with anxiety disorders but have little transportability beyond this narrowed population. A majority of the research has been conducted with either generalized populations (Goldin & Gross, 2010; Hoge et al., 2015) or young adult, college student populations (Hjeltnes et al., 2017; Hjeltnes et al. 2018; Barbosa et al. 2013). There are few thorough articles that have applied MBSR to more specific anxiety populations: comorbid depression, veterans, and global white populations.

To increase discourse regarding the population, Hazlett-Stevens (2012) conducted a study with a sample of MBSR group members with comorbid anxiety and depression. The article appears to discuss a group with these diagnoses but only explains the results from one member of the study. Upon further exploration for an article on the whole population, the researcher did not publish the findings from the MBSR group members. The current article does not give an explanation for not discussing the group’s findings. Therefore, the transportability to this population is still left not researched on a generalizable level.

Arch et al. (2013) and Arch and Ayers (2013) studied adapted MBSR’s effects on veterans with heterogeneous anxiety disorders and compared the MBSR group findings to a control CBT group. While the study notes it uses an adapted version of MBSR, the instructors and therapists were trained by the UMCM and may have a better understanding of what adapted MBSR is in comparison to other researchers who may not know the difference between adapted and true MBSR. Building from current discourse, the researchers adapted MBSR for their veteran population using another study’s recommendations for treatment. In terms of transportability of MBSR with anxiety disorders to the veteran population, this study supported that “adapted MBSR [was] efficacious in treating the principal anxiety disorder(s)” and that this treatment supported previous MBSR results. Therefore, this study supports MBSR’s transportability to veterans with anxiety disorders.

Research has been conducted by two other majority white countries, Norway and Canada. In Norway, Hjeltnes et al. (2017), Hjeltnes et al. (2018), and Vøllestad et al. (2011) researched the application of MBSR. Hjeltnes et al. brought MBSR to a young adult, student population and found the treatment beneficial with a “large and moderate-to-large effect size” (p. 88). Through the researcher’s use of measures, “approximately two thirds of the participants who were in the clinical range at pretreatment recovered or improved after completing the MBSR program” (p. 88). Sadly, this study did not have a control group, so the findings cannot be solely attributed to MBSR. Vøllestad et al. (2011) applied MBSR to a more general Norwegian population with heterogeneous anxiety disorders and found statistical improvement for clients along with a sustained benefit. These findings are generalizable to a larger population as the study included a control group and had a reputable sample size in comparison to other MBSR research teams. In Canada, Koszycki et al. (2007) supported MBSR for clients clinically diagnosed with SAD. Researchers supported that clients, after being treated with MBSR, “produced significantly meaningful changes on measures of social anxiety, mood, disability and quality of life” (p. 2524). Without further research into how these countries differ from the US, there is research to support the possibility of MBSR being beneficial to other primarily white populations in other countries that have similar individualistic cultures to the US, Canada, and Norway.

There is a lack of research on transportability of MBSR with anxiety disorders. It is important to note that research was limited to studies written in English; there is a chance that other countries have research supporting or rejecting the use of MBSR with other cultural populations. Further research needs to be conducted with more diverse populations with anxiety disorders in order to fully generalize the use of MBSR with anxiety disorders to all diverse populations. Based on Hays’ (2001) ADDRESSING model, it is recommended that future research transport MBSR to the following populations with anxiety disorders: various ethnic and racial backgrounds, elders, children, gender minorities, low socioeconomic status, people with disabilities, and sexual minorities. Based on personal experience and critical thinking, the following populations are also recommended: forensic populations, foster care children and workers, comorbid populations, and couple and family therapy settings.

## Economic Evaluation

MBSR is a cost-efficient treatment on both governmental and personal levels. MBSR promotes self-care and encourages self-management of symptoms, which aids clients in continuing their healing practices after termination of counseling (Knight et al., 2015). Self-management helps both the client and the government lower costs because fewer clients would need to go back into therapy after termination.

### Governmental Cost

Self-management has many benefits to the government beyond the money spent on healthcare. There are particular populations that better benefit from learning self-management skills that, if continued management does not happen, it directly impacts the cost of the government. If MBSR is used with forensic populations, then the recidivism rates of those clients would decrease, therefore saving the government money in criminal justice costs. The same applies for substance use populations who need rehabilitation. By decreasing recidivism and substance use, the government also can decrease the case loads of social workers and pay less for foster care homes because less children would be taken away from families due to substance use or imprisonment.

On a practical level, there are certain benefits that in the way MBSR is used that can aid the healthcare system economically. This practice is usually used in group settings, which means the organizations do not have to spend more money for individual therapy because MBSR is helping more people in less time (Knight et al., 2015). In a large scale longitudinal quantitative research study, Canada researchers Knight et al. (2015) found that stress is a noticeable risk factor for psychological distress, causing higher rates of governmental funding covering mental health issues due to stress. MBSR has been found to decrease stress and be helpful for longer-term, therefore also decreasing the cost from the governmental healthcare system (Knight et al., 2015). Overall, Knight et al. (2015) found that MBSR participants decreased their healthcare costs by $250 per person, with a total of $375,000 when applied to the general population. The government can pay less money if MBSR was implemented on a broader level or invested in by the government.

### Therapist Cost

It is also important to consider the cost of become a certified MBSR trainer, which is the most reliable and credible form of MBSR counselor (University of Massachusetts, 2018). MBSR can be broken into three tiers: one course with some understanding of integrating MBSR into practices, having completed the certification and can fully do MBSR, and being a certified trainer to teach MBSR to other counselors (University of Massachusetts, 2018).

There are two locations that offer full course work on MBSR to reach the second tier of training: Center for Mindfulness in Medicine, Health Care in Worcester, Massachusetts, and Society and University of California San Diego Center for Mindfulness. For cost, the Massachusetts program states, “Sliding-scale levels are based on household income” (University of Massachusetts, 2018), but the site is not user friendly and is difficult for a person to understand the full cost, making it less accessible. It is difficult to understand how much the full cost of earning the credentials would be based on their website. There are tuition costs given, but it is not broken down into what particular prices are or what the numbers given are referring to (i.e. per credit, full cost, etc.). The program in California breaks down the cost fully and to finish the program would cost $10,850 (University of California, 2019). The cost of being fully trained to do MBSR is expensive for therapists and are only offered in specific locations.

Each program has an online course offered, but only for select courses. Therapists are not able to complete the full training online. The accessibility of the training being online is helpful, though practicing counseling skills online has its challenges. The cost benefits of MBSR is worth the second-tier level of training at least.

## Final Analysis

There are many strengths and weaknesses to clinicians using MBSR to treat clients with anxiety disorders. The foundation of the MBSR field was built from mindfulness alone benefiting many people. There are multiple aspects of MBSR that result in change for general clients, meaning aspects of MBSR can influence a larger amount of people due to the different mechanisms of change. These aspects, however, have yet to be connected directly to clients with anxiety disorders. With MBSR promoting self-management and continued practice, MBSR’s effects are long-term, which makes the practice economically beneficial for individual clients, insurance agencies, and government healthcare systems. Due to MBSR focusing on decreasing stress, MBSR is more cost-effective because distress is what brings most clients into counseling. MBSR research supports that this technique is beneficial for clients with anxiety disorders; however, more evidence needs to be gathered before a full analysis can be drawn.

The negative aspects to MBSR with anxiety disorders is that the research lacks reliability due to low replicability and transportability in current research. While research may support the use of MBSR in counseling, research has yet to support MBSR for clients with anxiety disorders. The research base, especially sample size, for anxiety disorder is not large enough to be generalizable to the population at large; therefore, clinicians cannot ensure they are providing the best care to their clients with anxiety disorders, especially if their clients come from minority backgrounds. Overall, this theory has many practical application strengths and benefits; however, before safely using this theoretical orientation in counseling settings, further research is recommended in regard to manualizing the orientation and transporting the protocol to diverse populations.

This research also has limitations on behalf of the researcher. The research gathered for this analysis was limited to a time span and limited databases based on the school affiliated. The information gathered was only from articles written in the English language. Therefore, these findings are only preliminary and a deeper investigation into MBSR with anxiety disorders is recommended by another independent researcher to draw different conclusions.
