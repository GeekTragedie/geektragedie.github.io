<p class="paper-title">Autobiographical Statement</p>

My desire to be a drama therapist started when I discovered my ambition to help other people. I first felt this desire while traveling on a mission trip to Ukraine where I assisted in organizing camp activities and taught Ukrainian adolescents English. I enjoyed working together with the students to break down the language barrier as we took on a variety of tasks, such as Camp Olympics and enhancing visual outlook of the school. I thoroughly enjoyed discussing their culture and how it differed from the United States. On my flight home, I reflected on the trip and realized how much I enjoyed helping other people, and from that moment, helping others became my main goal in life. This experience laid out the groundwork for making the decision to become a drama therapist.

I reached a crucial turning point in my life regarding my future in drama therapy while taking a theatre class in college. I watched an NBC news broadcast highlighting the work of Tim Robbins and The Actors’ Gang in the prison system in California. The program led by Robbins teaches prisoners to express the emotions they have been taught to suppress. The broadcast stated that those who had taken the class and left the prison were never reconvicted. I have always found the progress of this group inspiring. This program has shown me theatre can be used to improve the lives of others, which motivated me to combine my aspiration to help others with my love for theatre by becoming a drama therapist.

Once I decided to pursue drama therapy, I declared a double major in theatre and psychology. To learn more about drama therapy, I asked my advisor to mentor an independent study where I wrote a research paper about how drama therapy has aided incarcerated populations. After this independent study, I was motivated to continue pursuing this career and learn more about how theatre has been used to help others, which was why I took Arts in Health courses, interned and worked at the Family Visitation Center, and received a grant to research how theatre effects youths in juvenile detention centers. These experiences only reassured my determination to become a drama therapist.

My path with drama therapy started during my senior year of high school when I created a drama program for the sixth graders in my school district because they did not have this type of program at their level. The students enjoyed creating theatre, and I looked forward to rehearsals with the students and watching them learn. Through this experience and my trip to Ukraine, I realized my passion to provide opportunities for adolescents to experience theatre, so I pursued more opportunities to work with young adult populations. My work at the Family Visitation Center has shown me what foster children go through, especially those with mental health issues or behavioral problems. These youths were in a state of flux and were attempting to adapt to multiple changes at the same time. Drama therapy could help them learn to adapt to their new surroundings and deal with their emotions in a safe environment. This time in a young adult’s life is already difficult as they are going through puberty; adding family difficulties on top of this poses a serious challenge for a person to deal with. I would like to help guide these youths through these difficult times using drama therapy.

I want to also help at-risk and incarcerated youth populations using drama therapy. While The Actor’s Gang inspired me, I do not feel I personally have a calling for working with adults. I would like to teach incarcerated adolescents theatre so that they can discover the triggers for their negative emotions and find a positive outlet for them. I also want to help give “a voice to those who have lost theirs… who are locked away from a society that has never been kind to them” (Taylor 205). I want to teach adolescents how to redirect their emotions and deal with the multiple issues that have contributed to their situation. I want to teach the youths tools they can use throughout their lives after their adolescent years. Ultimately, I would like to use drama therapy to help adolescents learn about themselves and improve their lives, particularly those in foster care, incarcerated, or at-risk.

I believe the only way to ensure that drama therapy will continue to be used as a therapeutic practice in the future is through research. In the second edition of Dr. Robert Landy’s book, *Drama Therapy: Concepts, Theories, and Practices*, he writes that “drama therapy is still a relatively new, emerging discipline” (241). To show that drama therapy would be a useful therapy method with adolescents, on-going research needs to be conducted to help prove the effects and cause its use to spread. When attempting to find research in this area, I have noticed that there is a limited amount of information regarding drama therapy with the populations I lean towards. With more research, people can be made aware of the healing power of drama therapy and more will be able to benefit from the field. I enjoy conducting research to see how theatre can affect others. While my area of interest is with the populations above, I have thoroughly enjoyed working with any population I have been assigned to in my work and studies, including dementia patients, college students, those with developmental disabilities, and racial and gender minorities. I am also interested in how multiple theatre techniques can be used with different populations. Accumulating quantitative and qualitative data has helped me learn the most beneficial way to help others through theatre. With a degree in drama therapy, I would also like to be a researcher to enhance this field and show others the benefits of drama therapy.

Upon reading literature, there have been multiple essays influencing my thoughts regarding the field of drama therapy. During my independent study, I read Dr. Landy’s book, cited above, which enhanced my ambition to become a drama therapist greater by expanding my knowledge regarding the drama therapy field. Two concepts in this book that influenced my views of drama therapy were the many ways to use drama therapy and the idea that people have multiple roles in their lives. The reason drama therapy intrigued me was because of theatre’s diversity as a field. All theatre productions need people with various talents to be successful. Dr. Landy explained that with theatre having such a wide variety of aspects, drama therapy also has many approaches. The mask technique he explains piqued my interest because of its connection to role theory. All people wear multiple masks in their lives, but they do not always notice them. By creating physical masks, one can manipulate how they view themselves and how others may see them. This role creation idea has also been in other drama therapy techniques, such as photography, video, makeup, and puppetry. These multiple approaches are beneficial because they can be used to assist people with different personalities that play multiple roles in their lives.

I also have found performance studies fascinating; the fact that all people perform multiple roles throughout their lives is a testament to theatre’s importance to everyone. I have been specifically drawn to Dr. Landy’s writing regarding role theory: “the human personality is a system of interrelated roles which provide a sense of order and purpose” (102). The idea that everyone’s personality is complex excites me and make me want to do drama therapy to help others navigate their complex personas. Differing personalities evolving from various experiences has made multiple approaches to drama therapy necessary.

Dr. Landy’s discussion regarding how theatre works in prisons combined with my interest in The Actor’s Gang influenced me to further research theatre with incarcerated populations which is why I am drawn to John McCabe-Juhnke’s article, “Faith, Hope, and ‘Sweet Love Re-Membered’: ‘Restoration’ Theatre in Kansas Prisons.” McCabe-Juhnke explains how “theatre develops cooperation, discipline, and self-expression” which are “necessary skills for reentering society” (127). This article engaged me more than others because of its discussion of hope. Theatre showed the incarcerated population in Kansas “that hope can be rebuilt in prison when theatre establishes a performance community that stimulates the imaginative” (132). Theatre has shown prisoners that “they’re still somebody,” not just numbers (138). After reading about how theatre has worked in prisons, I did more research to see how theatre has been used with the incarcerated adolescent population.

I found an article by Julia Taylor called “Sculpting Empowerment: Theatre in a Juvenile Facility and Beyond.” Taylor did a theatre program at a juvenile facility for girls in Michigan through the Prison Creative Arts Project. In this program, the girls played improvisation games, wrote their own play, and then performed it. Her program inspired me to create my own program at a local juvenile detention center that was structured in the same way. Taylor’s essay discussed freedom in theatre, specifically in improvisation and that “theatre in prison is an escape from the madness.” What most influenced me in Taylor’s article were the stories of the individual girls she worked with and what she learned from them. After my own JDC experience, I reread Taylor’s article and was able to connect to the girls that felt locked away, unable to have freedom. Taylor wrote, “this theatre is about freedom… individuals become free, free from our histories that keep us confined in a one-dimensional version of ourselves” (211). While someone may not be incarcerated, they can still feel as though they have been trapped by society’s expectations. Through drama therapy, I want to bring this freedom to the people that I work with in the field.
