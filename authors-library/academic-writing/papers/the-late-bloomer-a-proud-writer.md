<p class="paper-title"><strong>The Late Bloomer: A Proud Writer</strong></p>

Writing abilities are like a flower. Someone plants a seed and that seed needs nourishment for it to start to bud. The care cannot stop there, however. There needs to be constant attention to that bud in order for it to fully blossom into a flower. Once it is a flower, others in the world can see and appreciate its beauty. Writers evolve over the course of their life, like a flower. Hedgcock (2008) believes writers learn their process and skills, not from direct academic course work but from “routine encounters with texts and with fellow novices, gatekeepers, and experts” (p. 33). After reading this claim, I thought about people I have met in my life who played a role in my writing development and realized there were different stages in my life (from grade school to the beginning of my undergraduate degree to my upper-level undergraduate courses) in which my writing flower was tended. My writing abilities, like Hedgcock’s (2008) argument, developed over the course of my life due to helpful encounters with many influential people who have played different roles in my life.

## Grade School Encounters

During my elementary school years, I sought out additional help from my mother with my reading abilities, and I never believed I was a good writer due to my educational structure. Smith (1988) states, “it [can] only be through reading that writers learn all the intangibles they know” (as cited in Hedgcock, 2008, p. 42). This lesson seemed to be true during my elementary school years as the multiple schools I attended always combined reading and writing lessons. I have always been a slow reader; my mom corrects me now and says, “not a slow reader, a comprehensive reader” (personal communication, October 11, 2018). When I was young, however, my slow reading abilities lead me to believe I was a poor writer due to reading and writing being taught simultaneously. I remember a night when my mom, an elementary reading specialist, was trying to help me speed up my reading skills as I felt inadequate reading aloud during my classes. We were reading the book *Ruby Holler* by Sharon Creech, and as I would read a page, my mother would time me. As I read, I could get faster, but my comprehension would decrease. I was confused at how much my mom loved teaching reading. I eventually gave up trying to increase my reading speed and instead I developed a distaste for reading. Due to my schools teaching reading and writing together, I grew to dislike writing as well, but because of my mom’s efforts, I always found the subjects interesting and strived to understand why she loved them enough to teach them.

My curiosity led me towards taking AP Literature my senior year of high school which diminished my poor relationship with reading and writing because the teacher was greatly dedicated to her students. Before taking AP Literature, I faced the same frustration Hedgcock (2008) expresses: “I might have struggled less… had I been told what to do and how to do it” (p. 36). During this literature class, however, my teacher taught us what Hedgcock (2008) calls “the much-maligned five-paragraph essay formula” (p. 33). This format of writing decreased my anxiety of a blank page as I was able to create an outline of my ideas. My success in this class decreased my distaste for writing as I had found a format to portray my thoughts more easily. My literature teacher had planted a writing seed inside of me that wanted to start growing.

## Early College Growth

The next person in my life who inspired my writing skills was a fellow writer, my English Composition professor, Wes. I was nervous having to take an English course in college because my reading and writing skills were new to me, and I had not had the ability to practice them in a safe setting. I became less nervous when Wes introduced himself to the class as a graduate student who was also taking courses at our university. He wanted us to view him as what Hedgcock (2008) calls another “novice” writer (p. 33). Throughout his course, I found myself participating a lot because I liked his teaching methods and the course focused on writing, not literature. Like my high school teacher, Wes taught the five-paragraph essay format, which gave me the practice I had needed to hone my abilities with this method. I received positive feedback from my professor regarding my writing skills, and at the end of the semester, I felt more comfortable with my writing.

With more confidence in my abilities to write, I enrolled in my mandatory Introduction to Literature class the following semester, which Wes expressed interest in having me as a student again. The literature class scared me, as I was most self-conscious about my slow reading, but taking the course with Wes relieved my anxiety. I confided in Wes that I was a slow reader, and he told me the speed with which one reads does not affect one’s ability to comprehend. This information was a revelation, and it created a whole new respect for reading and writing. I started to use his office hours to brainstorm topics and discuss my drafts. During brainstorm sessions, I realized I enjoyed academic conversations regarding the literature and maybe even liked reading. Wes’s reassurance helped my seed for writing start to bud.

## Upperclassman Endeavors

During the upperclassmen years of my undergraduate degree, I started learning from what Hedgcock (2008) called the “gatekeepers and experts” (p. 33). The first expert I learned from was an adviser I met at an undergraduate writing conference where I had the privilege to present. This conference separates participants into different groups and assigns each group a professor from a prestigious, US college who specialized in writing. The participants present to the group and then receive feedback from the members and advisers. After, the adviser informed my group members that he had noticed many of our papers’ organization did not flow well, so he introduced us to a new way of organizing a paper. He told us that each topic should be organized in a way for the reader to best understand, not by the five-paragraph method. He informed us that many advanced writers do not use the five-paragraph method but create their own. We were challenged with looking at our papers and finding a new form of organization to best convey our message to the audience. It was at this stage when I struggled with the same situation as Hedgcock (2008): “my template stopped working, and my reading and writing strategies stopped working” (p. 34). Hedgcock (2008) discusses using your own intuition to figure out what professors want and using your own critical thinking skills to meet those expectations (p. 36). My conference encouraged me to evolve my abilities as a student, learning the same skills as Hedgcock discusses in his essay. I started considering my paper’s audience and used organization in my writing to accommodate what the reader may want or need to hear. I was able to apply these techniques when writing a paper in my undergraduate Shakespeare course. The class was taught by an English professor, but I was using a theatre director’s perspective to critique *Othello.* Knowing my professor was not a director, I ensured to include a background explaining my lens in my paper. Learning lessons regarding the writing process and writing for a specific audience helped my writing bud start to open up to the world around it.

My most influential writing lessons came from taking theatre history courses taught by Dr. Raimondo Genna. Dr. Genna applies the teaching method mentioned by Norton (2003), “encourage[ing] students to think of themselves as members of larger scholarly communities” (as cited in Hedgcock, 2008, p. 41). Without realizing it, I started using Smith’s (1988) idea of “reading like a writer” (as cited in Hedgcock, 2008, p. 42) and used the formatting in my research articles to influence how I organized my theatre history papers. I used this technique when writing a paper comparing President Trump to the title character in *Ubu Roi*. When reading an article, I read a quote that was not fully supported in the author’s argument. Being curious of the quote, I researched the ideas and wrote my paper supporting the claim and used the article’s quote to organize the main points in the paper. After attending the undergraduate writing conference three times and taking six courses with Dr. Genna, I evolved my personal system of learning and writing. I educationally connect with Hedgcock’s (2008) learned skill of “looking for lessons about how to succeed, rather than assuming that my professors… [will] deliver them systematically” (p. 39). Dr. Genna helped me learn the best way for me to approach writing and helped my little blossoming bud fully bloom and radiate.

## Conclusion

I have gone through a long process in my life to develop my writing abilities. Many of my lessons have been influences from other people in my life, like my mother, AP literature teacher, fellow writer, writing expert, and professor and adviser. I have had similar experiences as Hedgcock (2008) and believe his claim that “certain fundamental lessons may actually take place in routine encounters with texts and with fellow novices, gatekeepers, and experts” (p. 33). Through my many encounters in my life, I went from hating writing to letting my critical thinking skills and reading abilities influence my writing organization. It took me a while to figure out my writing process, so I like to call myself a late bloomer, but I am proud of the writing flower I have become.
