## Abstract

While there is limited research on theatre implemented in juvenile detention centers (JDCs), studies showing the benefits of theatre with this population exist. This annotated bibliography analyzes programs in various facilities to see the effects of theatre on adolescents in JDCs. The programs show that the adolescents can voice their feelings and opinions in theatre while normally their voices have been silenced. The articles demonstrate that adolescents learn how to express and understand emotions through theatre. This population also acquires skills to interpret others’ feelings and emotions through role reversal. The adolescents learn to understand more about themselves and how to control their behavior outside of the theatre programs. Performers, audiences, and facilitators increase their understanding of sex education, trauma, sexual assault, addiction, and substance abuse from these programs. Through looking at these successful programs and studies, it is evident that theatre is beneficial with this specific population and more time and research needs to be given to this field of study for better understanding and program growth.

Bertuca, T. (2005). Juveniles discover therapy in the theater. *The Corrections Connections.* Retrieved from http://www.corrections.com/articles/5048-juveniles-discover-therapy-in-the-theater

</div>

This article covers the past, present, and hopeful future of the Unusual Suspects Theatre Company work with juveniles. The company is trained by the correctional staff in how to deal with low and high-risk juvenile offenders and then helps the juveniles write, act, and produce their own work. Many of the plays deal with “poverty, abuse, drugs, parental abandonment, and racism” (Bertuca, 2005, para. 2). The plays, once finished, are performed in front of the peers of the juvenile performers. Fairman is quoted in saying “‘And sometimes… there isn’t a dry eye in the house” (Bertuca, 2005, para. 35). The program started in 1993 while working with foster care children, but evolved into working mainly with juvenile correctional facilities (Bertuca, 2005).

The results this program has seen are inspiring, but there has not been a lot of research done directly on theatre programs with juveniles, longitudinally. While research shows that theatre helps adults in prisons and lowers the violence and costs for the prisons, the same research has not been conducted to see if juvenile facilities see the same effects (Bertuca, 2005, para. 11). The staff of the facilities do report that gang violence decreases and the lives of the adolescents is improved. The adolescents learn a better way to deal with emotions and violence through a safe environment.

Bertuca (2005) lays out important information about the daily life of a juvenile that is not seen in most articles about juveniles using drama therapy or psychodrama or theatre in general. Bertuca (2005) gives the ages of the population, the amount of reoffenders, the average time for a juvenile to be in the facility, and the emotional problems the adolescents are facing. The article also takes an economic stand-point, which is unique when looking at theatre programs.

While the article has a heading entitled “Drama Therapy,” the information in the section deals a little with drama therapy, but mainly with the program. The program is therapeutic, but the workers in the companies are not trained drama therapists, which is important to note. The theatre program discussed in this article is discussed alongside rehabilitation programs, which makes me wonder how the line between arts in healthcare and drama therapy is seen in this particular situation.

Borden, R. (1940). The use of the psychodrama in an institution for delinquent girls. *Sociometry, 3*(1), 81-90.

</div>

This article discusses the use of psychodrama with delinquent girls in an institute in Hawthorne, New York. The researchers interviewed the girls, split them into two groups, and never told they were going to be doing psychodrama; they were told they were signing up for a personality training class.

The researchers used theatre with these girls for three re-training reasons: vocational training, social situations, and inter-personal relations. Through the three areas evaluated, Borden found that the girls increased their abilities. The psychodrama helped the girls increase their relationship abilities and dealing with different situations. The relationships between the girls in the institute and their families, boyfriends, and institution staff members were enhanced. This article breaks down some of the activities used with the adolescents. Borden created and facilitated her program and, even though it was published in 1940, it is still important research now-a-days.

Cossa, M. (1992). Acting out: A pilot project in drama therapy with adolescents. *The Arts in Psychotherapy, 19*. Retrieved from http://web.b.ebscohost.com.ezproxy.usd.edu/ehost/detail/detail?vid=7&sid=5eb5d122-0038-4c5c-9b12-1af4047484bd%40sessionmgr103&hid=118&bdata=JnNpdGU9ZWhvc3QtbGl2ZSZzY29wZT1zaXRl#AN=1992-32234-001&db=psyh

</div>

This article is relatively short, but it discusses the outline of a program called ACTING OUT that attempts to create “empowering environments for youth within which… an alternative peer culture could flourish” (Cossa, 1992, p. 53). There were two groups, the theatre group and the therapy group, that met for a total of 35 weeks. The article speaks mainly to the outside work the group did and does not give light to the therapy the girls did separate from their performance. The theatre group traveled to different schools and community locations doing improvisation to educate students in the area on various subjects. The group does “psychodrama, sociodrama, and roleplaying” during their therapy to help themselves through the process. There is a large success rate from this group performing and for the students involved in the program. The article does a case study on one of the participants in the group and how ACTING OUT helped her. The article warns that “it is still early to evaluate the effectiveness of this program” (Cossa, 1992, p. 55).

<div class="references">

Hensley, M. (2016). It’s just a play (and that’s enough). *Theatre Survey, 57*(3). Retrieved from http://web.a.ebscohost.com.ezproxy.usd.edu/ehost/detail/detail?vid=4&sid=e5658ae6-67db-4835-bc02-1fb0e8cfacdc%40sessionmgr4006&hid=4107&bdata=JnNpdGU9ZWhvc3QtbGl2ZSZzY29wZT1zaXRl#AN=117416337&db=aph

</div>

This article is about what Ten Thousand Things in the Twin Cities does and their goals. This company does performances for “correctional facilities… low-income centers… as well as… shows for the paying general public” (Hensley, 2016, p. 415). Hensley makes it very clear that the company does not attempt “to try to change anyone’s life” but wants the company members to learn from the audiences (Hensley, 2016, p. 415). This company first started when Hensley was bothered by the average theatre audience and wanted to find an “audience who cared, and audience for whom the stories [they] told might actually matter somehow to their lives” (Hensley, 2016, p. 416).

The company strives to not be condescending in any way because the audiences “often find themselves looked down upon by others” (Hensley, 2016, p. 416). The goal for the company is to expand their knowledge using the audiences they reach out to. The audiences they perform for understand the struggles of the characters more than the traditional audience does and they are more honest about what they see. The company is constantly challenged through the spaces they have to perform in and the limited costumes, props, and budget. Though the company deals with these struggles, they use it as a learning opportunity because, as this article shows, it is important to understand “nontraditional audiences” and it is important to reach out to them (Hensley, 2016, p. 417).

Karatas, Z. & Gokcakan, Z. (2009). A comparative investigation of the effects of cognitive-behavioral group practices and psychodrama on adolescent aggression. *Educational Sciences: Theory & Practice, 9*(3), 1441-1452. Retrieved from http://web.b.ebscohost.com.ezproxy.usd.edu/ehost/pdfviewer/pdfviewer?vid=1&sid=6c3d5641-eb3e-4bb6-a489-f1908cc84c9b%40sessionmgr103

</div>

This article covers the research of how cognitive-behavior and psychodrama, separately, influence adolescent aggression. The researchers split the group of 36 ninth grade students (found through administering The Aggression Scale to 200 ninth grade students) into three groups: a cognitive-behavior group, a psychodrama group, and a control group. They used cognitive-behavior techniques for 10 sessions on one group, psychodrama for 14 sessions on the second group and then did nothing with the control group except the beginning and ending survey. In the end, the results “showed that cognitive-behavioral approach was effective in decreasing aggression scores, except verbal aggression and psychodrama approach was effective in decreasing all aggression scores except verbal physical aggression” (Karatas & Gokcakan, 2009, p. 1441). The researchers also surveyed students after a 16 week period and found that the students’ aggression was still decreased, showing that the approaches were long-lasting.

The studies that the researchers reviewed in their article “show that psychodrama improves self-expression skills” (Karatas & Gokcakan, 2009, p. 1443). The articles reviewed also show that psychodrama is effective with adolescents by helping “adolescents cope with anger management problems” (Karatas & Gokcakan, 2009, p. 1443). Through the psychodrama sessions, the group warmed up with multiple games. The games played following mainly dealt with anger and aggression.

<div class="references">

Lauby, J., LaPollo, A., Herbst, J., Painter, T., Batson, H., Pierre, A., & Milnamow, M. (2010). Preventing AIDS through live movement and sound: Efficacy of theater-based HIV prevention intervention delivered to high-risk male adolescents in juvenile justice settings. *AIDS Education and Prevention, 22*(5). Retrieved from http://web.b.ebscohost.com.ezproxy.usd.edu/ehost/pdfviewer/pdfviewer?sid=c54e20c2-09d8-4ddd-85d0-1c63979b14fd%40sessionmgr103&vid=4&hid=118

</div>

This article discusses the experimental study of juvenile males in the juvenile justice system looking to see if the use of Preventing AIDS through Live Movement and Sound (PALMS) helps this population. This article concludes that PALMS helps increase the knowledge of the adolescents on HIV and that the participants that were involved in the program were more likely to use condoms during sexual activity. These findings were measured through self-report measures. These participants also showed signs of having a better attitude towards learning about AIDS and testing for AIDS.

PALMS has three sessions of HIV prevention where performers come in and “portray dramatic performances of real-life situations often faced by adolescents and young adults” (Lauby et al., 2010, p. 405). After the performance stage 1, the next two stages are focused on, “games and role-playing exercises [that] provided opportunities for participants to learn and practice communication and condom-use skills” (Lauby et al., 2010, p. 405). A limitation of this study is the measures were individually based and not biological through the participants . While it was really the only way to get answers, self-reports are not always the best way of getting information. The adolescents may want to over or under report their sexual activities.They still believe that the program is effective for adolescents, both audiences and participants.

<div class="references">

Palidofsky, M. & Stolbach, B. (2012). Dramatic healing: The evolution of a trauma-informed musical theatre program for incarcerated girls. *Journal of Child & Adolescent Trauma, 5*, 239-256.

</div>

This article discusses how musical theatre can be useful for incarcerated girls dealing with traumatic experiences. The article shows the growth of a “community-based urban theatre company’s innovative program” (p. 239). Starting out as a touring company that wrote their own plays, the group grew to helping incarcerated adolescents tell their story through the creation of their own plays. The research in this article supports the therapeutic effects of theatre on the girls dealing with the trauma. The facilitators in the program worked with the participants to create their own story based on community issues. The girls then create their characters based on their own traumatic experiences, used as back-story.

One idea to keep in mind when looking at this article is the gray area between doing theatre and doing therapy with these adolescents. Dealing specifically with trauma seems like more of a therapy session versus doing theatre. However, doing the theatre to create a voice for these girls is important because it is giving a voice to those who feel as though they have lost theirs.

This article does a nice job of laying out the program’s steps and goals with those steps. It also includes examples and then use those to discuss the issues the program faces.

<div class="references">

Smeijsters, H., Kil, J., Kurstjens, H., Welten, J., & Willemars, G. (2011). Arts therapies for young offenders in secure care – A practice-based research. *The Arts in Psychotherapy, 38*. Retrieved from http://web.a.ebscohost.com.ezproxy.usd.edu/ehost/detail/detail?vid=6&sid=e5658ae6-67db-4835-bc02-1fb0e8cfacdc%40sessionmgr4006&hid=4107&bdata=JnNpdGU9ZWhvc3QtbGl2ZSZzY29wZT1zaXRl#AN=58094851&db=aph

</div>

This article analyzes different arts therapies and their effects on young offenders in secure care (Smeijsters, Kil, Kurstjens, Welten, & Willemars, 2011). It discusses how each therapy has been researched already and the outcomes, strictly with this population. Smeijsters et al. (2011) interviewed different arts therapists to see what the therapists believe is the biggest problem with this population: self-image, emotions, interaction, and cognitions. The different therapies were then split up and discussed based on the modality’s best solution.

In this article, drama therapy was said to have the most effect on the emotions problem area for young offenders. Drama therapy helps teach people about their emotions and learn how to recognize them. Smeijsters et al. (2011) describe “the rationale behind this play form is that it trains young people in recognizing, properly and spontaneously, the emotion displayed and interpreting it and responding to it” (p. 45). Most of the games explained in this section are improvisational games used by a therapists to help understand where youths are struggling. These games help young offenders understand how to respond to authority figures and uses role reversal to allow each person to understand situations from different perspectives.

<div class="references">

Storycatchers Theatre. (2017). *Storycatchers Theatre.* Retrieved 13 February 2017, from http://www.storycatcherstheatre.org/

</div>

This is a website for a company that has been nationally recognized for its youth programs. It was awarded the National Arts & Humanities Youth Program Award from Michelle Obama in 2013. This company creates their own musical theatre productions based on personal stories. They says that “participants examine their lives, relationships, environments, choices and the consequences of those choices” and they use these brainstorms as their productions. Storycatchers mainly works with “youth who live in under-resourced urban neighborhoods or in juvenile justice facilities” (Storycatchers Theatre, 2017, “Who We Are,” para. 3). There are many goals this group has for the youth involved through this program. A few of these goals are to “learn writing and performance skills, achieve personal development goals, improve peer and family relationships, and acquire practical knowledge that can promote success” (Storycatchers Theatre, 2017, “Who We Are,” para. 3).

There are four different groups that are ran by Storycatchers Theatre: Fabulous Females, Firewriters, Temporary LockDown, and Changing Voices. Fabulous Females is a group of incarcerated girls that start writing annually in January, do previews in the summer, and do four performances in November. Firewriters does the same as Fabulous Females except is an all boys group. Temporary LockDown works strictly with boys who “face charges in adult court” (Storycatchers Theatre, 2017, “Temporary LockDown,” para. 1). Changing Voices works with young people who have been recently released from the court system to help them stay on the right track. This website has great information regarding the aspects to consider when working with juveniles. It is a great example of a working program that helps juveniles through theatre.

<div class="references">

Taylor, J. (2011). Sculpting empowerment: Theatre in a juvenile facility and beyond. In J. Shailor (Ed.), *Performing new lives: Prison theatre* (pp. 197-212). Philadelphia, PA: Jessica Kingsley Publishers.

</div>

Julia Taylor took a college course that that worked together with the Prison Creative Arts Project (PCAP) that places people into facilities with incarcerated populations. One idea that Taylor lines up with that the PCAP believes is that “Creating art is dangerous. Art gives a voice to those who have lost theirs” (Taylor, 2011, p. 198). Taylor was given the assignment of working with another student to develop a theatre program at Vista Maria, which was a school for juvenile girls. This article is fully about her experience doing theatre, specifically improvisation, with these girls.

Taylor does a good job of explaining how the juvenile girls felt during their time in the program and the tactics/games the two facilitators used to engage the girls. Taylor writes of the empowerment improvisation gives to the girls that may feel as though they have lost their freedom in the detention center; “locked away from a society that has never been kind to them… theatre in prison is an escape from the madness” (Taylor, 2011, p. 205; Taylor, 2011, p. 207). After meetings using improvisational games, Taylor, her partner, and the adolescents started to create a storyline for a play. Through these games and creative process Taylor watches the participates grow closer to one another and trust each other more through the games played. Taylor has a breaking point when she realizes that she is only a step in the lives of the girls living in the facility. This observation is key in what people do as AIH because even a little time doing theatre can really show someone that you care. This article is empowering in the realization that doing theatre, “individuals become free, free from our histories that keep us confined in a one-dimensional version of ourselves” (Taylor, 2011, p. 211).
