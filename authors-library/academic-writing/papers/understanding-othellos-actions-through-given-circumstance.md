<p class="paper-title">Understanding Othello’s Actions through Given Circumstance</p>

All people have a past that helps mold them to who they will become in the future; this concept also applies to characters in plays. Every character has a past that shapes who they are and the actions they take. While this past does not take place on stage, it is referred to in the dialogue. An example of this idea can be seen in William Shakespeare’s *Hamlet* where the title character is upset about the murder of his father and the wedding between his mother and uncle. These past actions cause frustration and grief and, when combined with his father’s wishes for revenge, fuel Hamlet to kill Claudius. These actions can be utilized in a play when a director and their actors are working with the script. It is important to think about the characters’ past because there may be information that fuels a character’s decisions later, such as Hamlet’s grief. While the audience may not fully understand the character’s past, it is important for the director and actor to know this information to create specific choices for the production’s overall theme. William Shakespeare’s *Othello* is an example of how past information helps readers and audiences understand key actions that take place, such as Othello murdering Desdemona. For the purpose of this paper, a character’s environment and past is defined as given circumstances, which are better described later in the essay. Through these given circumstances, plays can be understood differently. Othello’s actions throughout William Shakespeare’s *Othello* can be better understood through analyzing the given circumstances of the play and Othello himself.

Francis Hodge and Michael McLain, in *Play Directing Analysis, Communication, and Style,* lay out the framework to help directors analyze plays and communicate their ideas and styles to others involved with the production process. In chapter three, “The Foundation and Façade of the Playscript Given Circumstance and Dialogue,” Hodge and McLain define “given circumstances” as “concern\[ing\] all material in a playscript that delineates the environment – or the special ‘world’ of the play – in which the action takes place” (15). As the authors point out, “\[d\]ialogue is the only reliable source of given circumstances” (16). The dialogue is the primary source for any of the information in the play and, for Shakespeare, it is the only real source. The authors, using the text as evidence, then break down given circumstances into three sections: environmental factors, previous action, and polar attitudes (15). This paper does not use polar attitudes for analysis and limits the number of environmental factors utilized.

Environmental factors, as related to given circumstances, can be found, according to Hodge and McLain, in five different ways: geographical location, date, economic environment, political environment, social environment, and religious environment (16-7). While all factors are important to consider when analyzing a play, many plays have an emphasis on a limited number of factors. Hodge and McLain state, “Some plays will involve all of these categories; others will emphasize some of the categories but not all” (17). For the purposes of *Othello*, this paper picks out the key categories of political, social, and religious environments. Political environments look at how the government affects the lives of the characters in the play (16). These may include laws created by the government and the responsibilities given to those who hold political power. Political factors can be found in the text of the play itself, but can be researched to receive a better understanding of the given circumstances based on the period of the play (17). The social environment deals with the “social institutions under which the characters live” (17). These factors will come from other characters in the play and their acceptance of different actions in the play (17). The social environment is the social understandings of behavior. Finally, religious environments are not particularly connected to a church, but are the “formal and informal psychological controls” that affect various aspects of a play (17). A good example explaining religious environments can be seen in C.S. Lewis’ *Mere Christianity* when he describes the idea of the “Law of Nature” (4). Lewis defines this as “some kind of Law or Rule of fair play or decent behavior or morality” (4). While Lewis goes on to discuss how these should be implemented into Christianity, these universally understood “laws of nature” are the best way to describe how a director may see the religious environment of a play. While analyzing a play, such as *Othello,* and its characters, it is important to understand political, social, and religious environments through given circumstance.

The second element of given circumstances utilized in this paper is previous action. To understand previous action, one needs to understand what Hodge and McLain call “present action” (17). Present action includes the actions that the audience may see happening in the play (17). The given circumstances come from the previous action, or events that “happened before the present action begins” (17). Another way of differentiating these two is knowing the different between plot and story. Plot is the actions that happen during the play using cause and effect, while the story is everything that has happened in the character’s life. An example of this can be seen in Sophocles’ play *Oedipus the King.* The plot of this play is relatively simple. Oedipus takes on the mystery of finding Laius’s killer. Once he finds out it is himself, Jocasta kills herself, Oedipus stabs out his eyes and is thrown out of the city. The story of Oedipus tracks back to when Oedipus is born, given up by his birth mother and father, adopted and raised by Polybus all the way until he is thrown out of the city (Sophocles 1737-76). The previous actions in *Oedipus the King* are Oedipus being born, given away, and adopted. When looking at given circumstances, previous action is analyzing the storyline of characters. One factor of previous action that is important to note is the relationships that may have existed before the play’s start, Oedipus and Jocasta’s marriage, for example. As all given circumstances are, previous action will be found in the dialogue of the play.

Using this definition of given circumstances as framework, it can then be applied to plays to understand what the characters have gone through. The given circumstances can then be applied to characters to better understand why they perform the actions they do in a play. The best way to approach this application is to start with the information written in the text.

Understanding the given circumstances in William Shakespeare’s *Othello* help readers, actors, and directors better comprehend Othello’s actions in the play. The aspects of Othello’s life regarding his given circumstances are seen influencing his decisions about Cassio, Iago, Desdemona, and himself.

One previous action in *Othello* that sets up the foundation for the rest of the given circumstance is the fact that Othello is a well-respected General (1.2.55). Othello has a vast military background, as he describes in 1.3, lines 134-45. Analyzing these lines, Othello has struggled through many events in his life and not only in his career. These events provide his given circumstances. Othello says that he has experienced “battles, sieges, fortune” (1.3.130). Othello recalls of telling tales, “Of moving accidents by flood and field;/ Of hairbreadth scapes i’th’ imminent deadly breach” (1.3.135-7). He has been a prisoner of war and then sold into slavery (1.3.132-3). He has lived while walking through many different types of terrains (1.3.135-6). These are a hand full of trials that Othello has experienced. Given Othello’s past, his title of General is understandable. Others in the play support the belief that Othello is qualified for his position by both their compliments and how they address him. In 1.3, the Duke tells Desdemona’s father, “your son-in-law is far more fair than black” (1.3.287). This line gives evidence to the respect that the Duke has for Othello. The Oxford English Dictionary defines “fair” as beautiful, meaning that the Duke believes Othello is more beautiful that he is black. While “beautiful” can be applied to appearances, the Duke is also giving credit to Othello and respecting him as a person. This comparison shows how much respect Othello has from those around him, making his position more credible and permanent. The people who selected Othello to be General do not second guess their decision in the least. The play provides evidence of the given circumstances based on previous action that Othello has the qualifications for his position and many are pleased with his rank as General.

Due to Othello’s life surrounded by the military, his environmental factors relate to the military. Therefore, his political, social, and religious ideas come from the military. These environmental factors specifically impact four main decisions Othello makes in the play: removing Cassio as Lieutenant, trusting Iago, killing Desdemona, and committing suicide.

Othello’s decision to remove Cassio as his lieutenant mainly draws from two environmental components of given circumstance: social and religious. Othello understands the military’s social environment’s standards and believes others will or should adhere to these values as well. Robert Barret’s article “The Theory and Practice of Modern Wars” (1598), outlines the expectations of the military during the Early Modern English period. Barret writes, “\[i\]n any wise let him \[soldiers\] eschew the beastly vice of drunkenness” (301). It is socially unacceptable for military members to be drunk. While this is not a law of the military, it is a social understanding that people in the military should follow. If a military member does drink, they should at least have control of their intake to prevent themselves from “causing… whole provinces to revolt from their Princes, caused by the disorder of such insatiable drunkards” (301). The military society socially believe that soldiers should have control over the amount they drink. Military members who do not comply with this unwritten rule are a bad example in the eyes of both their comrades and society, because of the underlining moral consensus providing the foundation for that de facto rule.

The military in the Shakespearean period also had important principles to follow that enforced the social standards. These morals parallel the current United States Air Force’s morals of putting integrity first, service before self, and having excellence in all they do. The moral codes show the religious given circumstance of the period. The morals in the Early Modern English period are outlined by Thomas Styward (1581) when he writes the “six principles of a soldier… as ‘silence, obedience, secretness, *sobriety* \[emphasis added\], hardiness, truth” (310). Sobriety is a moral principle that soldiers should understand and follow. Through Othello’s social and religious standards, Othello wants people who work with him to also follow these standards. Othello believing that military members should restrain from alcohol is important to his given circumstances due to Cassio drinking in the play. When Othello catches Cassio drunk in 2.3, Othello needs to show those who follow him that he will enforce social and religious standards. To do this, he needs to remove Cassio from his position as lieutenant. If Othello does not abide by the standards of the military, then he will not be respected by other officials and will not be adhering to what he has learned. Othello’s loyalty to his word and vow to the military principles also helps explain why Othello never accepts Cassio back into his position, no matter how much Desdemona begs. Othello cannot be swayed by Desdemona, but he is viewed as manipulated by Iago.

Othello appears manipulated by Iago and this can be understood through previous action, political environment, and religious environment. Iago is an example of a military relationship Othello has prior to the play’s beginning. This relationship can be found both through Othello and Iago’s lines throughout the play. Weston Babcock creates a compelling argument in his article “Iago – An Extraordinary Honest Man” that Othello does not respect Iago as a military comrade and friend due to the way Othello addresses Iago throughout the play. Babcock’s argument is founded on the definition of “honest” during the time and the use of thou and you in the play. While this argument is certainly interesting, it does not support why Othello trusts Iago in the play.

In support of Othello and Iago’s relationship, they are comrades prior to the play’s start and this understanding can be drawn from Iago’s line, “And I – of whom his eyes had seen the proof/ At Rhodes, at Cyprus, and on others’ grounds” (1.1.26-7). Iago has a connection to Othello on the battlefield, making Othello easier to manipulate because of the personal connection. While Babcock argues that Othello does not consider Iago honest, it is difficult to agree with this because Othello believes Iago throughout the play. If Othello did not trust or believe Iago is honest, he would not have believed Desdemona cheated on him and have killed her. It takes a real comrade relationship to believe a fellow co-worker over a wife. Iago says that Othello has seen the great warrior that Iago can be. Iago stating this relationship can help create Othello and Iago’s past relationship and the military being the main background. Othello may trust Iago because they both have seen war together and this creates a different kind of bond. Iago backing up Othello in times of war is important to the bond the two share.

While there might not be enough evidence through Iago’s words alone, Othello also gives evidence to this relationship. Multiple times throughout the play, Othello says, “Honest Iago” (1.3.91, 2.3.56, 5.2.72). Other times Othello refers to Iago as honest, but the root of the honesty between Othello and Iago comes from their military background together. If Iago and Othello were not comrades, Othello would not trust him with the full amount he does in the play. Their relationship is enhanced by Iago in 1.2 when he sets up a scenario to save Othello. Iago prepares Roderigo to seem as though he was coming to attack, so Iago can prove that he is still loyal to Othello. Desdemona’s father yells to Roderigo, “down with him, thief!” and Iago responds by saying, “come, sir, I am for you” (1.2.57-8). Iago’s leap into action is to show Othello that Iago is still there for him and willing to protect him, no matter what the circumstances. Showing his loyalty to Othello helps show how Othello trusts Iago throughout the play and how it appears Othello is easy to manipulate.

Another idea concerning Iago and Othello’s relationship is Iago hinting that Othello may owe him something. Iago uses a metaphor of a debtor and creditor. The metaphor can be hinting to Othello owing Iago. If something had happened at war that Iago did have to save Othello, Iago may feel as though Othello owes him. If Othello feels in debt to Iago, he will want to flatter him more through words, explaining the lines “Honest Iago” multiple times throughout the play (1.3.91, 2.3.56, 5.2.72). Iago saving Othello can also enhance the trust Othello has for Iago. Othello, in the previous action, also passed over Iago as being the lieutenant and chose Cassio, even though “three great ones of the city, / In personal suit to make \[Iago Othello’s\] lieutenant” (1.1.7-8). Through this given circumstance, Othello may feel badly about passing over Iago for this position. While feeling badly, Othello may feel as though he owes Iago his trust. The trust is only increased once Iago removes Cassio from the lieutenant position and becomes the new lieutenant and Othello’s right-hand-man.

Through Iago and Othello’s military friendship, Othello believes Iago would follow the political environment of the military, the military laws. Given the time that the play was written and with the military as the political environment in the play, looking at how members of the military would have behaved and understood the world during the Early Modern English period influences how the play can be understood. People in the military have a different mindset on life and the people around them, as Kim Hall’s book *Othello Texts and Contexts* includes in her chapter, “Masculinity and Military Life.” While some of the chapter discusses her views on the matter, most of the chapter consists of parts of various book or articles. One passage she includes is written by Thomas Styward called “The Laws and Constitutions of the Field”. This section includes the laws military men of Early Modern England are to follow such as “there shall no soldier… hold or keep any whispering… without license from the chief Captain upon pain of the loss of his life” (311). This law pertains to Othello as he knows to not keep information from others. With Othello understanding this law, he also believes that others in the military will do the same and not break the law, or else it will cause “the loss of his life” (311). Othello believes Iago would hold true to this law due to their past relationship in combat and Iago’s honesty.

Othello’s religious environment is important to consider when analyzing his relationship with Iago. Kim Hall states, in the Early Modern English period, that a military title is more than having the responsibility, rather “rank… marks a series of relationships” (296). Military members who work together through battles create a family. Within a family, people are supposed to act a certain way. In her book, Hall includes multiple articles that discuss “how soldiers should behave towards each other” (296). Supporting Hall’s claim, a belief for military members discussed by Robert Barret (1598) is that soldiers should “observe Justice, Loyalty, constancy, patience, and silence, and above all, obedience” (302). These are morals held by those in the military that should be followed as soon as one takes oath. By Iago attempting to save Othello, as discussed previously, he also shows that he has these morals, especially loyalty. Thomas and Leonard Digges (1579) seem to disagree with the possibility of military members achieving these goals, given “that an *Englishman* will be trained in a few weeks to be a perfect Soldier” (309). They argue that it is not possible for military members to gain the religious morals of the military in such short amount of time. While their argument is understandable, I firmly believe that Othello understands these traits given his trusted military rank and his extensive experience. If Othello follows these morals, he will also believe his comrades will follow them as well, especially those he has been in combat with multiple times, Iago. Overall, Othello believes these standards as part of his religious environment that should be upheld by all military members. Through Iago attempting to prove to Othello that he has these morals, Othello believes his comrade Iago follows these morals, making Othello seem easy to manipulate.

Othello and Iago’s relationship being strong causes Othello to seem easy to manipulate throughout the play, especially with Othello’s beliefs of military comrades being honest and loyal. The audience believes Iago is tricking Othello due to the audience hearing Iago’s soliloquies. Othello does not see these actions as manipulation, he sees the actions as though Iago is trying to help him as a friend or family member. When looking at the manipulation through Othello’s point of view, Othello is only listening to a trusting friend and comrade that he has known longer than his wife. Othello would not have been as easy to manipulate had his relationship with Iago had been poor, as Weston Babcock argues. Due to Othello thinking so highly of Iago and his social and religious given circumstances, Iago easily fools Othello. The main scene where Othello wrongfully trusts Iago is 3.3, where Iago convinces Othello that Desdemona is cheating on him with Cassio (3.3.89-274). By believing this deceit as fact, Othello no longer trusts Desdemona and views her as a traitor that has broken the military law, shown through Othello’s political environment.

Othello not trusting Desdemona can be tracked back to the beginning of the play when she tells Othello she wants to go with him to battle. This moment is when Othello starts struggling to differentiate his military family from his home family. Analyzing Othello and Desdemona’s previous relationships, it is important to consider their previous action and social environment; Othello’s stories and personality helped him win the heart of Desdemona, but not specifically on purpose (1.3.164). Othello successfully won Desdemona’s heart through his stories and through her fascination with his past (1.3.145-50). She sympathized with him and his adventures (1.3.156-7). The connection Desdemona feels towards Othello is not with his personality, but with his stories and his experiences. The foundation of Desdemona and Othello’s relationship is with Othello’s military career. Having this foundation, Othello’s separation between home life and the military becomes difficult. When the Duke tells Othello to go to battle, Desdemona demands that she go along with him (1.3.245). Through Desdemona inserting herself into the war, she is also inserting herself into Othello’s military life. Othello starts to view Desdemona as a comrade. Through Desdemona’s invasion, Othello cannot easily separate these two important parts of his life. Without this separation, Othello is constantly mindful of his military family and cannot create a normal family idea. Othello combines his political environment with his social environment. While these two environments should have different rules and regulations, Othello applies his military rules to both situations. However, if Othello views Desdemona as a comrade, would their relationship be viewed as homosexual on Othello’s behalf? This question opens the door to the idea of Othello and Desdemona consummating their marriage.

An important idea that also needs to be understood by the previous action is the idea that Othello and Desdemona never consummated their marriage. T. G. A. Nelson and Charles Haines strongly argue in their article *Othello’s Unconsummated Marriage* that “Othello fails to consummate his marriage with Desdemona because of the pressures placed on him during the couple’s turbulent first night in Cyprus” (1). Nelson and Haines create a nice foundation that Othello and Desdemona do not consummate their marriage to support their argument that this lack of consummation creates “misery and frustration” that then causes Othello to be in “a state in which his judgement is fatally impaired” and thus causing Othello to murder Desdemona (1). While Othello’s sexual frustration causing him to kill his wife is certainly well argued, I believe Othello’s actions are drawn from a different source. David Schalkwyk in his article *Othello’s Consummation* takes Nelson and Haines’s foundation and expands this idea to explore the “philosophical problem” caused by the relationship due to the couple not consummating their marriage (206). As Schalkwyk does, my argument will take Nelson and Haines idea of Othello and Desdemona not consummating their marriage and expand on it to understand Othello’s actions in the play. Othello and Desdemona not consummating their marriage provides more evidence towards Othello viewing Desdemona as a comrade and not a wife.

Othello believing that Desdemona committed adultery, is the motivation behind killing her. Othello views Desdemona as a traitor to his military family. As a part of the military political environment of the time, “no man… shall commit adultery with married wives… \[and\] shall without mercy be punished with death” (Barret 312). Once Othello believes Iago’s claim that Desdemona is having an affair with Cassio, Othello sees Desdemona as a person corrupting his military family. Othello and Desdemona never consummating their marriage allows Othello to have a stronger bond to the military than to Desdemona. After Desdemona wedges herself into Othello’s military family, she then breaks the duties and responsibilities that a comrade should uphold. Desdemona does not follow the political and religious environmental factors that Othello has grown up with and understands by committing adultery and not remaining loyal. Desdemona is viewed as a traitor to the system and this treachery should not go undisciplined. Othello, being the general of the military, needs to put a stop to the deceiving party. As per Othello’s military laws, Desdemona “shall without mercy be punished with death” (312).

Having the military law being the background motivation, Othello’s thoughts during the murder reveal a different idea. As stated previously, Othello has been through a lot in his military life and these experiences should be considered in his given circumstances. In Thomas Hicks’ master’s thesis titled *“Undone”: a Novella Using Othello to Discuss Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder*, he utilizes Othello to explain to veterans the symptoms and effects of PTSD. While my paper is not arguing whether Othello has PTSD, the idea of Othello’s military past causing PTSD symptoms certainly comes into effect. Hicks argues that through the events Othello claims to have gone through, it is possible for him to suffer from PTSD symptoms (4). Othello not having a foundation for overcoming trauma makes him more susceptible to having PTSD and the disorder influencing Othello killing Desdemona (5). Othello having PTSD explains more fully why he kills Desdemona, even though he may have regrets about it. Hicks discusses the valid story of “a veteran… who could not stop himself from committing violence one his fight-or-flight response kicked in” (5). Iago initiates Othello’s fight-or-flight response by getting Othello to believe that Desdemona cheated on him. In the scene when Othello is killing Desdemona, Othello states, “I that am cruel am yet merciful;/ I would not have thee linger in thy pain” (5.2.90-1). While Othello says he may be cruel, it is not possible for him to stop this murder of Desdemona, even if he wanted to. Like the story Hicks discusses, it is not easy for a military member to stop violence once their muscle memory has already kicked in to the possibility. After murdering Desdemona, Othello realizes that she was not the one disobeying the laws of the military. While Iago is a traitor, Othello also realizes the real traitor is himself because he killed Desdemona on the wrong terms.

Emilia tells Othello that Iago lied to Othello and has tricked him into believing that Desdemona cheated with Cassio (5.2.219-29). Having incorrect information from Iago, Othello understands that Desdemona was a true, loyal part of Othello’s military family and home family. By Othello killing Desdemona, he breaks his own rules of his military standard. While working through the just thing to do, Othello attempts to kill Iago, the source of the lies, to follow the laws of the military (5.2.280). After this attempt, the only justice to make up for Othello killing Desdemona is for Othello to also kill himself. Understanding Othello’s political given circumstances, it is just for him to kill himself. He commands himself to rid his military family the trouble of having to deal with the killing themselves by saying, “I took by th’ throat the circumcised dog/ And smote him thus \[*He stabs himself.*\]” (5.2.348-9). Through Othello’s political given circumstance, he rights the wrongs committed in the play.

Through the dissection of Othello’s given circumstances, it is more apparent as to why Othello performs the actions he does throughout the play. To prove his military position and fulfill his military duty, Othello must remove Cassio from being Lieutenant. Due to Othello and Iago’s military connection, Othello believes Iago more than Desdemona, making him seem as though he can be easily manipulated. Othello kills Desdemona due to her treachery to Othello’s military family, of which she makes herself a part of. Finally, after discovering Desdemona is innocent, Othello kills himself to do his military duty because of his own treason to his military family. Overall, it is important to take given circumstances into consideration when looking at the actions a character does during a play. While the characters are not real and nothing happens before the start of the play, their given circumstances still come into effect when they perform the actions during the play.
