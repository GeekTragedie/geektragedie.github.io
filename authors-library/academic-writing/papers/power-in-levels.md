<p class="paper-title">Power in Levels</p>

*Jesus Christ Superstar* is a 1970’s rock opera in which Andrew Lloyd Webber created the music and Tim Rice wrote the lyrics. The University of South Dakota performed this opera as part of their 2016-2017 academic season. I attended this production Sunday, November 20th and was impressed by the talent the script allows and the story the actors told. Many scenes in this show had immense effects on the audience and moved the audience in different ways. The show took the story of Jesus’s last seven days and help shine a new light on the people in charge of Jesus’s crucifixion. One scene that I believe largely effected the audience was the whipping scene due to the lighting, direction, and musical performance.

The direction at the beginning of the scene shows the audience who has power and who is isolated in their own decision making. The whipping scene opens with Pontius Pilate asking why Jesus has returned. Caiaphas tells Pontius Pilate that Jesus needs to be crucified and that Pontius Pilate is the only one that has the power to demand this deed to be done. While Pontius Pilate has does have the authority to crucify Jesus, the placement of the chorus and Caiaphas help emphasize where the power truly comes from. Caiaphas and the chorus are on a higher level than Pontius Pilate, showing that they have more power than Pontius Pilate. Caiaphas and the chorus being on a different level than Pontius Pilate also isolates Pilate, showing that she is alone in her decisions. The chorus also chants, “We have no king but Caesar,” “Crucify him,” and “We need him crucified, It’s all you have to do.”  With the pressure from Caiaphas and the chorus, Pontius Pilate agrees to flog Jesus to relieve the pressures from those that can turn Pontius Pilate in for not following the law. The use of these levels show the social pressure put on Pontius Pilate to flog Jesus. This staging helps the audience empathizing with Pontius Pilate for doing the job she was given.

The blocking before the whipping scene also shows an isolation of Pontius Pilate and Jesus through the use of levels and location upstage and down stage. The chorus and Caiaphas are all upstage of Pilate; this placement isolates Pilate. Through this isolation, the audience can better understand that Pilate has a different view of Jesus than the other level of characters. While Pilate sings that he does not understand why she must crucify Jesus, it is more clear to the audience that there is a different point of view by isolating Pilate, physically, on stage. Pilate is on the same level as Jesus at the beginning of the scene. By Pilate being on the same level as Jesus, it shows that she identifies with him more and is on his side. After the pressure of the chorus and Caiaphas, Pilate commands that Jesus be flogged. During the flogging, Pilate remains on the same level as Jesus, showing that she still does not want to crucify him. Jesus and Pilate are shown as isolated until Jesus does not tell Pilate to not crucify him. Once Pilate commands Jesus to be crucified, she moves to a higher level with the chorus and Caiaphas. By moving to this level, it physically shows her joining their side and abandoning Jesus, leaving Jesus as the only one isolated, crawling around the stage.

The flogging itself certainly had an effect on the audience due to its staging, tempo change, and duration. When the whipping scene begins, the man whipping is also at a higher level than Pontius Pilate, showing that this man also has power over Pontius Pilate. The real people who whip Jesus is the chorus and Caiaphas and this is shown in USD’s production using levels. After the flogging, these ideas change as Pilate changes levels. Jesus is isolated center stage as the flogger moves to the highest level. There is a large space between the flogger and Jesus; this space was smart on blocking behalf because the flogging still had a large impact, but the audience was not scared for the safety of the actor. The flogger was higher than Jesus, showing that he had more power. Jesus was center stage and everyone on stage was looking at him; this drew the audience’s eyes to Jesus during the action. With the audience watching Jesus, they viewed his pain from every whip. There was a change in tempo leading into the flogging. The scene built up and the chorus kept getting faster. Once the flogging started, the tempo slowed down. This tempo change startles the audience into feeling every deliberate flog. By feeling every flog, duration of the flogging is also important. While the number of flogs is in the music, the fact that they flog Jesus 39 times effects the audience. The more times the flogging happens, the more the audience can comprehend, in the moment, what is really happening. The chorus represents society. Society caused the death of Jesus and caused all the pain he suffered. After this comprehension, the audience continues to watch the flogging, knowing it is the chorus, representing society, that caused it.

After the flogging is finished and the audience feels awful, Jesus does not tell Pilate to not crucify him. The chorus chants “Remember Caesar. You have a duty to keep the peace, so crucify him. Remember Caesar. You’ll be demoted. You’ll be departed. Crucify him.” A combination of Jesus not answering Pilate and Pilate being threatened, she begrudgingly joins the mass against Jesus and Jesus crawls around stage. Through Jesus being the lowest level and the other characters being the highest, emphasizes both his isolation and lack of power over the situation.

Overall, the use of levels, focal points, and staging largely effected the audience in different ways throughout the song “Trial before Pilate.” The direction uses levels to show who has power throughout the scene. The staging also shows who is isolated at each instance in the scene. Finally, the flogging itself, using tempo and duration, has certain effects on the audience. In the end, the audience understand the logic behind Pilate crucifying Jesus and feels the responsibility for the death of Jesus.
