## Key Concepts and Terms

Behavioral therapies are all about what the client is *doing,* and to some extent, what they are thinking (thinking came to be seen as a behavior).

Pathology is assumed to occur when clients *learn* maladaptive behaviors.

There is no theory of personality associated with behavior therapies. People are assumed to learn, make decisions, and change through stimulus and response, reinforcers, observing others, and trying new things.

Classical conditioning: learning which occurs when a reaction becomes associated with a stimulus due to the process of a reflex occurring at the same time the stimulus is introduced (such as when a neutral stimulus like the color orange becomes associated with terror because one was hit by a school bus).

Unconditioned Stimulus (UCS): Something we do not need to be trained to react to (food, cold, pain, etc.)

Unconditioned Response (UCR): A response we do not need to be trained to have (salivation, shivering, moving away, etc.)

Conditioned Stimulus (CS): Something we can be trained to react to (a light, a bell, money, etc.)

Conditioned Response (CR): A response we are conditioned to have in relation to the CS (shivering at the sound of a bell).

Operant conditioning: learning which occurs because reinforcers were introduced. An assumption is made that positive reinforcers will increase the chances of the behavior occurring again, while punishments will decrease the chance of the behavior happening again.

Social Cognitive Theory/Social Learning Theory: a theory introduced by Albert Bandura, which emphasized the social nature of learning. Bandura believed that we learn not only from our own experiences (classical or operant conditioning), but that we can learn from watching others. His theory was interested in themes such as how children learn aggression, how people develop a sense of self-efficacy, and how our self-talk influences our behaviors. Social cognitive theory also emphasized that emotions play a role in behaviors (Bandura was himself not comfortable calling himself a strict behaviorist, though he is usually listed under this category).

## Goal of Therapy

- Identify maladaptive behaviors and change them.
- *Note that other complaints/goals are operationalized into behaviors (e.g. “I feel depressed” could be operationalized into “I stay home all of the time, avoid people, tell myself that I am worthless, and forget to take my medication”). The behaviors are then the focus of treatment.*

## Common Applications

- Specific fears and phobias (spiders, heights, airplanes, dogs, etc.).
- Social anxiety and agoraphobia.
- Behavioral challenges in children (tantrums, aggression, school refusal, etc.).
- Obsessive compulsive disorder and ritualized behaviors.
- Trauma responses.

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Shaping*: the counselor and client reinforce new behavior via gradual steps toward a goal. For example, if a client has a goal of getting on an airplane without fear, they might start with watching planes take off, and then reward their step in the right direction.

*Modeling*: the counselor demonstrates new behavior (for children, they would likely teach the parents to model it). If there is no model available, symbolic modeling can done via videos, books, etc.

*Vicarious reinforcement:* the client imagines the benefits of changing their behavior and establishes goals to seek these rewards. For example, they are reinforced to continue working hard because they have seen others at their job get raises and they imagine they could get one too.

*Self-reinforcement:* the client sets up a system of rewards for changing their behavior; when the client accomplishes a goal, they get something (day at the spa, new clothes, etc.). For children, this often looks like sticker charts, prizes, etc.

*Encouragement, teaching, coaching:* the counselor builds up the client’s sense of efficacy by reminding them of their abilities and past successes, teaching new skills which the client can use, and coaching the clients on how to maneuver expected challenges.

*Imaginal flooding:* with the counselor’s help, the client imagines the feared stimuli and practices sitting in the full fear until it fades away on its own.

*Systematic desensitization:* like imaginal flooding, this technique depends upon imagined (and usually, progressing to actual) exposure to feared stimuli. Unlike imaginal flooding, systematic desensitization is done gradually. The client creates a hierarchy of fears (e.g. looking at a plane, seeing a plane take off, boarding a plane, riding a plane) and works through the hierarchy gradually, learning to calm anxiety at each stage before moving on.

*Virtual reality*: exposure to the feared stimuli without the actual stimuli, such as through a video game or virtual reality computer program.

*Role-playing:* the client tries on new behaviors in session, to learn how to use them outside of session (e.g. assertive communication).
