## Key Concepts and Terms

Adler believed that people could not be broken down into parts. As such, his model of personality was one of holism, or the belief that emotions, thoughts, motivations, memories, etc., all work together all of the time and cannot be pulled apart.

Purpose: Adler believes that all behaviors serve a purpose. If a client is doing something which is no longer working for them, there is some reason that this behavior developed. There may even be consequences to giving up the behavior (e.g. losing support because one no longer acts helpless).

Social interest: Adler believed that humans are social animals with an inherent desire to connect with others. He believed that when people have high social interest (high investment in relationships) and engage in pro-social behaviors, they can be considered healthy.

Inferiority/Superiority: Adler believed that people have a natural tendency to try to overcome feelings of inferiority. Feelings of inferiority can be caused by things such as physical ailments, social struggles, or emotional states. Healthy striving for superiority (mastery) means identifying a personal struggle and working toward improvement, utilizing pro-social behaviors. Inferiority becomes pathological (an *inferiority complex*), when the individual gets stuck in their sense of inferiority and rejects personal responsibility and pro-social behavior (giving up, embracing the helplessness, etc.). A superiority complex occurs if the individual attempts to overcompensate for their inferiority in arrogant, narcissistic ways.

Birth order and family constellation: Adlerian counselors are known for their interest in birth order. The family is assumed to act as a microcosm for society at large. Early relationships with siblings and parents establish lifestyle patterns and areas of inferiority, which will play out later in life.

Lifestyle: the ways in which one lives, believes, self-understands, etc., based upon early experiences and social adaptations (especially to one’s family of origin).

Basic mistakes: the self-defeating aspects of one’s lifestyle, or the core beliefs which are causing hardship (e.g. “I’m not good enough,” “life is too hard for me,” acting helpless, etc.).

## Goals of Therapy

- Increase social interest and social behaviors.
- Identify and change unhelpful core beliefs.
- Overcome feelings of inferiority, in a pro-social, growth-oriented manner.

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Acting “as if”*—the therapist encourages the client to explore one of their “useful fictions” (an idea that may not be “objectively true,” but is helpful for organizing thoughts and behaviors). The client is then encouraged to act as if the fiction were true.

*Might sound like:*

- “What if you spent all day acting as if it were true that you are good enough?”
- “Pretend for a moment that you are in fact confident and assertive; now tell me what you need in your marriage.”

*Exploration of maladaptive beliefs*—the therapist and client together explore the ways in which unhelpful lifestyle patterns developed. Early childhood experiences are usually the focus.

*Assessing and highlighting strengths* –adaptive lifestyle features are explored; how did the client gain mastery (superiority) in certain areas of life?

*Exploring dreams and other elements of the unconscious* – for the purpose of exploring lifestyle patterns.

*Encouragement/instilling hope*—the therapist may reflect back to the client evidence of efficacy or personal strengths, as a way of encouraging the client to keep working.

*Offering interpretation*—the therapist offers interpretations in a gentle and open way, allowing for the possibility of being wrong (Adlerian therapy is not a deterministic model).

*Might sound like:*

- “I think I hear that….am I’m curious if it might suggest…”
- “I’m wondering if it might be the case that…”

*Reorienting* –in order to establish new lifestyle patterns, clients need to be aware of what is occurring, and then practice something new. The therapist might highlight a gap between a goal and a lifestyle element, to help the client challenge a belief, identify a path toward mastering a new behavior, and trying it on.

*Might sound like:*

- “You say that you want to be more open with your feelings, and yet I think I heard that you stuffed your feelings down yesterday at that meeting at work. Is that right?....What would feel right to you, in moments like that?”

*Images and rehearsals*—clients practice imagining the new behavior or goal. They may also practice what they are going to say or do, in session.

*Spitting in the client’s soup* – the therapist highlights the negative aspect of a behavior (e.g. the loss of connection when one is always serious and “in charge” in groups).

*“Push button” technique*—clients use their imaginations to conjure images that create different feelings. Through this technique, clients learn that feelings can be voluntary invoked.

*Prescribing the symptom/paradoxical intention*—the therapist encourages the client to do more of whatever is problematic (sulking, cleaning, organizing, etc.), with the goal of highlighting the consequences and unpleasant elements of the behavior.

*“The Question”*—this can be worded a number of ways and is sometimes also called “the miracle question.” It is some form of “what would be different if you woke up tomorrow and everything was better?” The goal is to examine the purpose of behaviors.
