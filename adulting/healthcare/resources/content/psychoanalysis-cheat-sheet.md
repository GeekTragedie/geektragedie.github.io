## Key Concepts

All people have the conflicting drives of *libido* (life, pursuit of pleasure, avoidance of pain, sexuality) and the *death instinct* (aggression).

There are three levels of consciousness: the conscious, the preconscious (sometimes now called the subconscious), and the unconscious. That which is immediately accessible to us is conscious; that which is retrieved with slight effort is preconscious; and that which has been pushed away because it is too threatening and is not accessible to our awareness is unconscious. Analysis is needed to access the unconscious.

The personality is organized as the id, ego, and superego. The id seeks pleasure and avoids pain; the id is impulsive, selfish, and short-sighted, but needed for survival. The ego mediates between the realities of the world and the individual’s desires, or id drives. The ego learns, plans, cooperates, coordinates, and develops logical ways to get needs met in the world. The superego represents parental and societal values; the superego contains the perfectionistic, ideal self.

The ego resists being confronted with undesirable truths and protects itself with ego defense mechanisms. The analyst’s job is to break through the defenses and help the client come to terms with that which has been pushed down into the unconscious.

Individuals move through developmental stages (psycho*sexual* in classic Freudian, or psycho*social* in ego psychology). This represents a deterministic viewpoint, or the idea that one’s past determines one’s future.

## Goals of Therapy

- Bring the unconscious into the conscious so that clients may achieve insight
- Move past ego-defenses and resistance
- Use insight to analyze and make sense of past experiences, allowing for restructuring of personality
- Make changes in the present and avoid ego defense mechanisms
- Gain self-knowledge and interact more authentically with the world

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Free association* – allowing the client to speak, write, or remember without any attempt to control the content or create order or structure. The analyst offers interpretation when appropriate.

*Might sound like:*

- “Say more about that...”
- [Repeating the last word that the client said to prompt them to continue]
- [Silence, to keep them talking without direction]
- “You used your brother’s name when you meant to say your husband’s name. You seem to feel anger at them both.”

*Transference* – the analyst acts as a neutral enough screen that the client can project unconscious material onto the analyst and the relationship. This experience is used to provide access to unconscious material and create space for examining it with the client.

*Might sound like:*

- “You sound angry with me right now; let’s explore that.”
- “I have noticed that you appear to wait for me to nod or approve of what you are saying before you say more. When has that shown up in your life before?”
- “Who am I reminding you of right now?”

*Dream analysis* – interpretations made by the analyst of the client’s dreams, usually focusing upon apparent conflicts between conscious and unconscious aspects of the mind, conflicting drives, or conflicting ego states.

*Interpretation* – the analyst shares interpretations by giving voice to unconscious themes or messages and providing meaning. Interpretation must be done carefully, with attention to the relationship, because if it is not delivered at the right time or in the right way, the client will almost certainly reject it and then increase ego defense mechanisms to remain protected from the interpretation.

*Countertransference and Relationship* – the analyst notices their own feelings toward the client or when with the client and examines for possible meaning and use (i.e. “do others also feel pity when they see this? How might that be affecting my client’s relationships?”). The relationship itself can be used to explore patterns and try on new behaviors (i.e. “try telling me how you feel rather than asking me; let’s see how that feels to both of us”).
