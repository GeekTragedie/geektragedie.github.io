## Key Concepts and Terms

Founded by William Glasser, rooted in choice theory, Reality Therapy is all about the choices individuals make and the behaviors which define their lives.

*Quality world*: the mental perceptions (images) clients have made about the world and themselves. Perceptions are subjective, and guide decisions. There is an assumption that our perceptions are our reality, and there is no need for either the counselor or client to find the “real, objective Truth” because it doesn’t really exist.

*Total behavior*: the four areas of behavior—doing, thinking, feeling, and perceiving. Our behaviors are within our control and shape the quality of our lives.

Glasser assumed that most behavior could be accounted for by examining which of the five basic human needs the behavior was serving: survival, belonging, freedom, power, and fun.

*Organized behavior* is that which we do routinely (go to our jobs, talk to our friends, bring in the mail) and is performed in response to our established perceptions. *Creative behavior* occurs when we change our perceptions or acquire a new idea, are faced with a new challenge, or need to find a new way to meet needs.

Treatment generally focuses on changing doing and thinking behavior; these are considered more accessible targets, and emotions and physiology will change as the doing and thinking changes. In other words, clients will feel better when they act and think differently. This means changing unhelpful perceptions and making choices which are more effective for meeting needs.

Problems are assumed to arise when clients are not making choices which meet their needs. Problems can be caused by pitting two needs against each other, behaviors which move the client away from goals, or thoughts which diminish personal agency (“I can’t,” etc.). Even serious psychosis is assumed to be about choices; symptoms like delusions are believed to result from creative attempts to meet needs during difficult circumstances (for example, the client may feel unable to meet the conflicting needs of survival and belonging in an abusive environment, and their mind may invent delusions to try to bridge the gap).

Reality therapists act as supporters, guides, and “mirrors” to their clients, so that clients face the reality of being in charge of their own lives, feel empowered to make changes, and have help with taking the risk of making a new choice. Reality therapists do not accept excuses when clients try to diminish their own responsibility (“I have no control,” etc.), do not punish or criticize, and never give up.

## Goals of Therapy

- Acceptance that behavior (including feeling) is a choice and the client must take responsibility for their own choices.
- Examine choices and perceptions, challenging ineffective thoughts and decisions.
- Make new choices, which serve needs in a healthy, accountable way.

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Questions*—in this model, questions serve the function of encouraging clients to examine their own behaviors and are carefully worded to keep the client accountable. For example, the counselor might ask “what role did your thinking play in that conflict?” in order to help the client think about their behaviors and alternative choices which could be made. A question like “what did you chose to tell yourself that meant?” reminds clients that they are constructing their own perceptions about the world.

*Optimistic statements/encouragement*—if reality is constructed, it can be changed. Counselors show optimism that the client can make new choices. Counselor encourage trying new behavior.

*Metaphors, reflection, and joining*—when the client shares details about their perceptions, counselors try to use these metaphors to gave more understanding of the client’s experiences. Counselors use reflection to join with clients or to help clients examine their perceptions.

Humor—humor serves the function of meeting the “fun” need and also helps clients practice flexibility and challenge unhelpful perceptions.

Confrontation—excuses are not accepted. Confrontation does not need to be aggressive, but it is assertive. Counselors remind clients that they are not helpless, they have choices, and their behavior makes a difference.

“You didn’t go to work yesterday—what led to that decision?”

Paradoxical intervention—clients might be asked to exaggerate a symptom or act opposite to a perception (e.g. relax and have fun one day, in an exaggerated fashion, even though the client’s mental picture of adults is that adults are always serious and stoic). The client learns that they are in control of their own behaviors and their perceptions.
