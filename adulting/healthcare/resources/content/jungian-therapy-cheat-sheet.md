## Key Concepts and Terms

Like Freud, Jung was very interested in the unconscious. To this concept, he added the idea of the “collective unconscious,” based on the philosophical belief that all human beings have the same instinctual tendency to create meaning about certain concepts.

Levels of consciousness: consciousness (that which you know), personal unconsciousness (your own thoughts/feelings/experiences which the ego has blocked from your conscious awareness, or events the ego has disregarded because they do not appear necessary to the self in the present moment), and the collective unconscious, which is shared by all people. The collective unconscious is believed to show up in cultural symbols, images, and rituals.

Archetypes are critical to Jung’s theory. Archetypes are the representations of shared human experiences, such as death, birth, wisdom, motherhood, heroism, etc. Jung believed that all people inherit the tendency to create archetypes, or to form representations of these fundamental experiences.

The Self is considered an important archetype in that by developing the Self, the client is able to integrate the levels of consciousness and move toward wholeness. A well-developed Self can be authentic and aware of personal processes.

Persona: the “mask” that we present to the outside world, including the roles we play (parent, employee, friend, spouse). The persona may demonstrate our unconscious representations of archetypes.

Anima: the feminine elements of the psyche.

Animus: the masculine elements of the psyche.

Complex: a cluster of associated experiences, beliefs, ideas which share an emotional “charge.” Complexes can become rigid and fixed, thus leaving us “stuck” in them.

Analysts are expected to undergo analysis themselves and be as familiar with themselves as possible.

## Goals of Therapy

**Individuation:** knowing oneself as completely as possible, including the contents of the personal and collective unconscious.

**Development of the Self:** making the unconscious conscious.

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Hypnosis* – exploring the unconscious presumably without the filter of the ego getting in the way.

*Projective measures (word association, ink blots, etc)* – the goal is to find unconscious thoughts, feelings, and “stuck points” (complexes) and make them available to the conscious mind.

*Dream analysis* – examination of the content of dreams, looking for signs, symbols, archetypes, and meaning. The personal unconscious may be represented when dreams are deemed to be objective (e.g. people and things represent what they actually are). The collective unconscious is believed to be visible when archetypes show up in the dreams.

*Exploration of active imagination/fantasy* – an advanced technique for clients well familiar with analysis. The client engages with an archetypal image, likely from one of their dreams, in session. The client might focus on the image and free associate, imagine a conversation or interaction, or imagine an interaction.

*Use of transference and countertransference* – the analyst pays attention to the relationship, looking for patterns of projected Shadow emotions, archetypes, or other symbolic content.

*Art* – Jungian work lends itself very well to the creative therapy modalities such as art, dance, music, and drama, in which symbols and archetypes are common and can be explored without language.

*Play therapy* – play can also be used to identify unconscious content and archetypes, which may arise through the toys a child engages with, the patterns they recreate, or the rituals they play out.
