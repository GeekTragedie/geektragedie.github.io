## Key Concepts and Terms

Founded by Aaron Beck. Beck later changed his theory to Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, though other practitioners also use CBT to describe what they do. Beck also developed the commonly used Beck Depression Inventory, which is an evidence-based screening for the diagnosis of depression.

Cognitions are believed to shape individuals’ experiences of themselves and the world. Cognitions become beliefs, which eventually become rigid beliefs, or schema.

Automatic thoughts are the thoughts which pop up without intention. Automatic thoughts often point to deeper beliefs, and may be coming from schema. Counselors in this model pay a lot of attention to automatic thoughts and encourage clients to become highly aware of them.

*Schema* are the core beliefs, standards, or rules individuals define for certain concepts. For example, self-schema could include beliefs about being unlovable, being fundamentally incompetent, or being different from other people. In this example, if the person was treated with kindness, this experience would be interpreted through the schema and might be turned into a thought like “they were nice to me because they pity me.” Without intentional action to change schema, schema tend to resist evidence which contracts them.

Schema can be adaptive or maladaptive. Schema can also be active (always working) or inactive (triggered only by certain events, such as a death).

Cognitive therapy is interested in early life experiences. Early childhood experiences, especially those with strong emotional impact, create belief systems and start the process of schema formation.

Cognitive therapies are often very structured, and many have been manualized. Though good rapport and a collaborative client-therapist relationship are viewed as important, working through automatic thoughts and schema are considered the true element of change. Recent trends have even included self-guided cognitive work in the form of workbooks and internet programs, which do not require a counselor at all.

## Goals of Therapy

- Challenge and eliminate distorted thinking.
- Change cognitive schema. Schema can be challenged at three levels, from restructuring to reinterpreting; reinterpreting is seen as the highest goal.
  - Restructuring: awareness of the maladaptive schema, but with no real change.
  - Modification: some change of the maladaptive schema, perhaps based on context.
  - Reinterpreting: changing or eliminating the maladaptive schema entirely; replacing with a new, adaptive schema.

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Assessment (interviews, journaling, questionnaires, etc.)* – cognitive therapy utilizes a lot of assessment, in the form of interviews, thought-tracking assignments like journaling, questionnaires, and inventories. The purpose is to identify thought patterns and set behaviorally-specific goals.

*Guided discovery and Socratic questioning*—clients are encouraged to question their automatic thoughts, beliefs, and ultimately, their schema. Counselors assist by asking open questions which explore the thoughts. The “three question” technique looks like some version of 1) what is the evidence for that belief? 2) how else could you interpret the situation? 3) if your belief were true, what would the implications be?

*Behavioral homework*—clients are often challenged to go out into the world and try on a new belief system. For example, they might experiment with positive self-talk, telling themselves “I can do this” when they attempt a new project at work. The client might also write down the effect of their new thinking and bring it into session to discuss their work.

*Looking for evidence*—the counselor encourages the client to “unpack” their belief and examine the truth of it (for example, “what does it really mean to be a failure?...is that what you do?”)

*Challenging absolutes*—the counselor calls out black-and-white, absolute thinking (“are you really the *worst* person in the world? What did you do to earn that title?”)

*Reattribution (the “responsibility pie”)*—the counselor helps the client identify how much responsibility they really have for the situation; the client needs to assess how much blame to really attribute to themselves. Sometimes a pie chart is helpful (“how much belongs to you, your boss, the copy machine breaking, the weather…?”)

*Labeling cognitive distortions*—sometimes just labeling a thought as a thought is helpful for taking the power out of it. When clients use a cognitive distortion, they practice labeling it (“that was catastrophizing,” or “I just pretended I could mind read”).

*De-catastrophizing (“what then?”)*—the counselor helps demystify the catastrophic thinking by guiding the client through the scenario. The client examines their fears and may realize that the worst case scenario isn’t actually so horrible that they couldn’t problem-solve it.

*Scaling to challenge black-and-white thinking*—when clients get stuck in a rigid either/or thought pattern, the counselor asks them to consider some in-between, “gray” areas. Scaling may be used by putting one possibility on one end (“let’s say divorce is a one”) and the other on a far end (“let’s say your wife not being mad at you at all is a ten”). The client then considers how likely some other options might be (“so, her getting really angry but not leaving you is a three…what evidence do you have that this could happen?”). The goal is to help the client see more possibilities than the catastrophe.

*Pro/con lists for belief systems (“is it helpful to think this?”)*—clients explore not how true their beliefs are, but how helpful their beliefs are. The counselor helps the client identify benefits to maintaining the beliefs (“you don’t have to change, you feel pretty safe in that belief”) and the difficulties with maintaining the beliefs (“you feel bad, you never let yourself have fun”). The client can then make a decision about whether or not to let the belief go.

*Cognitive rehearsal* – the counselor assists the client, in session, to imagine trying something new. The client can then challenge unhelpful thinking ahead of the actual behavior.
