## How Neo-Psychoanalytic Thinking Changed Psychoanalysis

| It challenges… | It emphasizes… |
|---|---|
| Freud’s personality structure | Biological and cultural evolution |
| Strict determinism | The tribal nature of human beings |
| The clinician as a blank screen | Dynamic change over the lifespan |
| Drive theory | The clinician as mentor and teacher |
| The mind–body split | Relational interconnectedness as a primary need |
| | The workings of the mind within the brain |

## Goals and the Clinician’s Role

| | Psychoanalytic | Neo-Psychoanalytic |
|---|---|---|
| **Goals** | Restructure the personality; make the unconscious conscious; gain self-awareness; challenge ego defense mechanisms | Reconnect with the tribe; gain insight into patterns of behavior; gain skills for living adaptively; challenge unhelpful schemas about oneself and the world |
| **Role of the clinician** | Neutral analyst; a blank screen for transference projections | Guide, supporter, and teacher; a relationship for practicing new skills |

## Common Factors

- Unresolved emotional experiences lead to ongoing psychopathology.
- Unseen processes happen below the level of full consciousness.
- Unhelpful patterns of beliefs and behaviors develop in response to past experiences.
- Early childhood experiences matter.
- Treatment is insight-oriented.
- Transference and countertransference are used as information.

## Attachment Theory

### Secure Attachment

1. The child experiences and expresses a need (cries).
2. The parent responds, works out what’s wrong, and meets the need.
3. The child feels attuned to and has the need met.
4. The child calms.

**Characterized by:** trust of others; trust in cause and effect and in self-efficacy; willingness to explore the world; a well-regulated nervous system.
**Internal motto:** “Everything will be alright.”

### Insecure-Avoidant (Dismissive) Attachment

1. The child experiences and expresses a need (cries).
2. The parent avoids the crying child or retreats quickly without giving enough help.
3. The child feels fear in response to being abandoned.
4. The child adopts a “freeze” response to avoid driving the caregiver away.

**Characterized by:** distrust of others; self-doubt and self-hatred; resistance to change, influence, and risk; a highly activated nervous system, with suppression.
**Internal motto:** “You can’t count on anyone.”

### Insecure-Ambivalent (Preoccupied) Attachment

1. The child experiences and expresses a need (cries).
2. The parent responds sporadically: sometimes helping, sometimes not.
3. The child feels anxious even as help arrives, and expresses the need more.
4. The child stays in a state of nervous system activation.

**Characterized by:** distrust of others; dependence on others; resistance to being comforted, and anger; a highly activated nervous system with outward distress.
**Internal motto:** “Everyone is going to leave me.”

### Disorganized Attachment

1. The child experiences and expresses a need (cries).
2. The parent responds in a way that frightens the child.
3. The child needs protection from the very caregiver they fear, an impossible contradiction.
4. The child tries any strategy available, sometimes paradoxically.

**Characterized by:** distrust of others and self; emotional volatility and low ability to self-regulate; erratic behavior; a highly activated nervous system.
**Internal motto:** “Something is wrong.”

### Common Presenting Concerns

Things clients with preoccupied, dismissive, or disorganized patterns might say:

- “I’m lonely.”
- “I’m different from everyone else.”
- “People seem concerned about me.”
- “My life is in chaos.”
- “People treat me horribly.”
- “I’m too much for people.”
- “I can’t seem to make good relationships last.”
- “I feel bad, but I don’t know why or what to do.”
- “I ruin every relationship that I touch.”

## Assumptions

- The client’s behaviors, thoughts, and emotional reactions are reasonable and adaptive, given what they’ve experienced.
- Attachment patterns tend to perpetuate themselves unless the client engages in change.
- The client’s attachment patterns will show up in the counseling relationship.
- The counselor can help provide a corrective attachment experience.
- The counseling relationship can become a secure base; when it does, exploration and change in the world can happen.
- A new attachment style can be earned (earned secure attachment).
- Other concerns, such as depression or anxiety, are likely tied to the attachment pattern and a loss of meaningful connection.
- Change is action-oriented: to get something you’ve never had, you’ll need to do things you’ve never done.

## Discussion Scenario

For a client you’re working with, ask:

- What might the client be thinking about the counseling process?
- What might the client be feeling right now?
- How is the client likely to react?

## Healing Attachment Injuries

**Phase 1**

- **Awareness:** explore current patterns and their history; explore and make meaning.
- **Grief:** self-validate and receive validation from the counselor; accept and grieve what was lost.
- **Goals and contracting:** identify new ways of being in the world; explore the benefits and risks of change.

**Phase 2**

- **New self-behavior:** experiment with new self-talk, self-treatment, and self-care; explore new ideas of self-concept.
- **New social behavior:** experiment with new relationship behaviors, with support from the counselor.
- **Goals and contracting:** celebrate successes and rewards; identify new goals and ongoing behaviors.

## Role of the Counselor

- Act as a secure base: honest, genuine and authentic, non-blaming and non-shaming, reliable, and acting with integrity.
- Lean into, explore, and repair relationship ruptures.
- Educate, guide, and support.
- Challenge.
- Reflect and celebrate successes.
