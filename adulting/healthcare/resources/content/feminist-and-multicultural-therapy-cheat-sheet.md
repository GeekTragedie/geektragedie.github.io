## Key Concepts and Terms

Feminist and multicultural theories are constructivist. There is a fundamental assumption that realities are constructed based upon experiences. There is no objective truth.

The person and the political are inseparable. Everyone exists within the political context and all aspects of their identities are politicized. The individual cannot be removed from the contexts of social norms, power and privilege, and discrimination.

It is not the individual who is perceived as having the problem; the problem (or pathology) is viewed as belonging to the entire culture which created the circumstances from which the problem arose.

“Normal” is defined by the dominant group. This usually results in the traits of the dominant group being valued and upheld as prototypically human, and the traits of the marginalized groups being seen as pathological and inferior. This dynamic is challenged.

Historical patterns of power and oppression are challenged in individual therapy and more broadly in political activism.

Counselors are very conscious of the power and privilege dynamics within the counseling room itself and seek to equalize and share power with clients.

Fundamental assumptions and deeply held beliefs (or schema) are challenged in order to build client self-efficacy and self-esteem, undo internalized oppression, and create more freedom for the client to make choices.

Feminist and multicultural counseling can be considered philosophical stances more than specific arrangements of techniques and practices, and are built upon various theories (feminist theory, queer theory, racial identity development theory, etc.).

## Goals of Therapy

- Help the client to problem-solve, not simply adapt to the oppressive circumstances.
- Cultivate self-esteem and empowerment.
- Help the client develop quality relationships and connections within their culture.
- Affirm diversity and diverse strengths and abilities.

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Cultural analysis*—the problem is contextualized by examining the norms, beliefs, and agenda of the dominant culture. In this manner, the counselor helps the client to challenge self-blaming narratives by framing the problem as part of a broader societal issue. For example, a client might blame themselves for their own poverty and the counselor might walk the client through a cultural analysis of the ways in which racism, capitalism, and gender oppression *set them up* for poverty.

*Role analysis*—the counselor helps the client to explore their own learning about the roles and identities which they occupy. This might be a discussion of FOO, social learning, or formal education. The client is then able to identify ways in which their role(s) are contributing to their situation.

*Self-disclosure*—the counselor utilizes self-disclosure to demystify their role as counselor and neutralize power. The message to the client is “I’m not above you.”

*Assertiveness training*—the counselor helps the client learn to speak up for themselves and advocate for their needs in clear, unapologetic ways which increase personal power.

*Validating anger*—anger is an emotion which often motivates actions such as self-preservation, group protection, and initiating change. Righteous anger is seen as a strength and the counselor might help the client get in touch with that anger and the goals and values it suggests.

*Change*—ultimately, feminist and multicultural counseling is about change out in the world. Counselors help clients examine their own power and choices and help them implement changes to their circumstances.
