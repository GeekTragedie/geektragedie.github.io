## Key Concepts and Terms

REBT is the work of Albert Ellis. He placed a big emphasis on the idea of *irrational thinking*. Ellis saw irrational thinking as thinking which is rigid, not substantiated by the facts, and sabotages the individual’s attempts to achieve goals.

There is both optimism and pessimism in Ellis’s view of people. Optimistically, he viewed people as basically good and capable of change. Pessimistically, although he believed that all people have an aptitude for both irrational and rational thinking, he saw irrational thinking as a very common way in which people self-sabotage. Further, he identified that many clients cling to these beliefs and felt that people have a tendency toward negativity.

“Musterbating”: this is Ellis’s term for the way in which clients trap themselves with lots of “I should,” “I must,” “I have to” thinking. In REBT, these are flags for rigid, irrational thinking and should be explored.

The emphasis on “rational” does not mean that REBT is anti-emotion. The purpose of REBT is to notice unpleasant emotions (emotional disturbances) and do the work to *feel better*.

In Ellis’s mind, being healthy means having a sustained positive view of oneself (self-esteem not based upon the assessment of others), enough cognitive flexibility to question one’s own beliefs and react appropriately in the moment, and thought patterns which are logical and positive. Ellis believed that if a person lived this way, they could focus on the important things in life such as relationships with others, hobbies and interests, being creative, and exploring ideas and philosophy.

One of the biggest contributions from REBT was probably the ABC model:

A: activating events.

B: beliefs.

C: consequences of the beliefs.

The ABC model is a means of evaluating a client’s irrational beliefs and chronic ways of self-sabotaging.

The treatment in REBT focuses of the ‘D’ and ‘E’ following the ABCs:

D: detect beliefs, discriminate between rational and irrational beliefs, and debate irrational

beliefs.

E: identify a new effective way of thinking, for a better end result.

The focus on the ABCDE model speaks to the behavioral change emphasis in REBT. This puts the counselor in the role of teacher/collaborator/coach. Ellis believed that empathy meant listening to the client’s problem and helping them to solve it. He did not focus on a warm, supportive relationship. Though a strong therapeutic alliance is viewed as helpful, positive regard in REBT is not seen as a necessary or sufficient condition for change. Rather, the assumption is that the change happens when problems are solved—the work is the healing, not the relationship itself.

## Goals of Therapy

- Decrease self-defeating beliefs and behaviors.
- Cope with difficult situations and engage in goal-oriented behavior

## Common Therapeutic Interventions

*Self-talk and affirmations*— the client practices new ways to speaking to themselves, with the goal of eventually changing the underling beliefs. For example, the client might tell themselves “I am a good person” ten times a day.

*Self-dialogue/fact checking*—moving beyond the single statement, the client might analyze their own beliefs, in the moment, using a Socratic dialogue-like model. In this manner, they fact-check their beliefs like a lawyer, looking for proof or inaccuracy.

*Cost-benefit analysis*—the client creates a pros and cons list of their behaviors or beliefs, to evaluate if their current choices are what they really want to be doing.

*Psychoeducation and bibliotherapy*—the client reads books about REBT, uses worksheets, watches lectures, or otherwise gains an education in the model and its use.

*Teaching others*—the client practices negating irrational beliefs in the world around them, in order to better identify their own beliefs and start catching their irrational thinking.

*Problem-solving*—if an activating event is a tangible problem like living with a nasty roommate or having a boring job, the therapist might engage the client in changing the situation. The client identifies solutions, such as moving or looking for a new job. The therapist can then help them challenge any irrational beliefs which are getting in their way of making change.

*Imagery*—the client imagines an upcoming situation which could cause emotional disturbance and pre-emptively challenges any irrational thinking. For the example, the client might imagine asking someone on a date, notice their automatic thought of “I’m going to embarrass myself” and identify a new, effective belief in session, before they go to the real situation.

*Role-playing*—the therapist helps the client enact difficult situations in the session, in order to identify the ABCs and find the problematic beliefs.

*Shame-attacking*—the client exposes themselves to a situation which tends to trigger irrational thinking and emotional consequences (generally something that causes ego anxiety, such as public speaking or asking someone on a date) and repeats the activity out in the world over and over, challenging their irrational thinking each time. This works as an exposure until the irrational thought is gone (this is like proving the irrational thought wrong: “I must not be incapable of doing this, because I just did it” or “it must not make me unlovable to set boundaries because I set a boundary and I’m still okay”).

*Self*-*disclosure:* REBT therapists selectively use self-disclosure to share the ways in which they have challenged and overcome illogical thinking using ABCs. This instills confidence in the model.
