Hypermobility

A plain-language guide to joint hypermobility, hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD), and hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS): what they are, what often comes with them, and what helps. Every point links back to its source below.

This isn’t medical advice. It’s general information to help you have better conversations with your doctor and physical therapist. Everyone’s body is different.

The criteria are being updated. The Ehlers-Danlos Society expects new hEDS and HSD diagnostic criteria in spring 2027. Until then, clinicians still use the 2017 criteria, and people who already have a diagnosis won’t need to be re-assessed [8].

What hypermobility is

  • Joint hypermobility means a joint moves further than most people’s. You might have heard it called being “double-jointed” [1][2].
  • It’s common. Estimates vary, but roughly 1 in 10 people may be hypermobile. It’s more common in women and children, and in dancers, gymnasts, and musicians [1].
  • For many people it causes no problems at all and often lessens with age. Symptoms tend to start when the body shifts from coping to struggling, for example after an injury, an illness, a stressful stretch, pregnancy, or a change in activity [1][2].
  • Clinicians often screen with the Beighton score, a 9-point check of the little fingers, thumbs, elbows, knees, and whether you can put your palms flat on the floor with straight knees. What counts as “generalized” depends on age: about 6 or more points before puberty, 5 or more from puberty to 50, and 4 or more after 50. A high score alone isn’t a diagnosis [3][4].

HSD and hEDS

  • There are 13 types of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. All but one can be confirmed with a genetic test. Hypermobile EDS (hEDS) has no known gene or test, so it’s diagnosed with a clinical checklist [4][5].
  • An hEDS diagnosis under the 2017 criteria needs three things: generalized hypermobility; a set of related features (such as soft or stretchy skin, a family history, or long-lasting joint pain or repeated dislocations); and ruling out other conditions that look similar [4][5].
  • Hypermobility spectrum disorders (HSD) describe symptomatic hypermobility that doesn’t meet the hEDS criteria. HSD can affect the whole body, just the hands and feet, or one area. Hypermobility with no symptoms isn’t HSD [6][7].
  • HSD isn’t automatically milder than hEDS. The Ehlers-Danlos Society notes that no test cleanly separates the two, and the care is the same [7].
  • Any doctor can use the checklist, and primary care doctors can recognize these conditions and coordinate care. Genetics teams help rule out other EDS types. Children are assessed with a separate pediatric framework [4][9][10].

What it can come with

  • Pain, fatigue, and unstable joints. Joint and muscle pain, stiffness, repeated sprains, partial dislocations (subluxations) or full dislocations, poor balance, and tiredness are common [2][11]. Fatigue often has several causes at once, such as poor sleep, pain, and low fitness, so it’s worth checking for other causes like anemia or thyroid problems too [12].
  • Body awareness. Many hypermobile people have a weaker sense of where their joints are, especially in the legs and fingers, which can affect balance [13].
  • POTS and dysautonomia. Dizziness, a racing heart, or nearly fainting when standing up shows up often alongside hEDS and HSD [14][15]. In one U.S. study of people with POTS, about a third met hEDS criteria [14].
  • Gut symptoms such as reflux, IBS-type symptoms, and constipation are often reported. The link looks real, but the research is still limited [16].
  • Anxiety. Most studies find hypermobility and anxiety, especially panic, occur together more often than chance. Why is still debated [17].
  • Neurodivergence. Studies suggest hypermobility is more common in autistic people and in people with ADHD, but the evidence is still emerging, and these are links, not causes [18][19].

Taking care of yourself

  • Pace yourself. Spread activity across the day, rest before you crash, and plan ahead for busy days [2][20].
  • Build strength and balance with the right PT. Physical therapy is the main recommended treatment. Look for a physical therapist who knows hypermobility, start small, and build slowly. Core and joint stability, balance work, Pilates, tai chi, and pool exercise are common choices. Research is promising but still small [21][22][23].
  • Don’t chase the stretch. Pushing past your normal range can make joints less stable. Just because a joint can go there doesn’t mean it should. High-impact and contact sports, sudden twisting, and forceful neck or spine manipulation are best avoided [2][21][22].
  • Supports can help, with care. Supportive shoes, orthotics, ring splints, taping, and compression garments can help some people with stability, body awareness, and pain. Rigid braces worn for long stretches can weaken muscles, and anything too tight can hurt your skin. Have supports fitted by a PT, OT, orthotist, or podiatrist, and keep exercising alongside them [24].
  • Sleep. Keep regular sleep and wake times, and use pillows to support your joints. If poor sleep lasts more than a couple of months, ask a doctor about it [12][25].

Riding, travel, and long sitting

No research we found looks specifically at motorcycling, riding vibration, or riding gear for hypermobile people. These are general points that sources do support for long trips and long stretches of sitting. Bring your plans to a physical therapist who knows hypermobility; they can help with posture, gear fit, and building endurance for the positions you’ll be in.

  • Change position often. Hypermobility groups suggest not staying in one position for more than about 30 minutes when you can avoid it [26][20].
  • Plan breaks and recovery. Budget your energy around a big trip, stop before fatigue builds, and leave time to recover afterward [20][27].
  • Get supportive gear fitted, and keep strengthening. Braces and supports work best when a professional fits them, when you check for pressure on your skin, and when you use them alongside exercise, not instead of it [24].
  • Neck braces are a clinician decision. For people diagnosed with upper-neck instability, clinicians sometimes suggest a fitted collar for short periods, such as during travel, balanced against the risk of weakening muscles. That guidance is for that diagnosis, not general use [28].
  • Train for the positions you’ll hold. Strength, posture, and body-awareness work with a PT builds endurance for long sitting, driving, or riding [21][28].
  • If you have POTS, long periods upright and heat can make symptoms worse. Fluids, compression garments, and tensing your leg and buttock muscles can help. Check with your doctor first, especially if you have heart, kidney, or blood pressure conditions [15][29].

When to get care

  • See a primary care doctor if you think you or your child has symptomatic hypermobility, or if symptoms are getting worse or getting in the way of daily life [2][30].
  • After a dislocation or subluxation, get urgent care if the limb changes color, goes numb, or the joint doesn’t settle [11][20].
  • New or worsening trouble walking or balancing, fainting when you move your neck, or fast-changing nerve symptoms need a prompt medical check, not just a PT visit [28].
  • Fainting, chest pain, or a racing heart when you stand up should be checked by a doctor [15][29].
  • Call 911 for sudden severe pain in the chest, belly, or side, or a red, painful eye with a pulsing sound in your head. These can be signs of vascular EDS, a rare and different type, and are an emergency [31].
